He just asked me where you've been all day.

Elena read it three times in the blue dark of the car, and then she put the phone face-down on the passenger seat and did the only useful thing, which was nothing, which was sitting still with both hands on the wheel above the cannery road while the first real weather in days came in off the strait and ticked against the glass like a finger testing a window. She did not call back. Calling back was a thread the watcher could climb, and a frightened woman answering her desk phone at full dark, after a question like that, was a woman drawing a circle around herself and standing in it. She made herself count the wipers' first slow strokes. Sleet, not rain — the season had turned somewhere in the dry days she'd spent at payphones, autumn finally letting go of the coast, and the road ahead shone black and mean under it.

What the message told her was simple and very bad. Reyes was no longer asking Marguerite the make-a-copy question he had walked from mouth to mouth around her. He had started asking about Elena. Where she had been. The silence of the channels all afternoon had not been a gap in the watching; it had been the watching turning its head. She had spent the day in dead zones, on a payphone, and the price of being a place they could not reach was that the listening, deprived of her, had gone looking for someone who saw her come and go. It had found the woman at the desk. Marguerite had stood up, and the building's first reflex when a thing stood up was to ask the people nearest it where the trouble had wandered off to.

She did not drive to the clinic. The instinct was to put her body between the old woman and the warm room two floors above her — and that was exactly the move the morning had been built to want, the detective arriving after dark, conspicuous, confirming with her own headlights that the desk clerk was worth a visit. She had learned the shape of her own usefulness too well now to indulge it. Instead she drove to the all-night chemist on the dry side of the ferry plaza, with its lit sign and the payphone bolted to the brick by the propane cage, and she called the front desk of the Mercer Institute on the public line, clerk to clerk, the dullest call in the world.

Marguerite answered on the second ring, and her voice was wrong in the specific way Elena had been afraid of — too even, the evenness of a hand held flat to keep it from shaking. "Mercer Institute."

"It's the records audit," Elena said, in the gray administrative voice, the one with no person in it. "I'm sorry to catch you so late. I have one closing question and then your file's done for the quarter." She let the nothing of it sit between them, and she heard Marguerite understand: a phone that wasn't the desk line, Elena had told her, but Elena had called the desk line on purpose this time, because a call to the desk line was a call anyone could overhear and find boring, and boring was the only safe thing left. "Are you still on shift?"

"Until nine. He asked me to stay." A pause she filled with paper sounds, a drawer, the small theater of a woman doing her job. "He's upstairs."

"Did you eat?" Elena asked, which was not in any audit, and was the realest thing she could risk.

"No."

"There's a thing about audits," Elena said. "We close them in person. Signatures. It's tedious. I'll need ten minutes of you off the clock — not at the desk, the desk is county property and I can't have you signing county forms on his clock, it muddies the record." It was nonsense, beautiful nonsense, the kind of procedural fog a place like the Institute swam in all day. "There's a diner by the ferry. The Anchor. When you clock out at nine, come sign and you're done with me. Bring nothing from the desk. Not the book. Your own pen."

The silence on the line had a foghorn in it somewhere, far out, and under the foghorn the small held breath of a woman being handed a way out and being asked to walk through it slowly, calmly, without looking back at the stairs. "I can do that," Marguerite said. "After nine."

"Good. Drive the long way. The plaza's flooding by the slip; take the upper road." There was no flood. The upper road had no cameras, was the thing, and looped behind the headland where no window of the east wing could watch a pair of taillights climb. Marguerite, who had spent nineteen years learning which instruction was the real one wearing the coat of another, said only, "The upper road. Of course," and hung up the way you set down something you are afraid to drop.

Elena stood in the sleet by the propane cage with her hand still on the cold receiver and made herself wait a full minute before she moved, because a person who hangs up and runs is a person being watched run. One minute. The sign buzzed pink over her. Somewhere across the black water the lantern window would be gold by now, lit for its expected guest, and the guest tonight was not going to come; she was going to sit in a diner and keep an old woman breathing instead, and she found she did not care what the window made of that.

The Anchor was nearly empty, the late ferry crowd long gone, two truckers and a waitress with a paperback. Elena took the booth at the back with a sightline to the door and the lot and ordered coffee she would not drink and laid out, on the cracked laminate, a single blank county form she carried for exactly this kind of cover, and a pen, so that anyone glancing in would see precisely what she had said: tedium, a signature, an audit closing. At eight fifty-one a pair of headlights came down off the upper road and swung into the lot, and Marguerite climbed out into the sleet in a good wool coat buttoned to the throat, and she came in and sat down across from Elena and put her hands flat on the table to stop them.

"He didn't ask it the way he asks things," she said, before the coffee came. "He asks things like he's giving you a present. This he asked the way you ask a window if it's locked." She looked at the blank form and almost laughed and did not. "Where has she been all day. Like it was nothing. Like the weather. And then he brought me tea." Her mouth did the thing that was not a smile. "He's never brought me tea twice in one day in nineteen years. Today it was twice."

The cold found the back of Elena's neck the way it had in the windowless room. Twice. She thought of Acheson on the payphone saying every one with that same little word tucked where the family would never see it — and of the kindness, always the kindness, the gentleness that was the program's true instrument, the warm cup set down in front of the person it had finished with. When he's gentlest with you is when you should be most afraid, she had told Marguerite that morning, and the prophecy had come back at her in the form of a second cup of tea, and she hated, suddenly and completely, how right she had been.

"You did everything correctly," Elena said. "You came the long way. You brought nothing." She kept her voice the clerk's, because the clerk's voice was the only blanket she had to put around the woman. "Now I need you not to go back."

Marguerite's hands pressed flatter. "I have a shift Monday."

"You're going to call in sick. You're going to have a niece in Tacoma who needs you, or a hip that's gone, or any true small thing you can stand behind for two weeks, and you're going to be a woman who got tired and took her leave, which is the most ordinary thing a woman your age at a desk has ever done, and nobody writes it down." She slid the blank form an inch toward Marguerite, theater for the room. "Sign this. It means nothing. It means we're two clerks doing tedium, in case anyone asks the waitress."

The old woman signed it. Her signature, Elena saw — and the seeing went through her like the sleet — was nothing like the murdered name in the logbook. It was open and legible and unafraid, a woman who had never once in her life needed her name to disappear, and Elena understood in that moment the precise distance between Marguerite and the hand they were both hunting: one had spent a lifetime making her name plain so that the book would be true, and the other had spent a lifetime drowning his so that the book could never testify. Two hands at the same cabinet for nineteen years. One honest enough to swear. One careful enough to vanish.

"There's one more thing," Elena said, "and I shouldn't ask it, and I'm going to." She had told herself the whole drive she would not. "You read the book back nineteen years. The hand. You said it surfaces and submerges, after hours, always for keys to rooms where records live." Marguerite nodded, slow. "When it surfaced — was it ever the same nights? The same season? Did the hand have a calendar?"

Marguerite was quiet a long moment, looking past Elena at the black window with the sleet on it. "I didn't think of it as a calendar," she said. "But yes. Late autumn, mostly. This time of year. The cold weeks." She frowned, the bookkeeper finding a column she'd never thought to total. "There was one I noticed because it fell on All Souls' two years running. I thought — what a thing, to sign out a key in the dark on the night for the dead." She looked back at Elena. "Why."

Because that was the season Mara died, Elena did not say. Because that was the season Sophie drowned. Because the man with the calendar did his graduating when the light went out of the year, when the strait went the color of a coin and the lantern window had the most dark to burn against. She wrote nothing. She had reached the edge of the thing again, the place where the three corners wanted to fold up into a fourth wall, and she made herself stop short of it, because the proof she needed — the proof that would hold in a room with Sandoval in it — was not a season and not a swearing old woman. It was the words themselves, laid side by side where no defense could call them coincidence. She had the vocabulary from Acheson. She had the hand from the book. She did not yet have the two margins set against a third in a single frame, in a way that made the language undeniable. That was tomorrow's work, in the cold, alone, with the folders she had left in the un-oiled drawer.

She drove Marguerite to her car and watched the good wool coat get in and the taillights climb the upper road and disappear behind the headland, and only then did she let herself feel how thin the wire was that she'd just strung between an old woman and the warm room. Two weeks of a true small lie. A niece in Tacoma. It might hold. It had to hold, because the alternative was a woman found sitting very still, and Elena had run out of room in herself for any more of those.

Her phone woke as she pulled back into coverage on the cannery road, the messages arriving in the order the watcher could not stage. Nothing from the channels. Nothing from the pier number, the fourth, the fifth. And then one, from the clinic exchange that had been silent since how is sophie, elena — three words, timestamped to the minute Marguerite's taillights had cleared the headland, sent not to threaten but, she understood with a cold that the heater could not touch, to report:

She came the long way.
