The clinic did not look like a place where anything went wrong. That was the first thing Elena distrusted about it.

She had driven across the bay in the thin gray middle of the morning, the wipers ticking through a mist too fine to call rain, and the Mercer Clinic had risen out of the bluff the way good architecture is meant to: as though it had grown there, as though the land had decided on its own to produce something pale and calm and certain. White stone, tall windows, a lawn kept green into November by money. A bronze plaque by the door, weathered to the color of old tea, read THE MERCER INSTITUTE FOR APPLIED PSYCHOLOGY, and under it, smaller, in letters she had to lean close to read, *We are not what was done to us.* She stood a moment with the mist beading on her coat and read it twice, the way she had read the screen of the dead woman's phone, and thought that it was a strange motto for a hospital to choose — not *we heal*, not *we hope*, but a flat denial of the very thing that brought people through the door.

Inside, the air was warm and smelled faintly of cedar and coffee, and the silence was the engineered kind, the kind that cost a great deal to install. A young woman at the front desk had been crying and had stopped, the way people do when a stranger appears and grief becomes a thing to be performed correctly or put away. Elena showed her badge and asked for Dr. Reyes, and the young woman said he was expecting her, which Elena had not told anyone.

He came down the wide stair himself rather than sending someone, and that too she filed. Dr. Adam Reyes was forty-seven and wore it lightly — a lean man in a soft charcoal sweater, no tie, the deliberate informality of someone who had earned the right to it. His face was made for reassurance; she could see how it would work on a patient, the open brow, the eyes that found yours and stayed. His were red-rimmed this morning but dry. He had been crying earlier, she judged, and had washed his face and chosen to be useful instead, and she could not decide whether that was the most admirable thing she had seen all morning or the most rehearsed.

"Detective Vega." He took her hand in both of his, briefly, the way one holds something that might be cold. "Thank you for coming to us. I didn't want to make you ask. I've had Marguerite pull Vivian's schedule, her appointment book, the keycard logs for the building — whatever you need, it's already on the table in my office. I thought it might save you a day of letters and warrants." A small, sad smile. "Vivian hated paperwork. I told myself the least I could do was spare her file some of it."

"That's unusually cooperative," Elena said.

"It's unusually awful," he said simply, "and I'd rather be useful than sit with it." He turned to lead her up, and over his shoulder, lightly: "I assume you're treating it as a suicide."

"I'm treating it as a death," she said, "until it tells me what it is."

She watched the back of his neck for the half-second it took him to absorb that, and there was nothing — no stiffening, no pause in his stride — which proved exactly nothing, because the people who gave you nothing were either innocent or very good, and she had stopped, years ago, believing those were different enough to relax about.

His office was on the second floor, broad and bright, the desk angled so that whoever sat there looked not at the door but at the window, and through the window at the water. He had told the truth about the table: Vivian's leather appointment book, a tidy stack of printouts, a coil-bound binder of what looked like building access records, all squared at the edges and waiting. Elena did not sit. She walked the room first, as she always did, reading it — the framed diplomas, the photographs. There was one of Reyes and Vivian, younger, on the clinic steps the day the plaque went up, both of them laughing at something outside the frame. There was one of Vivian alone, formal, the official portrait. And on the credenza, a little apart from the others, a photograph of a teenage girl in a swim team jacket, sun-faded, the colors gone toward orange.

"Who's this?" Elena asked.

Something crossed his face, quick and genuine, the first thing she'd seen all morning that hadn't been chosen. "Sophie," he said. "A patient. A long time ago — before your time, before mine really, I was practically a student. She drowned. Fifteen years ago this winter." He came and stood beside the credenza and looked at the girl with an expression Elena could not name, something between grief and apology. "Vivian kept her there. She said it kept her honest." He seemed to hear himself and added, "About the limits of what we can do. The ones we don't save."

Elena wrote the name down. *Sophie. Drowned, ~15 yrs.* She did not yet know why; only that a thing tucked a little apart from the others, kept to keep someone honest, was the kind of thing that earned a line in her notebook. She turned the page and let the silence sit, the way she had let it sit with Gerald, and Reyes — unlike Gerald — did not rush to fill it. That too she filed. He was comfortable being watched.

"Dr. Reyes," she said, "I need to ask you about keys."

"Of course."

"Dr. Mercer's home office was locked from the inside when her husband broke in. Deadbolt. Thumb-turn. The trouble is that none of the keys on her ring fit that lock, and one of them is a deadbolt key of the same make — but for a different door." She watched him. "Do you know that office? Had you been to the house?"

"Many times. Dinners, in the good years." He frowned, thinking, and it looked like real thinking, not performance. "That door — yes, I think I know the one you mean, off the upstairs landing. She had it changed. Two years ago, maybe three." He looked up. "There was a — we had a difficult discharge. A patient who became fixated. Made threats. Vivian got nervous about the house and had the locks done, the office especially, because that's where she kept her work. Files. The clinic doesn't keep everything here; some of the sensitive material lived at the house." He spread his hands. "She was careful about that. Almost too careful. She used to say the most dangerous thing in any building was a record."

Elena let that land in the cold drawer with the rest. *The most dangerous thing in any building is a record.* "This fixated patient. Does he have a name?"

"Voss," Reyes said, and then, carefully, as a clinician guarding a confidence even now: "Daniel Voss. I'll have to check what I can give you without a court asking. But Detective — if you're looking for someone who frightened her, who'd been near that house, who the city would be glad to —" He stopped himself. "I'm doing your job for you. Forgive me. I read too many of the wrong books."

It was deftly done. He had handed her a suspect and then apologized for handing her a suspect, which left the suspect in her hands and his own fingerprints wiped off the gesture. She had met a great many helpful people in twenty years of this work and had learned that helpfulness, like grief, came in sizes, and that the ones who measured theirs so exactly were worth measuring back. She wrote *Voss* and beneath it, small, in the private shorthand she used for things she would not say aloud, a single underlined word: *given.*

"The keys," she said, returning him to it. "If the office lock was changed three years ago, there'd be a record of who holds copies. A locksmith. An invoice. The clinic uses a vendor?"

"We do. Hartwell Lock & Safe, on the cannery road — we've used them forever, they did the whole building." He pulled the coil-bound binder toward her, opened it to a tabbed page with the ease of a man who had already found what she would want. "And we keep our own keys here, all of them, in a cabinet in the records office downstairs. Master copies, spares, the lot. Every key in this clinic and a few of Vivian's personal ones, because she trusted the cabinet more than her own pocket. It's signed in and out. Old-fashioned — a logbook, a pen on a string. Vivian wouldn't let us put it on a computer. She said a computer remembers forever and a logbook can be read by anyone with eyes, and she preferred the kind of secret you could hold in your hands."

Elena looked at the binder, then at him. "Show me the cabinet."

The records office was in the building's quiet spine, windowless, lit cool and even. The key cabinet was exactly what he'd described: a tall steel case, grey, the kind found in any facilities manager's closet, its door standing very slightly ajar. A spiral logbook hung from a chain beside it, a chewed pen on a string, the whole apparatus so determinedly humble that Elena understood at once why Vivian had loved it. You could not subpoena a feeling about a logbook. You could not get a warrant for a pen on a string.

"It shouldn't be open," Reyes said, and his voice changed — the first time all morning it had moved without his choosing it. He stepped toward the cabinet and Elena said *don't touch it* and he stopped, hand suspended, and she came around him and looked.

Inside, rows of small brass hooks, each numbered, each labeled in a neat hand on a strip of tape. Most held keys. Some did not. She did not need a glove to read; she read with her eyes, the way the cabinet was built to be read. Her gaze went down the column of labels — FRONT, EAST WING, ARCHIVE, PHARM 1, PHARM 2 — and stopped at the bottom of the second row, at a hook whose label had been written more recently than the others, the tape whiter, the ink darker.

V. MERCER — HOME OFC.

The hook was empty.

She took the logbook by its edge, by the chain, and turned it toward the cool overhead light, and ran her eye up the last page of sign-outs — dates, scrawls, the little human shorthand of a building's daily life. Most lines paired a key with a name and a date in the same easy hand, the records clerk's hand, signing things out and back the way a building breathes. But three lines from the bottom there was an entry that did not breathe like the others. The home office key — the column noted it by its hook number, the one whose tape was new and white above — had been signed out on October the twenty-ninth. Six days before Vivian Mercer died with that same lock turned against her by a hand that left no print. And in the borrower's column, where every other line carried a name, there was only a name she could not read: not a scrawl exactly, but cramped, pressed hard enough to dent the page, the letters built one on top of another as though the writer had tried to be both legible and gone at once. She could make out a capital at the front and the long descending tail of something at the end, and nothing in between that would hold still.

"That one's not signed back in," she said, keeping her voice level. "October twenty-ninth. Who reads this log? Who'd know whose writing that is?"

Reyes leaned in beside her — careful not to touch, she noted, careful the way she'd told him to be and a half-second faster than instruction — and looked at the line for a long moment. Something moved behind his eyes and was put away. "I don't know that hand," he said. "And I should. I sign that book myself." He straightened. "Marguerite keeps the cabinet. She'd know. Detective, I want to be — if a key to Vivian's office walked out of this building a week before she died, then I have been telling you the wrong thing all morning. I've been telling you who frightened her." He looked at the empty hook, and for the first time the famous calm of his face cracked, just at the corner, just enough. "I should have been asking who she trusted."

It was, Elena thought, the single most useful sentence anyone had said to her in two days. And it had come, as everything in this building came, exactly when she needed it — handed to her clean, by a man who measured his help in such perfect doses that she had begun to keep a private tally of the doses themselves. She wrote down the date. She photographed the page, the hook, the white new tape. She did not write down the name, because there was no name to write.

But she stood looking at the dented line longer than she let him see, because the cramped capital at its head and the long tail at its end had snagged on something in her, some shape she half-knew the way you half-know a face in a crowd before the crowd closes over it. She would carry it home. She would lay it beside the photocopy she was not supposed to have, the one soft at the folds in the drawer by her bed, and she would find out whether the thing it reminded her of was a coincidence or the bottom of the well.

A dead woman's office key, signed out by a hand no one could name, six days before the lock turned on her. The clinic burned its one gold window into the dusk behind her as she drove back across the bay, steady and certain and unbothered, the way it burned every night of the year — and Elena understood that she had not come to the lighthouse to read a logbook. She had come so that the lighthouse could get a long, warm, unhurried look at her.
