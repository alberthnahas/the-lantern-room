The cannery road ended where the land gave up. Elena had parked the unmarked Crown Victoria at four in the afternoon in the lee of a gutted ice house, nose pointed at the water so that the windshield framed the dead end of Harbor Street like a stage she had bought a ticket to and would not be allowed to leave. By half past four the light had gone the color of a bruise healing. The cold had stopped pretending to be autumn days ago, and now it had stopped pretending to be anything but what it was: a hand laid flat against the glass, patient, waiting for her to give up the small warmth of the engine she kept switching off so the exhaust would not give her away.

She had chosen the fifteenth the way a person chooses to look at an old wound — knowing what she would find and going in anyway. Dolores Pham had given her the rhythm without meaning to give her a calendar: near the first, near the fifteenth, twice a month for twenty-two months, a woman in a good coat carrying a plain envelope on foot into the worst of the waterfront and coming back twenty minutes later with empty hands and a churchgoer's face. Vivian Mercer had been dead twelve days. But the resumed tape order had not noticed. The relit lantern had not noticed. And Elena had bet the afternoon on the chance that the debt — the one that wasn't on any books — had not noticed either.

A thermos of coffee she had let go cold on purpose, because warm coffee made a person sleepy and cold coffee made a person angry, and angry was closer to awake. The Mara file in her head, not her hands; she had left the photocopy at home, in the un-oiled drawer, because she had learned not to bring her sister to places where her sister might be used against her. The phone face-down on the passenger seat. It had not made a sound since the three words from the recycled number — *it isn't paid* — and the silence of it was its own kind of company, the way a dog that has bitten you once changes how you sit in a room.

At 4:51 the foghorn out past the breakwater spoke once, low, and she felt it in the floor of the car before she heard it. The same horn that had been behind the open line on the second night, when she'd called the clinic number back and a breather had answered her with nothing and the horn had told her where they stood. She had stood, since, in a dozen places that horn could reach. She wondered, not for the first time, whether the watcher was a person who went to the water to listen or a person for whom the water had simply always been there, the way it was here, an address you were born into and never left.

People came and went on the dead end of Harbor Street the way people do on the edge of any city's giving-up: a man with a shopping cart whose wheel screamed against the asphalt; two cannery hands in reflective vests sharing a cigarette under the corrugated overhang of a building that processed nothing anymore; a woman walking a dog that did not want to be walked. None of them carried envelopes. None of them was waiting. Elena had learned the difference between people who were passing and people who were appointed, and she catalogued each one and let them go.

She thought about Vivian walking this road. Pham had said she came on foot, that she did not bring the car down to the doctors' lot but walked it, the full length of Harbor Street, in a coat that cost more than the buildings she was passing, an envelope of counted cash in her gloved hand. Elena had hated Vivian Mercer for six years with a hatred so clean it had organized her life, and the hardest thing about the last two weeks was the way the woman kept slipping the shape of the monster. A monster did not walk her own penance down a cold street twice a month and come back at peace. A monster did not record her decision to put out the lantern herself, *while the decision is still mine*, and get interrupted by someone who never had to knock. Elena did not want Vivian to be complicated. Complicated was the most dangerous thing a case could hand you, because complicated was where the real architect hid.

At 5:20 the streetlights came on, the two that still worked, throwing cones of sodium orange onto wet pavement. And at 5:24 a figure detached itself from the long shadow of the old net loft at the very end of the road and began to walk.

Elena went still in the particular way she had, the stillness that had let her sit a foot from a confession and not breathe wrong. The figure was small, hunched into a coat too big for it, hood up against the cold. It did not walk like the passing people. It walked like the appointed — straight to a thing it had done before, a gravity in its steps. It went to the third piling on the disused finger pier, the one with the rusted cleat, and it crouched, and it put its hand into the gap where the piling met the decking, the way you reach into a mailbox you have reached into a hundred times.

And it came up with nothing.

Elena watched the hand go in and come out empty, go in again, pat the dark seam, withdraw. The figure straightened. It stood very still, facing the water, the hood a black oval against the bruised sky, and even at sixty yards Elena could read the language of a body that had expected something and found absence. She knew that body. She had worn it for six years, every time she dialed a number that no longer reached anyone.

The envelope had not come. Of course it had not come. The hand that carried it down Harbor Street was in a drawer at Hartwell Bay General with a case number on its toe. But the one who came for it had not been told.

Elena's own hand was on the door handle before she had decided anything, and she made herself take it back. *A door someone else lit for me.* If she got out now, badge up, the figure would scatter into the net of alleys behind the cannery and she would have a frightened person and no thread. If she sat, she would have a face. She reached for the phone, turned it over, opened the camera, and through the dirty windshield and the failing light she got three frames of a small hooded shape on a pier before the figure turned.

It did not run. That was the thing that stayed with her afterward, in the cold drawer of her memory where she kept the details that did not fit. It turned, and it looked — not around, not the scan of someone who fears being seen, but *at the car*. Directly at the car, across sixty yards of dark, at the one vehicle on the dead end with its lights off and its engine cold, as if it had known the whole time exactly where Elena was sitting. As if the empty seam in the piling and the long wait in the shadow of the net loft had been not a payment missed but a stage dressed, and the missing of it the point.

The hood tilted. And then the figure lifted one hand — not a wave, nothing so warm; a small, deliberate gesture, palm out, the way you show someone your empty hand to prove you are holding nothing — and Elena's phone, face-up now on her thigh, lit and buzzed against her leg.

She did not look down at first. She held the figure in her eyes the way you hold your place in a book with your thumb. But the figure was already moving, already folding back into the dark of the net loft, unhurried, and by the time she had the door open and her feet on the broken asphalt and the cold taking her breath, there was only the pier, the rusted cleat, the foghorn answering itself, and the orange cones of two streetlights with nothing in them.

She crossed to the third piling at a run that the ice underfoot made foolish. She crouched where the figure had crouched and put her own hand into the seam between piling and decking. Cold, damp, the grit of marine rot. Empty. She went in to the wrist, fingers spread, and touched the far edge of the gap, and there — taped to the underside of the decking where no envelope-collector reaching for an envelope would ever find it, but where a detective who got down on her knees might — was a single object, flat and hard and small.

She worked it loose and brought it up into the sodium light.

A microcassette. Plain shell, the kind Oskar Lind had sold by the case for thirty years. No date in Vivian's bold hand. No date at all. Only a label strip, the adhesive kind Noah Okafor had typed by the hundred, and on it, in the small clean type of a label machine, two characters and nothing else.

*L-9.*

The code that had stopped appearing two years ago. The one Noah had asked about. *What happened to L-9?* And Vivian, not looking up: *L-9 graduated.*

Elena knelt on the freezing pier with the tape in her glove and felt the case do the thing it had been doing to her since the first morning — reach past the evidence and into the part of her that was not a detective, the part that was only a sister with an un-oiled drawer. Because the figure had left this for her. Had stood in the cold pretending to collect a payment it knew would not come, so that she would see it, so that she would get out of the car too late, so that she would come to the piling on her knees and find a graduated woman's voice waiting in the dark.

She finally looked at the phone. The text was not from the clinic exchange. It was not from Mara's recycled number. It came from a third number, a local prefix she did not know, and it said only:

*Now you have something of hers. Don't play it where they can hear.*

The foghorn spoke again, longer this time, and behind it — or under it, she could never afterward be sure — she thought she heard, far off across the black water, a window of gold come on in the east wing of a building people called a lighthouse, and a small hooded figure walking toward it, unhurried, the way you walk toward a lamp that has been lit for you.

Elena closed her hand around the tape until the plastic edges bit through the glove, and did not move for a long time, kneeling on the pier at the end of the road where the land gave up, holding ten thousand hours of a dead woman's voice reduced to a single hour she had not yet heard, and understanding — the way you understand a thing in the body before the mind consents — that she had just been handed not evidence but an invitation, and that the one who came for the payment had never come for the payment at all.

She had come for Elena.
