The cold rain that had failed to be snow all weekend finally committed to itself on Monday morning, and Adam Reyes stood at the leaded window of his second-floor office and watched it lean in off the strait in long gray sheets, and thought, as he often did at this hour, that there were two kinds of people in a city like this. There were the people who hated the rain because it took something from them — the view, the warmth, the hour they'd meant to spend outdoors — and there were the people who had learned to love it because it gave something back. It closed the world down to the size of a room. It made everyone glad of a lamp and a kettle and a person who already knew their name. Vivian had loved the rain. She had said once that you could not do this work in a dry climate, that healing required a certain amount of being trapped indoors with yourself, and that the strait did half her job for her by simply refusing, eight months of the year, to let anyone leave.

He missed her in the way you miss a hard chair you have sat in for twenty years. Not comfort. Correction. There had been no one else alive who could look at him across a case file and say the single thing he had been working around for an hour, and he had not yet found the bottom of how much smaller the world felt without that.

The kettle began its low climb toward noise. He took it off before it could whistle, because the whistle was a vulgar sound and Vivian had hated it, and pouring the water over the leaves he allowed himself the thought he allowed himself most mornings now: that the detective would come today. She had not come yesterday, or the day before. He had felt the absence the way you feel a tooth your tongue keeps returning to. Elena Vega had spent the cold dry week somewhere he could not picture her, and a person he could not picture was a person doing something, and the something was almost certainly correct, because she was the most correct person he had met in years. It was the thing he admired in her and the thing he would have to be most careful of. Correct people did not stop. You could not reason them off a scent. You could only make sure that the scent led, by the gentlest possible grade, somewhere you wanted them to arrive.

His phone lit on the credenza. Not her. The morning's first complication, which was the same as the morning's first gift, because they were so often the same object turned a quarter turn: the board.

He read it standing, the cup warming both hands. The hospital board — which had, in its grief and its appetite, decided that the Mercer Institute's reputation was now a thing that belonged to the city and not to a dead woman — wanted a statement before the week was out. They wanted, more precisely, a name. The papers had been circling for a month, and circling things eventually land, and the board had decided it would rather choose the place they landed than wait to be surprised. There was a patient, discharged years ago, who had made threats against Vivian, who had been seen near the building, who had a face the kind of face the public liked to be afraid of. Daniel Voss. The board did not say *arrest him*. The board was far too careful to say a thing like that. The board said it would be a comfort to the community to know the police were giving *appropriate weight* to all credible concerns, and that the Institute stood ready to assist, and could Dr. Reyes perhaps convey, through his good relationship with the detective, that the Institute's cooperation was *complete*.

He set the cup down and stood very still for a moment, the way he had taught himself to stand still when something arrived that he had been expecting for so long that its arrival felt almost like fatigue.

He had not given them Voss. He wanted that to be clearly true to himself, and it was: he had said the name to the detective once, weeks ago, lightly, the way you mention a stone in a field so that no one trips on it later, and he had said it because it was a true name attached to true threats and to have withheld it would have been the suspicious thing. He had given the police a fact. The city had built the monster out of it on its own. That was the elegant part, the part Vivian would have appreciated if she had lived to see it — you did not have to construct the wrong answer for people. You had only to leave the materials lying about and trust their fear to assemble it. A frightened town wanted a face more than it wanted the truth, because a face could be locked up and the truth, very often, could not.

He typed a reply to the board that promised nothing and reassured everyone. He was good at these. *The Institute's cooperation is and will remain total. I will speak with Detective Vega.* Both sentences were true. He had built a life out of true sentences arranged so that the space between them did the lying.

By ten the rain had not let up and Marguerite's desk in the lobby was still empty.

That was the second complication, and it did not turn into a gift, and he stood at the top of the lobby stairs for longer than he meant to, looking down at the dark wood of the unmanned desk, the logbook on its chain, the little brass lamp she always left burning. Family matter, the temp had said. A niece in Tacoma. Two weeks. He had brought Marguerite tea on Friday — twice, because she had looked grayer than the weather — and she had thanked him with her eyes somewhere over his shoulder, and on Saturday she had been gone. He told himself there was nothing in it. People had nieces. People got tired. The woman had kept that logbook for nineteen years and was owed a fortnight by anyone's arithmetic.

But he had learned, a long time ago, to notice the precise moment a person stopped meeting his eyes, because that moment was data, and the only people who had ever frightened him were the ones who managed to make their fear quiet. Marguerite had gone quiet. And the detective had gone where he could not picture her. And these two facts, which a careless man would have filed in two different drawers, he held side by side in the cold lobby light and did not like the shape they made.

He went back up and made a second call he had been postponing, and this one he made gladly, because it was to Noah.

"You sound like you haven't slept," he said, when the boy picked up, and meant it kindly, and then heard, a half-beat late, that it was the same thing he said to everyone, and that he had begun to say it to Elena, and that Elena had a sister who had once been told things in this building in a voice very like the one he was using now. The thought passed through him and left no mark, the way most thoughts did. He had stopped, years ago, examining the things he said for where they had come from. A man who checked the provenance of every kind sentence would never get a kind sentence out of his mouth.

He told Noah only that the board was making noise, that the boy should route any press calls upstairs, that he was not to carry the building's weight alone — and he heard, in the careful flatness of Noah's *of course, Dr. Reyes*, a thing he had heard in Marguerite's averted eyes and could not yet name. The boy was being careful with him. They were all, lately, being careful with him. Three weeks ago this building had leaned on Adam Reyes like a house leans on a beam, and now the house had begun, very slightly, to hold its breath when he walked through it.

He understood. He even forgave it. Grief made everyone suspicious of the person still standing; it was the cruelest arithmetic of a death, that the survivor inherited not only the absence but the doubt. He would simply have to be, as he had always been, more patient than their fear. Patience was the only thing he had never run short of. You could not rush glass and you could not rush people; both of them, hurried, only cracked.

The detective came at four, when the light was already failing.

He saw her cross the lot from the window — no umbrella, collar up, the unhurried walk of someone who had decided something on the drive over — and he had the cups out before she reached the stairs, and he turned to the door to say her name, and found that he was, beneath the warmth he genuinely felt, listening. Listening the way he had learned to listen: not to the words a person would say but to the room they brought in with them. Some people brought in fear. Some brought in need, which was fear wearing better clothes. Elena Vega, every time, brought in a kind of held weight, a person carrying something heavy and refusing to set it down even to shake your hand, and tonight the weight was heavier than he had felt it, and her hands, when she took the cup, did not quite settle around its heat.

"You found something," he said. He did not phrase it as a question. With her, questions were an insult; she heard them as a smaller person fishing.

"I found a lot of nothing," she said. "The way you do." She drank, watching him over the rim, and he understood that she had come not to tell him a thing but to watch him not be told it, and he felt the old pleasure rise in him, the pleasure of a difficult instrument, and was ashamed of it, and let himself be ashamed because the shame too was useful — it kept his face exactly right.

"The board wants Voss," he said. He gave it to her plainly, because plainness was the most disarming thing he owned, and because if he did not say it she would learn it within a day and wonder why he hadn't. "They won't say arrest. They'll say *comfort the community*. I told them the Institute's cooperation is total." He turned his cup the quarter turn his hands did without him. "I'm telling you the same thing, with the part the board doesn't get to hear: I don't think it's Voss. I think the city wants it to be Voss because Voss has the right face, and I think someone is going to be very glad when a frightened department gives a frightened city the face it's asking for." He let that sit. "I'd rather you heard the trap from me than walked into it."

It was, he thought, perhaps the truest thing he had said all day. It was certainly the most dangerous, and he had said it on purpose, because the surest way to keep a correct person walking toward the right wrong answer was to be the one who warned her against it. She would trust the man who'd told her not to trust the easy story. That was not cruelty. He had decided, long ago and finally, that it was not cruelty — it was stewardship, the careful tending of a thing too valuable and too breakable to be left to its own momentum, and if a part of him flinched at the word *tending* when the thing was a person, he had a room for that part, and the room was very far down, and its door had been shut so long he had forgotten it could open.

"Why are you telling me this," she said.

He almost smiled. It was Vivian's question. It was the question Vivian had taught him to fear, the one that went under the kindness and tapped the foundation to hear whether it was poured solid or hollow, and he had answered it from Vivian a hundred times and never once been sure she believed him.

"Because," he said, "I think you're the only one in this who isn't being steered, and I'd like to keep it that way."

And he watched her face do the thing he could not have engineered if he'd tried, the small involuntary stillness of a person hearing, from a trusted mouth, the exact word for the exact fear she had not told anyone she carried — *steered* — and he saw her decide, in the half-second before she hid it, that he could not possibly have known to use that word, and was therefore safe; and he saw, in the half-second after that, a second thought arrive behind the first and stop her cup halfway to the table.

He did not know what the second thought was. For the first time in a long time, looking at a face he had been reading for weeks, Adam Reyes did not know what the person across from him had just understood about him — only that she had understood something, and that she was now, very carefully, deciding not to drink her tea.
