The Internal Affairs office on the fourth floor had the only window in the building that did not look at the water, and Elena understood, the moment she sat down across from Lieutenant Priya Sandoval, that this was on purpose. From here you could see the parking structure and a wedge of gray sky and nothing that might soften you. A room designed so that the person in the chair had only the person at the desk to look at. She knew the principle. She had built rooms like it herself, in the cheaper version, the interview cubes downstairs with the bolted table and the mirror that fooled no one. She just had not expected to recognize the technique from the wrong side of it.

"Coffee," Sandoval said. It was not a question and it was not an offer; it was a thing she had already done, sliding a paper cup across the desk, the steam coming off it in a thin curl. "It's bad. I'm not pretending it isn't. I just don't like talking to people across an empty table."

"You'd rather they hold something."

"I'd rather they have something to do with their hands besides decide whether to lie to me." Sandoval smiled, and the smile was real, which was the dangerous part. She was perhaps forty-four, neat in the way of people who had stopped trying to be liked and discovered they were liked more for it, with a folder closed on the desk in front of her and her own coffee untouched. "You know how this works, Detective. I'm not the enemy. I'm the part of the machine that exists so that the rest of the machine can say it has a part like me. Most of what I do is paperwork that proves nothing happened. I'd genuinely prefer that outcome here."

Elena wrapped both hands around the cup. The warmth went into her palms and she let it, because the woman was right; it gave her hands a job. Outside, the first cold of the season had put a brittleness into the light. Three days ago it had only been rain. Now the rain had teeth in it, and the gulls were sitting lower on the railings, hunched, as though the air itself had gotten heavier.

"Then ask," Elena said.

"How did you catch the Mercer case?"

There it was, laid on the table flat and clean, the same flatness Osei used when he wanted a fact to carry no fingerprints. Elena had spent the drive over rehearsing the honest-sounding answers, the ones that were true at the surface and hollow underneath, and now that the question was in front of her she found she did not want to use any of them. Not because she had decided to be brave. Because Sandoval would know. The woman had asked Adeyemi first, and that meant she already had his version, and the only thing more dangerous than a lie was a lie that disagreed with one already on file.

"Ruben assigned it," she said. "Sergeant Adeyemi. You've talked to him."

"I have." Sandoval did not open the folder. "He says you didn't ask for it. He says it came down through the duty rotation and landed on the two of you the way these things do, and that you took it because it was your turn to take something." She tilted her head a few degrees. "Is that true?"

And this was the hinge. Because it was true, on the night of, in the literal mechanics of the rotation — and it was also the precise lie Elena had been telling herself for three days, the one she had begun, in the small hours, to doubt. She had requested proximity to anything Mercer-related, years ago, quietly, through channels that did not generate paper. She had told herself the rotation was coincidence. Since the night the photograph of her own lit window had come through from the clinic's number, she was no longer sure that anything that had happened to her in the last week was coincidence, including the rotation, including this chair.

"It's true as far as the rotation goes," Elena said. "Whether the rotation is the whole story is a question I've started asking myself this week. I don't have an answer I'd put my name to."

Sandoval went still. It was a small stillness, the kind a good interviewer learns to perform and a better one learns to mean, and Elena could not tell which this was. "That's a strange thing to volunteer."

"You'd have found it anyway. I'd rather you found it from me."

"Found what, exactly."

Elena set the cup down. She had walked herself to the edge of a thing she had not planned to say, and she made herself look at the parking structure and the wedge of sky and decide, in the space of one breath, how much of the truth she could afford to spend. Not the photocopy in the cold drawer. Not the texts from the clinic exchange. Not the handwriting, not the morgue, not the puncture, not the initials she had underlined once and not let herself underline twice. Those were not hers to spend; they belonged to Osei, to Marguerite, to a case that was not yet a case. But there was one thing that was only hers, that cost only her, and she had carried it alone for six years and it had not made her cleaner. It had made her blind.

"I have a personal connection to Dr. Mercer," she said. "Not to her. To her work. Six years ago my sister died. The death was ruled a suicide. The assessment that ruled it was signed by Vivian Mercer."

The room did the thing rooms do when a true thing is finally said in them, which is nothing at all; the radiator ticked, the gulls shifted, the coffee steamed. Sandoval did not write anything down. She had the kind of attention that made writing seem like a way of not listening.

"You've known this since the night you caught the case," Sandoval said.

"I've known it for six years. I've known it was a conflict since the night I caught the case." Elena heard her own voice come out level and was distantly grateful for it. "I should have recused. I didn't. I told myself I was the most qualified person in the building to read that scene, and that part is true, and it's also exactly what a person tells themselves when they want the thing they shouldn't have."

"Why are you telling me now."

"Because I think someone wanted me on this case for that reason." She had not meant to say it so plainly. It came out of her like the breath after holding one too long. "Because the deeper I go, the more it looks designed for the person I am — for the grudge I carry. And if that's true, then the conflict isn't my weakness anymore. It's the lever. And I can't see the lever clearly while I'm hiding it from the one person whose whole job is to find it."

Sandoval was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke again her voice had changed register, lower, the official edge filed off it, and Elena understood that she had done something she had not intended: she had made the interview into a conversation, and Sandoval had let her, which meant Sandoval had wanted to.

"Do you understand what you've just handed me," Sandoval said. "I could pull you off this today. I could have the case reassigned and your conduct reviewed by end of week. The connection alone is enough. You volunteering it doesn't make it not enough; it just makes it tidy."

"I understand that."

"Then I'll ask the question you came in here braced for, since you've decided to make my afternoon honest." Sandoval finally laid one hand flat on the closed folder. "Did your sister's death have anything to do with how Dr. Mercer died? Are you working a six-year-old grievance, or are you working a homicide?"

"I'm working a homicide," Elena said. "I think it's the same homicide. I think it has been for six years and nobody called it that, including me."

For the first time Sandoval opened the folder. She did it slowly, the way you turn over a card you already know, and Elena's stomach tightened despite everything, because there is no good moment to learn what a person has been keeping in a closed folder with your name on it. But Sandoval did not slide a photograph across the desk, or a printout, or a copy of anything from the cold drawer that Elena had spent the week dreading. She simply read from a single page, her finger under the line, the way a person reads something they want to get exactly right.

"I want to ask you about a name," Sandoval said. "When I started looking at how you caught this — just the paperwork, just the routing — I pulled the case intake to see whose desk it crossed before yours. Standard. And there's a referral notation on the intake. Not for you. For the case. A note that someone outside the department flagged the death as one your unit should take seriously, the morning it came in." She looked up. "It's signed. The signature's a mess — half of it's printed and half of it's run together, like the person started writing their name and decided partway through they'd rather not. I almost skipped it. But there's a line under it, a stamp the front desk uses for outside referrers, with a name typed clean."

Elena did not breathe. The cold drawer, the logbook, the intake sheet in Mara's file, the Oct 29 sign-out, the hand that started a name and learned to make it disappear — all of it stood up at once in her chest like a room full of people getting to their feet.

"What name," she said.

Sandoval turned the folder around so the page faced Elena, her finger still resting beside the typed line, and Elena leaned in over the bad coffee and the brittle light and read it.

She read it twice. She read it the way she had read the photograph of the arm — not because it would change, but because she could not yet make it mean what it meant.

The typed name under the stamp was the name of a patient. She knew the name. She had read it three days ago on a credenza in a second-floor office that faced the water, in the careful, sympathetic voice of Adam Reyes, who had stood beside a sun-faded photograph and said the woman had drowned, and that Vivian had kept her there because it kept her honest. The name belonged to a girl who had been dead for fifteen years.

The referral that had flagged Vivian Mercer's death for Elena's unit — the nudge that had helped steer the case toward the one detective in Hartwell Bay who would chase it into the ground — had been signed, on the morning of the death, in the name of someone who could not have signed anything at all.

"Detective?" Sandoval said. "You've gone somewhere. Do you know this name?"

Elena straightened. The radiator ticked. Across the bay, though she could not see it from this window, she knew the gold square in the clinic's east wing would be unlit now in the daylight, waiting for dark to become a lighthouse again, and she thought: they didn't use a fake name to hide. They used a dead one. They used a dead girl's name to walk me through the door, and they made sure I'd recognize it later — once I'd gone looking — so I would understand it had always been a hand on my back, and not a coincidence at all.

"I know it," Elena said quietly. "She's been dead since before my sister ever set foot in that building." She met Sandoval's eyes. "Lieutenant — don't reassign me. Not yet. Whoever signed this wanted a detective with my exact wound to catch this case. If you take me off it, you don't protect the investigation. You just hand them the next person to steer."
