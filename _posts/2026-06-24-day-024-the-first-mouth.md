The launderette on Verbena Street had been closed for two winters, but the dryers still turned if you fed them, and Elena had learned that a row of empty machines makes a sound like weather — a soft mechanical surf that swallows voices before they reach the glass. She had asked Noah to meet her there because it was a place with no record of anyone, a storefront the city had forgotten between a shuttered tailor and a check-cashing window, and because a man who said a dead woman's name at full volume needed, just now, to learn the trick of saying things quietly.

He came in stamping the cold off his shoes, lab coat still under his parka, the lanyard of too many keys tucked away for once. He had brought, without being asked, two coffees in paper cups, and the small offering of it — the assumption that they were the kind of people who brought each other things — made her throat tighten in a way she did not have time for.

"You look like you haven't slept," he said.

"Neither do you."

"I've decided sleep is for after." He sat on the long folding bench under the out-of-order sign, knees together, and turned the cup in his hands a quarter turn, and then another, and she thought, not for the first time, how many careful frightened people she had met who could not let an object simply sit. "You said the strip. On the phone. You said the window."

She had said almost nothing on the phone. That was the point. She took out the photograph she had printed at a drugstore kiosk two towns over — the enlargement of the planted cassette's bare side, the side the tape's own annotation had wanted read last — and she had cropped it so that only the corner showed: the tiny deliberate glyph she had once dismissed as a smudge. Four little panes around a center. A window divided into lights. She set it on the bench between them face-up and put her finger beside it, not on it.

"Tell me about this one," she said. "Not the L-codes. Not the dates. This."

Noah went still in the particular way he had, the stillness of someone who has just recognized his own handwriting in a stranger's pocket. "That's hers," he said. "That's the one she carried by hand."

"You told me she crossed out the old mark in front of you. Chose this instead."

"She never did that. Cross things out where I could see. She'd make me retype a slip three times so it set, she said, so it set, like the words were clay — but she never let me watch her change her mind." He pressed his thumb to the rim of the cup. "That one she did. She took the strip and she put a line through the old symbol and she drew this little window and she said, this one I'll carry by hand. And then she looked at me the way she looked at me the one time I touched the gray door's handle. Like she was sorry, but only a little, and mostly she was filing me."

"What was the old symbol?"

This was the thing she had driven across two towns and slept four hours to ask. Reyes had recited the second clause across a diner table eighteen hours ago — the water closed and the lantern stayed lit — a sentence on no door and no wall, a sentence she had heard only in two dead voices on a tape she had played alone above the strait. She could not spend that. She had decided that in a parked car with frost on the windshield: the knowledge was true regardless of how she felt, but it was a word welded to her own palm, and to lift it she would have to expose the off-grid tape and her concealment and hand the whole brittle architecture to Sandoval, who would, kindly, pull her off the case and hand them the next person to steer. So she had set it down. And she had chosen instead the colder, slower vector, the one that did not require her to be believed: the catechism was a script, and a script has a master copy, a page, a first mouth. Find the page, find the room. And the page, if it existed, lived inside the same strip Noah had built — under the one symbol Vivian had reached in at the very end and pulled out of the cold inventory and warmed in her own hand.

"I've been trying to remember it since you left the dock," Noah said. "It won't come the way the others come. The others I can almost see — a little crossed circle, a thing like a tally, an anchor she drew badly. But the one under the window —" He shut his eyes. "She drew it small. And then she drew the window over it, right over the same spot, so for a second both marks were on the strip at once. I only saw it for a second."

"What did you see."

"A door," he said. "Or half of one. A line down and a line across — but the across-line wasn't centered. It was low. Near the bottom. Like a sill." He opened his eyes and they were wet and frightened and trying very hard to be useful. "I keep thinking it was a number that got dressed up as a shape. I keep thinking I'm making it into a window because you said window."

That was the honest thing, and it was why she trusted him, and it was why he was in danger. She did not tell him that the across-line low to the bottom was the exact geometry of the gold-pane glyph she had been chasing, the square with the horizontal bar set low, like a pane, like the window across the water that turned gold every night for an audience of one. She did not tell him because the moment a thing arrived because she wanted it, she could not trust it, and she had taught herself that the hard way, on a referral signed in the name of a girl fifteen years drowned.

"The strip," she said instead. "Where would she keep a thing like that. The physical strip."

"Where she kept everything." He said it simply, and then his face did something complicated. "Behind the gray door. That's the whole answer to every question you've been asking me, isn't it. It's all the same answer. The tapes, the strip, the codes — it's all up that back stair, behind a door that opens by morning and I've never once seen open."

"Don't," she said.

"I didn't say I would."

"I mean it, Noah. Don't go up there. Don't try the handle again to be helpful. The last person who got close to that woman's records spent an unhurried hour in a sealed room being made to look like he sat down on his own." She heard her own voice go flat and careful, the cold clerk speaking, and she made herself soften it because he was not an instrument no matter how the program's verb kept trying to climb into her mouth. "Promise me. If remembering the symbol starts to cost your life instead of your nerve, you stop, you go somewhere with people in it, and you call me."

"My nerve I can spend," he said, the same thing he'd said on the dock. "I've been spending it since the third."

"Spend less of it."

The dryers turned. Outside, the foghorn answered nothing past the breakwater, and the cold came under the door in a thin blade and lay along the floor. She watched him turn the cup a third time and she thought about Reyes turning his coffee cup eighteen hours ago, the identical small economy of a hand that needs a job, and she thought about Gerald turning his cup, and Clara and her glass, all of them careful damaged people giving their hands something to hold so the hands would not say the rest. She had not known, until this week, how many of them there were, walking the city, set, finished, turning small objects. She did not let herself count.

"He came to see me again," Noah said, into the surf of the machines. "Yesterday. After you and he had your coffee, I think. He made the tea himself, like he does." He did not say the name. By now neither of them said it, as if it were a tab pushed shut on a shell. "He told me you'd be carrying a lot alone and I should let him know if you asked me to do anything that frightened me. So that he could help me carry it. Those were the words. Help me carry it."

The clerk in her wrote it down without a tremor, in the same column as the diner, in the same column as the morning he stood at an empty hook before any detective arrived and began managing how the missing key would be discussed. Over-helpfulness. The hand on the small of the back you mistake for tenderness until you notice it is steering. She could not spend that either. It fit both doors. Every fact about the man fit both doors; the trap had been built without a third.

"What did you tell him," she said.

"I told him you'd asked me about office supplies." Noah almost smiled. "Which is true. Tape is an office supply." Then the almost-smile went out. "He asked me yesterday whether you'd asked me to make a copy of anything. Just like that. Make a copy. And I said no, because you hadn't, and his face didn't change, but he poured the tea like the question had already been answered before he asked it."

Elena's coffee had gone cold in her hand. She set it on the bench very precisely so that her own hand would have nothing to do.

Eighteen hours ago, leaving the diner, the man had told her gently not to carry the only copy of a thing — put a copy somewhere a friend would think to look if you went quiet, that's me asking you to outlive it. A man who loved her would say that. A man measuring whether a copy existed, how many, and where, would say exactly that, in exactly that voice. She had given him nothing. And then, three blocks later, before she had told a single living soul, a fourth number she had never seen had texted her three lowercase words — he asked you to make a copy — and the floor of her stomach had dropped, because the only two people in the world who knew what Reyes had said in that booth were Reyes and Elena, and one of them had not told.

Now the second of those two people was sitting across from her in a dead launderette saying the man had asked him the same question, in the same words, on the same day. Make a copy. As if the man were walking a circuit of everyone near her, asking the same question at each station, listening not for the answer but for the place where the answer would eventually come from. As if he already knew a copy was the thing that could end him, and was simply finding out which hand would be holding it.

"Noah," she said, and her voice came out level only because she made it. "When he asked you that. Was anyone else in the room."

"No. Just us. The annex." He frowned. "Why."

Because, she did not say, a fourth number told me he asked me that, before I had told anyone, and now you tell me he asked you the same thing, and I am trying very hard to find the innocent shape of a man who keeps appearing at the exact mouth of every well I lower a bucket into. She thought of the tape's own annotation, the small ballpoint hand under for the audience: now turn it over. She had turned it over. She had turned over the thinking and not just the reel, and what she found was not a name but a method — that the watcher and the man and the missing copy were not three problems but one problem wearing three coats, and that every door she chose had already been the door someone wanted her to choose, which was the exact horror Mara's flattened voice had named above the strait: I can't tell anymore which of the things I feel are mine.

She made herself stand. The cold came up through her shoes.

"Go home the long way," she said. "Sit somewhere with a window only if the window faces a wall. And the symbol — don't strain for it. If it comes, it comes when you're not looking. Call me from a phone that isn't yours and just say the shape. Don't say my name. Don't say his."

"You make it sound like the building's listening."

"The building isn't listening." She gathered the photograph, slid it into her coat, felt the four little panes through the paper against her ribs like a small lit window held to the chest. "A man is. And he asks everyone the same question until he hears it come back from the right direction."

She let him leave first, watched him cross Verbena with his collar up and his too-many keys silent, watched until he was a coat among coats and gone. Then she stood in the surf of the dryers a moment longer, alone with the four panes against her heart and the cold lying along the floor like a thing that had been poured there and left to set, and she took out her phone to call no one — to call the recycled Bremerton number, the way her body sometimes wanted, the way you press a bruise to be sure it is yours.

The screen was already lit.

A new message, from a number she had not seen before — a fifth, local prefix, not the clinic exchange, not Mara's recycled line, not the pier, not the fourth. Five channels now, and the city had only so many ways to reach a person. The message was four words, no punctuation, and it had arrived, the timestamp said, at the exact minute Noah had pushed open the launderette door to leave.

it came back wrong direction
