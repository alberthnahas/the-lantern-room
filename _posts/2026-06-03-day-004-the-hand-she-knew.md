The drawer by her bed did not open easily, because she had made it that way. Elena kept the runner dry on purpose, no wax, no oil, so that it resisted, so that there was a small daily friction between her and the thing inside it — a tax she paid each time she went looking, a way of asking herself whether she was sure. Tonight she was sure. She knelt on the floor in the dark of her apartment with the harbor light coming gray through the blinds and worked the drawer out by degrees, and it gave its low wooden complaint, and there it was where it had been for six years: a manila folder gone soft as cloth at the creases, photocopied pages inside, the toner faded at the folds to the color of old bruises.

She had not eaten. She had driven back across the bay with the clinic's gold window burning in her mirror and had not stopped for the diner or the lights of her own kitchen; she had come straight to the drawer, because the thing that had snagged on her in that windowless records office was still snagged, a splinter under the nail of her attention, and she had learned long ago that splinters did not wait politely to be dealt with later. They worked deeper. They went septic. The only cure was to take the tweezers to them in the dark while your hands still remembered the shape of the wound.

She laid the photocopy on the floorboards and turned on the small lamp she kept low, the one that did not reach the windows, and beside the folder she set her phone, and on the phone the photograph she had taken three hours earlier of a logbook page hung from a chain by a chewed pen on a string. *V. MERCER — HOME OFC.* The empty hook. The line three up from the bottom, October the twenty-ninth, the borrower's column where every other entry carried a name in the records clerk's breathing hand and this one carried only a cramped, pressed, overbuilt thing — a capital at the front, a long descending tail at the end, and between them a knot of letters that had tried to be legible and gone at the same time.

She opened the folder.

She did not need to read it. She had read it so many times that the reading happened without her, the way a hymn happens to someone raised in a church they have since left: the words arriving in order, unbidden, complete. *Patient self-reported escalating ideation. Affect flat. Insight limited. In my clinical judgment the act was deliberate and self-directed; there is no evidence of third-party involvement.* And at the bottom, above the typed name and the credential and the date that had ended one life and rerouted another, the signature. *V. Mercer.* Vivian Mercer's own hand, the assessment that had closed the file on Mara Vega, twenty-nine years old, found in the bath of a rented room with the water gone cold, ruled by her own physician a death she had chosen.

Elena had hated that signature for six years. She had memorized it the way you memorize the face of the person who did the thing — every curve of it, the confident downstroke of the *V*, the way the *Mercer* trailed off into a line that was less a name than a closing of accounts. She knew that hand the way she knew her own. It was not the hand on the logbook.

She had known it would not be, walking in. That was not the splinter.

The splinter was the second signature.

Because the assessment was not the only page in the folder. Behind it, paper-clipped, soft, was the intake sheet — the form Mara had filled out herself the first day she walked into the Mercer Clinic, six years and some months ago, before any of it, when she was still a woman with a problem and not yet a closed file. Elena had taken the whole record, legally and otherwise, in the weeks after, when grief made her reckless and a sympathetic clerk in records had let her photocopy more than she should have. She had read the assessment ten thousand times. The intake sheet she had barely been able to look at — Mara's own handwriting, the careful block capitals her sister used when she wanted to be taken seriously, the home address in their mother's old apartment, the box checked for *referred by* with a name written in beside it that Elena had never been able to make herself care about, because it was just a name, just whoever had sent her sister through that door.

She slid the intake sheet out now and laid it beside the phone, and the lamp lay its small yellow weight across both, and she looked from one to the other, and the floor of her stomach went out from under her the way it had gone out at the desk of the dead woman two mornings ago when she understood that nothing here was an accident.

The name in the *referred by* box was cramped. Pressed hard enough to dent the paper. A capital at the front, a long descending tail at the end, and between them a knot of letters that had tried to be legible and gone at the same time.

The same hand. Six years apart. The hand that had referred her sister into the Mercer Clinic was the hand that had signed a key to a dead woman's office out of a steel cabinet six days before that woman died with the lock turned against her.

She sat very still. She did not let herself leap, because leaping was the thing she did not allow herself, the discipline that separated her from the version of herself that had spent the first year after Mara lost in a fog of coincidence, finding her sister's killer in every shadow and every signature, building cases out of grief and watching them collapse the moment a colder eye touched them. She had taught herself, at great cost, to treat her own certainty as the least reliable witness in any room. So she did not leap. She did the slow thing instead. She got a sheet of clean paper and a pencil and she traced — not the letters, she could not read the letters, but the *shape* of them, the rhythm, the place where the pen had dug in and the place where it had lifted, the way you'd compare two pieces of music by their rise and fall when you could not read the notes. The pressure pattern matched. The slant matched. The strange overbuilding — the writer's evident wish to leave a mark and leave no name in the same stroke — matched.

It was the same hand. She was as sure of that as she let herself be sure of anything, which was to say: enough to follow, not enough to file.

And the following led somewhere she did not want it to go, because she could feel, sitting on her own cold floor, the shape of the trap closing — not around the dead woman, but around herself. Six years ago someone had referred Mara Vega into the Mercer Clinic, into the care of a woman who would later sign her death. Six days ago that same someone had taken the key to that woman's locked office. And four days ago that woman had died, and Elena Vega — Mara's sister, who had spent six years convincing the department she had no personal stake in anything touching the name Mercer, who had requested proximity to every Mercer-adjacent case that crossed the wire and been quietly, carefully refused until now — Elena Vega had been handed this one. Adeyemi had handed it to her himself, in the dark, at 4:51 in the morning, as though it were any other call.

*You were the one they got*, she had thought, standing over the body. She had meant the killer wanted a careful person to find it. She was no longer sure that was the whole sentence.

She made herself breathe. She made herself do the discipline. There were innocent explanations, and she listed them aloud to the empty room the way her old training officer had taught her, because a suspicion you could not argue against was not a lead, it was a feeling wearing a coat. The referral hand and the logbook hand could resemble each other and not be the same; cramped pressed handwriting was common, was practically a genre, every doctor and every frightened patient wrote like that. The clerk who'd let her copy the file could have transposed a page from another patient's record by accident. The October twenty-ninth signature could be perfectly innocent — a staffer fetching the key for a legitimate reason, a name only illegible because the pen on the chain was bad and the page was on a clipboard against a steel door.

She wrote them all down. She believed none of them, and she noted that she believed none of them, and she put a star beside the noting, because the day she stopped catching herself believing was the day she'd build another case out of bruised photocopies and watch it fall.

But there was a thing the innocent explanations could not reach, and it sat at the bottom of all of them like a stone at the bottom of clear water. She had never once, in six years, looked hard at the *referred by* box. She had been so consumed by the signature at the bottom of the assessment — by Vivian, by the woman she could name and hate and visit in her mind — that she had let the name at the top of the file, the door her sister had been walked through, sit unread for six years. Someone had sent Mara into that building. Elena had never asked who. She had been looking at the woman in the chair, the way the suicide note told her to. *There is no one to blame but the woman in this chair.* She had spent six years blaming exactly the person she had been instructed to blame, and it had never once occurred to her to wonder who had written the instructions.

The lantern is lit, the dead woman had texted her husband at midnight. I tried to undo it.

Undo what. The thing she had done to Mara? Or the thing she had let someone else do, in her building, under her name, with her signature waiting at the end of it like a stamp on a passport?

Elena's phone buzzed against the floorboards and she nearly came off the ground. A text, this hour, from a number with no name attached — but she knew the area exchange, the clinic's exchange, the same three digits that had glowed MERCER CLINIC on a dead house's caller display at 4:46 in the morning. Her thumb hovered. She thought of the foghorn behind the silent open line, the listener down by the water. She opened it.

It was not words. It was a photograph. Grainy, dark, shot through glass beaded with rain — but unmistakable: her own building, her own lit window, the low lamp she kept turned away from the blinds throwing its small yellow weight onto a ceiling someone outside could not possibly see unless they already knew exactly which window was hers and had stood in the rain long enough to be sure.

Below the photograph, three words, in a hand she could not read but already, God help her, knew.

*Don't trace this.*

She did not move. The harbor light came gray through the blinds, and somewhere out past the water the foghorn opened its long low throat and held the note, and Elena Vega understood that she had not, after all, been the one watching the lighthouse.
