The sedan had been sitting on Verbena Street for eleven minutes when Elena and Adeyemi came around the corner on foot, and in that time it had not once cut its engine.

She could see the exhaust doing the talking for it — a slow gray plume climbing straight up into air gone still and close, the first real fog of the week rolling off the strait and settling over the block like something laid down on purpose. The streetlight two poles up wore a soft corona. Noah's building was a dark brick rectangle behind it, four stories, a single lit window on the third floor that she had learned, over three weeks of watching it from cars, belonged to an insomniac named Delacroix who worked nights at the ferry terminal and never once been the reason for anyone's worry.

"Dark green, older Impala, no plate light working," Adeyemi said, low, not looking at it directly, the two of them walking the way people walk when they are only headed to their own car. "Ray's been on it since four. Says the guy hasn't so much as cracked a window."

"Ray have eyes on the driver?"

"Silhouette. Big through the shoulders. Ballcap." Adeyemi's breath came out white and unhurried. "That's it. That's the whole file."

They were forty feet out when the sedan's brake lights woke, dull red smearing into the fog, and it pulled off the curb without haste, without a single wasted motion, the exact unbothered pace of a man who had already decided, the moment he heard two car doors, that he'd stayed exactly as long as he meant to and not one second more.

Elena ran the last of the distance anyway, not because she believed she'd catch it but because a body in motion made a different kind of report to whoever might be reading her later, and some report was better than the alternative. She got close enough to watch the tail end vanish around the corner toward the cannery road, close enough to read, for perhaps three-quarters of a second before the fog took it, a rear plate frame stamped in a car-lot font she'd seen a hundred times without ever once needing it: CASCADE FLEET RENTALS — SEA-TAC. No plate number. The bulb behind it really was out, or had been removed, which was its own kind of information.

"Rental," she said, more to the fog than to Adeyemi.

"Half this coast rents from Sea-Tac." He said it like a man talking himself down off a ledge he hadn't climbed yet. "Doesn't mean anything on its own."

"No." She wrote it down anyway, in the notebook she'd started keeping the week Osei showed her the puncture, the one with no name on the cover. CASCADE FLEET. Frame only, no plate. Third-floor Delacroix window still lit. Fog arriving 6:40ish. She had learned, across seven weeks, that the things that meant nothing on their own were the only things the case had ever given her for free.

Ray, the off-book watcher — a retired transit cop Adeyemi trusted the way you trust a man who has already lost the thing procedure was supposed to protect him from — met them at the mouth of the alley in a coat too thin for the cold, apologetic before either of them could ask. "Didn't want to spook it early," he said. "Figured live sighting beats a scared-off nothing."

"You figured right," Adeyemi told him, and meant it, and Elena watched something in the older man's shoulders come down half an inch at being believed rather than blamed. It was, she thought, the exact gesture Adeyemi had been making toward her for two months, and she had been too far inside her own case to notice how much of his job, lately, had become talking frightened, well-meaning people out of feeling like failures.

Noah's window, fourth floor, was dark. He'd told her that morning he'd be at his aunt's in Puyallup by early afternoon, finishing the reconstruction, and would call once he was somewhere with a landline. It was not yet four. She told herself the dark window meant nothing more than a man keeping his own schedule.

—

Sandoval's summons came at 4:50, delivered by a patrol officer with the particular blank face of someone who has been told not to characterize the tone of a request.

The fourth-floor office still had its view of the parking structure and not the water, still no soft place for the eye to rest, and Sandoval was standing rather than sitting when Elena came in, which was new.

"Marcus Choi's report crossed my desk two hours ago," Sandoval said, without preamble. "Before I ask you anything, I want you to understand I read it twice, because the first time through, the account creation date looked like a typo."

"It isn't."

"No." Sandoval set a single printed page on the desk between them, turned so Elena could read it without reaching. "October twenty-first. Nine days before the discovery. You weren't assigned. You hadn't asked your captain — on the record, at least."

"I asked off the record. Years ago. A standing request. If anything ever came in touching the Institute, I wanted to know."

"I know you did. Choi's report doesn't know that, and neither does the language IA uses for a device syncing audio to an unlinked account for six weeks without the owner's knowledge." Sandoval's voice stayed level, which was, Elena had come to understand, the sound of a woman being fairer to her than the facts strictly required. "I'm not opening a tampering file against you. You didn't create the account and you reported it the hour you found it. But I can't leave a compromised line of communication running under a detective's badge on an active homicide, and I can't take your word alone that your phone's the only device it touched."

"What does that mean today."

"It means your personal cell goes to Choi tonight, alongside the department phone, for the same audit — clean chain, timestamped, back to you by tomorrow afternoon at the latest." Sandoval slid a form across, already half filled out in her own hand. "I know what tomorrow is for you. Adeyemi doesn't hide much from me, and neither, lately, do you. If there's a call you're expecting, make it before six tonight, on a line that isn't yours, and I will not ask whose."

It was, Elena understood, as close to a kindness as Sandoval had ever offered her, and also a closing door, and the two things sat in the room together without contradicting each other, which she had learned was how most of the real kindnesses in this case arrived.

"Six o'clock first light tomorrow," Elena said. "That's the plan I'm expecting a call about."

"Then you have an hour and ten minutes to hear whatever you need to hear on a phone I can't later be told grew ears of its own." Sandoval almost smiled. "Sign here. And Vega — whoever built that account chose October twenty-first for a reason. People that patient don't stop being patient because one microphone got found. They start using a second one."

—

She borrowed Adeyemi's phone in the stairwell, because it felt truer to call from a place with no soft chairs in it, and Noah picked up on the second ring, out of breath in a way that had nothing to do with fear — he'd been carrying a space heater up from his aunt's cellar, he said, the Puyallup house was old and cold and his aunt kept threatening to sell it and never did.

"You sound better," Elena said, and meant it as a question.

"I finished the middle third today." She could hear him moving around a room, a door easing shut, the particular hush of someone choosing a quiet corner on purpose. "The part I didn't want to write. The sessions from the year before Sophie. I didn't know Vivian had a — a rough draft of it. Before it was good. Before it worked."

"Noah."

"I'm all right. I want to bring you the whole thing, not pieces. Tomorrow, first light, same spot as Sunday. I already told the roommate I'm staying another night for my aunt." A pause, and under it, faint, the tick of an old radiator finding its voice. "There's one more thing. The repeating number. I think I know what it is now, but I want to check it against something here first — she kept an old appointment ledger, my aunt has boxes of Vivian's discarded office supplies, don't ask, long story — and if I'm right I don't want to say it over a phone that isn't yours anyway."

"Say it to me tomorrow. In person. That's an order, not a request."

"Copy that, Detective." He said it lightly, the way he'd said things lightly since October, as armor rather than carelessness, and then, quieter: "My mother used to say the only rooms worth trusting were the ones you'd already cleaned yourself. I used to think that meant leaving nothing behind. I don't think that anymore. I think it means knowing exactly what's in them." A breath. "First light. I'll bring all of it."

"Adeyemi will be at the turnout the same as Sunday. Don't come early, don't come late, and if anything feels wrong—"

"I call you first and the terminal second. I know." Warmth, real and tired, came through the line the way it did in the ordinary parts of people's lives, the parts that never made it into a case file. "Thank you for believing me before you had a reason to."

He hung up before she could answer that, which she understood, later, to have been its own kind of mercy.

—

She handed Adeyemi back his phone in the stairwell, the fog against the window at the landing gone thick enough to erase the parking structure entirely, and for a moment neither of them said anything, the day's whole ledger — the sedan, the rental frame, Sandoval's form, Noah's voice going warm and tired and almost happy — settling into the particular quiet of a plan that had, for once, gone exactly as it was supposed to.

Then the phone buzzed once in Adeyemi's hand, and he looked at it the way a man looks at a thing he already knows he won't like, and turned the screen so she could read it without reaching.

Unknown number. No name, no history, a line that had never texted him before tonight and had somehow already learned the one fact Elena had spoken aloud in exactly two places — a stairwell landing, and a phone that was not, that was never, her own.

Six words, no punctuation at the end, the way the watcher never bothered with question marks anymore because it had stopped needing to ask.

First light. Same spot. Tell him not to bring all of it.

Elena's own phone — clean, Choi's word for it, severed and logged and sitting in an evidence bag two floors below them — had nothing to do with this. Sandoval's line was still open in her head: they don't stop being patient. They start using a second one.

She understood, standing in the fog-blind stairwell with her partner's phone lit between them, that the second one had never been hers to find. It had been listening to Noah the whole time, in a house with an old cold radiator and boxes of a dead woman's discarded things, and it had just told her, in six flat words, that it already knew what he meant to bring her before she did.
