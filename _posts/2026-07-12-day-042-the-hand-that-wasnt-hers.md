She did not stand in the old terminal working out how. That was the discipline she had built for herself over five weeks and it held even now, with the cold coming up through the bench into her spine and the certainty, fresh and total, that whatever had learned to hear Noah Okafor whisper a dead woman's second name had not needed a wire to do it. How was a question with no clock on it. First Strait Maritime closed at five on Fridays — she had banked there herself, years ago, before she moved to the Heights, and she still had that particular fact filed the way she filed everything useless until suddenly it wasn't. It was ten past one. She had under four hours, and the only clock that mattered today was the one that ended with a teller pulling down a grate.

She left the terminal at a walk that became, once she was out of sight of the boarded windows, something closer to a run.

In the car she did not call Adeyemi. She turned that decision over once, briefly, the way she turned over every decision that looked like caution and was actually its opposite — an affidavit meant a judge, and a judge on a Friday afternoon meant hours she didn't have, and worse, it meant the request would exist on paper, in a system that had already shown her, more than once, that paper moved faster to the wrong readers than to the right ones. A subpoena for box 214 would need to say why, and why was Noah, and Noah had told her himself, in a room with nothing between them, that nobody was safe to use even once anymore. She was not going to spend him to save three hours.

Her phone rang on the bridge, the number a name this time and not an absence of one, and she almost didn't answer it, and then did, because not answering was its own kind of information and she had grown very tired of feeding the machine data by omission.

"I heard about Voss," Reyes said, without preamble, which was its own kind of tell — he never opened cold unless he'd already decided exactly how warm to get by the second sentence. "I wanted to say, before you heard it from someone less careful with it: I'm sorry. Whatever else that man was, he watched a window for two years because some part of him needed to keep faith with something, and I think you're one of the only people in this city who understood that about him before he died."

"Thank you," she said, and meant the smallest possible fraction of it, and let the rest sit unanswered on the line the way she'd learned to let his kindness sit — received, not returned.

"If there's anything you need today," he said. "Anything at all. A door I can open faster than the department can, a call I can make that saves you a form. I mean that without asking what it's for. You've spent five weeks doing this the hard way because the easy way always seems to have my fingerprints on it, and I understand why, but I want you to know the offer doesn't have a string on it just because you're afraid of the hand."

The bridge deck ticked under her tires, seam after seam, and she watched the harbor slide by gray and chopped under the last of the blowing snow, and thought that if she had needed, this exact hour, a door opened faster than the department could open it, a call that saved her a form — she could not have written a more precise description of what she actually needed if she had handed him the list herself. She did not tell him what she was driving toward. She said she'd call if anything changed, and hung up before he could hear what her silence was doing, and drove the rest of the bridge with both hands tight on the wheel, thinking: he is never wrong about the shape of the need. That is either because he has spent thirty years around people in need, or because he already knows the exact size and location of this particular hole. Both had always fit. She had stopped expecting that to change and started, instead, simply logging how often it happened.

First Strait Maritime sat where it had sat for sixty years, a squat granite building with brass-barred windows and a ship's-wheel clock over the door that was, as always, four minutes fast, as if the bank had long ago decided its customers should feel behind before they'd even come inside. The lot had been plowed once and refrozen; she parked crooked and didn't care. Inside, the lobby smelled the way she remembered it — old paper, radiator heat, the particular hush of a room where money was handled slowly and on purpose.

Dolores Pham was at her window, reading glasses on their beaded chain, steadiest hands on the case, and she looked up before Elena had crossed half the lobby with the expression of a woman who has been half-expecting a visit for longer than she'd admit to.

"Detective." Pham said it quietly, glancing once toward the manager's glass-walled office at the back, where a man in a good suit was on the phone with his back to the floor. "You want to talk somewhere that isn't this counter."

They ended up in the little windowless room behind the safe-deposit vault where new customers signed their cards, a table, two chairs, a filing cabinet with a hand-lettered label reading ARCHIVE — DO NOT REMOVE that had clearly not been updated since a manager three managers ago. Pham closed the door with the particular care of someone who had spent forty years learning which doors in a bank needed to be shut all the way and which only needed to look shut.

"I've been waiting for someone to ask me about the box," Pham said, before Elena had said anything at all. "I didn't know who to tell, or if there was anyone to tell, or if I was imagining a thing that was really just an old woman noticing an old woman."

"Tell me about V. Marsh."

Pham took her glasses off, folded them, and held them in both hands the way some people hold rosaries. "Mrs. Marsh came to this branch for two years to visit box two-fourteen. Plain coat, plain scarf, never any jewelry, never any small talk beyond the weather — the opposite of the woman I knew from my own window, who tipped her head back to laugh at things that weren't especially funny and always, always looked like she'd rather be having a harder conversation than the one she was in. I signed Mrs. Marsh into the vault log a dozen times over two years and never once thought about her twice, because she was built, I think now, to be a woman nobody thinks about twice." She set the glasses down on the table between them, precisely, as if placing evidence. "Then the news ran her photograph. The good one, from the clinic's website, the one where she's got that look. And I stood in my own kitchen with the television on and I thought: that's Mrs. Marsh. That's the coat under a better coat."

"Did you tell anyone."

"Who would I tell." Pham said it without bitterness, a simple accounting of the facts of her life. "It's not my account. It's not my business what a customer calls herself upstairs as long as the paperwork's clean, and hers always was — a small local trust, a notarized alias authorization, the kind of thing a bank sees more often than people think, widows and second wives and men hiding money from ex-spouses. I filed it under none of my business, same as I file everything, until a detective who once asked me kind questions about a dead woman's cash came and asked me a name I hadn't said out loud to a living soul."

Elena felt something settle in her chest that was not quite relief. "I need to see the vault sign-in log. Not the box. I know I can't have the box."

"I can't let you touch the log either, not officially. It's a security record, and the branch manager back there would have my job and mine before yours." Pham's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "But I've been keeping this counter thirty-one years, and I've read that book to myself more than I've read anything else in my life, and there is nothing wrong with an old woman telling a detective what she remembers reading in a book she happens to be allowed to hold."

She did not open a ledger. She recited it, the way Marguerite had once recited nineteen years of a different logbook to a different frightened woman, the pattern of allies in this case revealing themselves not through documents handed over but through documents remembered aloud, which no rule anywhere had thought to forbid.

"Twenty-six visits in two years, near enough monthly, sometimes closer together in the last few months. Always alone. Always that same flat, quiet signature — Mrs. Marsh signed her name the way a woman signs a form she wishes didn't need her name on it at all, small, careful, the same shape every single time, because a woman keeping a secret for two years learns to make her lie look exactly the same twice." Pham's eyes had gone somewhere else, into the book she was reading from memory. "The last visit is the one I haven't been able to put down since I saw that photograph."

"When."

"The morning after. Not the morning the news broke — the morning after she died, before anyone outside that house knew anything at all. We open at nine. This name is the first line in the book that day. Not first that morning — first that week. Whoever it was, was standing outside these doors in the cold before Rosalind even had the grate up."

Elena did not move. Outside the little room she could hear, faintly, the lobby's radiator ticking, a customer's voice asking about a cashier's check, the ordinary machinery of a Friday afternoon grinding on around a fact that had just changed the shape of everything she thought she understood about the hour after Vivian Mercer died.

"You said it was the same signature. Mrs. Marsh's signature."

"I said that's what I thought, reading fast, at nine in the morning, before I'd had my second coffee, with three people waiting at my own window." Pham picked her glasses back up and did not put them on. "I went back and looked at it properly two nights ago, after the photograph, because something about it had been sitting wrong in me for weeks and I finally let myself sit with it instead of past it. It's close. It is very close. But I have watched that particular hand sign that particular name twenty-five times, and the twenty-sixth is not tired the way a hand gets tired copying itself for two years. It's careful the way a hand gets careful copying somebody else."

The radiator ticked again. Somewhere behind Elena's ribs the whole architecture of the last six weeks shifted half an inch and did not fall, exactly, but stopped standing quite where she'd left it.

Not staged after the fact, in an hour bought by an interrupted lividity clock. Rehearsed. Practiced somewhere, on some other sheet of paper, until a hand that was not Vivian Mercer's could put on Vivian Mercer's second name the way a coat gets put on before you walk out into weather you already know is coming — and then worn to a bank counter in the freezing dark of the very first morning, before the department had a case number, before Elena had a body to stand over, before anyone in the world but a killer knew there was anything left to hide.

"Mrs. Pham," she said carefully, "is there a security camera on that vault floor."

"There was. Budget cut it two winters ago — same as half this branch's wiring. We're old money pretending we don't need new locks." Pham looked at her, steady, unembarrassed, the way she had looked at her once before across a different counter about a different dead woman's habits. "I keep asking. Nobody upstairs thinks an old bank on a dying street is worth the expense of proving who comes and goes in it."

Elena thanked her, and meant all of it this time, and walked back out through the lobby past the ship's-wheel clock four minutes ahead of everyone in the room, and stood on the granite steps in the last gray hour of the light with the wind coming hard off the water, and understood that somewhere in this city there was a hand that had learned to become another woman's hand well enough to fool a teller who'd watched the real one sign for two straight years — and that hand had been standing in this exact cold, on these exact steps, waiting for a door to open, before Vivian Mercer's body had even gone stiff in her chair.
