She had not slept, and so the morning found her the way mornings found her now — already awake, already at the kitchen table, the bad drawer by the bed left open behind her in the dark with the photocopy taken out of it. Friday came up gray over the Heights and made no promises. Frost had gone past surrendering to the sun days ago; it sat in the seams of the window glass like something poured and left to set, and beyond it the city assembled itself one cold roof at a time. Elena did not look at the city. She looked at her sister's intake sheet, turned over to the side she had refused for six years to read, and at the box she had never once checked.

PATIENT DESIGNATION. A printed administrative rectangle, the kind that existed on a thousand forms to be left blank. It was not blank. In the cramped, vanishing hand she had now seen five times in eleven days — the hand that referred Mara in, signed out the office key, walked through Sophie Lund's file, received a doctor's keys at a lock shop two years gone — someone had written a letter and a number. On the photocopy it was faint, a ghost of pressure transferred through carbon and a clerk's careless angle, but it was there, and it was legible, and she had spent the night making certain she was not inventing it.

She was not. The letter was L. The number she would not write down yet, not even here, not even in her own kitchen, because the kitchen was a room and rooms could be heard and she had learned that lesson in a car above the water with a foam earpiece pressed to one ear. She had learned, too, that the watcher answered her inquiries in real time, and she did not intend to hand them confirmation that she had read the box. Let the silence run both ways for once.

L, and a number. Her sister had not been a patient. Her sister had been a subject.

The word had a temperature. She had spent six years building a small, survivable architecture around Mara's death — a building with one room in it, and Vivian Mercer in the chair, and a signed assessment on the wall that said *the act was deliberate and self-directed; there is no evidence of third-party involvement.* It had been a terrible building but it had been a building, and Elena had lived in it. Now she understood that the assessment was not the architecture. It was the plaster over a hole. *Subject* meant Mara had not walked into the Mercer Institute to be helped. She had walked in to be made into something — the way the boy on the rocks had been made into a man who could recite the closing of water over a girl's head like a grace before dinner. *Graduating,* he had called it. *L-9 graduated,* Vivian had said, not looking up. And somewhere in a list that Elena had not yet seen, in a hand taught to disappear, there was an L and a number that was Mara's, and a tape that held whatever was left of her sister's voice after the work was done.

She made herself put the photocopy down. She made herself drink the coffee. The discipline was not virtue; it was survival. *A detective who knelt where she was told and heard what she wanted was no detective at all.* She had said it to herself in the car above the strait and she said it again now, and it held, barely, the way a cleat holds a line in a current that wants the line.

Because here was the thing she could not let the grief swallow: the designation box on Mara's sheet was filled in by the obscured hand. The on-behalf-of line in the clinic logbook had been filled in by *Mara's own block capitals,* six years after Mara could hold a pen. Two different hands, two different times, both of them reaching across the years to touch Elena specifically. The obscured hand wrote for the world. The block capitals were written for her. Someone had not merely catalogued her sister. Someone had kept her sister's handwriting, studied it, and could produce it on demand — and had chosen to lay it down on the line that bolted Vivian Mercer's murder. That was not a record. That was a sentence with a reader in mind, and the reader was Elena, and she had taken six years to learn to read it.

The phone did not ring. She wanted it to ring. She wanted Adeyemi's name, or even Sandoval's, anything that was a person and not a window.

She did not call the Bremerton number. She wanted to — her body wanted to, the way it had always wanted to, twice a year in parking lots and stairwells, dialing the digits she knew like a scar to hear a stranger's irritated *hello* and confirm that nothing in the world was held. But the number was not hers anymore in the worst sense now. The watcher had reached her through it once already. *It isn't paid,* her sister's old line had said in a stranger's text bubble. If she dialed it she did not know who would answer, and she could not afford to find out, and the not-dialing cost her something she could feel leave her chest.

At nine she drove to the clinic. Not to the gray door — she had disciplined herself against that staircase, against a door someone else had lit a lamp behind for her. She drove to receiving, to the dock at the back of the east wing where Noah Okafor signed for tapes a dead woman was no longer alive to use, because Noah had been carrying something since the third, and because of all the people standing too close to this, he was the only one who had come to her instead of being managed away.

He was at the bottom of the back stair when she came around the building, lab coat over the two sweaters, lanyard heavy with more keys than a man of his rank should have, and the look on his face when he saw her was relief and dread fused into one expression, which she had learned to recognize as the face of someone who had decided to talk and was terrified of having decided. He had a cardboard tray of coffees from the place on Harbor Street and he handed her one without asking, the way frightened generous people did, giving you something so their hands would have done a true thing while their mouths did a hard one.

"You came back," he said. "I didn't think you would. He told me you'd route everything through him now."

"Reyes."

"He didn't say it like that. He said it so I'd feel taken care of." Noah looked at his own keys. "He's good at that. You don't notice the door closing because he's holding it for you."

She let that sit. It was the truest thing anyone had said to her about Adam Reyes, and the boy did not even know what he'd said.

"Noah. The labels." She kept her voice flat, the foghorn going somewhere out past the breakwater, gulls arguing over the gutted ice house down the road. "The ones you typed. The letter and a number. Did she ever keep a — a master. A list that turned the codes back into people."

He shook his head, then stopped. "Not a list. She wouldn't. You met her by now, even if you only met her dead — she thought a list was a confession that hadn't been read yet. *The most dangerous thing in any building is a record.*" He had her cadence. They all had her cadence; she had built them to. "But there's a—" He stopped again, and this time the dread won for a second, and she watched him decide. "There's a concordance. Not on paper. In her head, mostly. But she made me make her one tool, once, because her own hand was getting — she didn't like that I noticed. Two years ago. Her writing started to shake, only a little, only on the long sessions, and she made me build her a key strip so she could match a code to a tape without writing the name. Just the code, and a date, and a — a mark. A symbol she chose for each one. Private. So she could read it and I couldn't."

Elena's coffee had gone cold in her hand and she had not drunk it. Two years ago. The writing starting to shake. *Seed M3, confirm M9,* said a voice in her that was all detective, the part of her that had survived the kitchen and the box and the unringing phone — *that is the diagnosis, that is the clock she was running out of, and she handed the boy a piece of it without telling him what it was.*

"Where's the key strip, Noah."

"I gave it back to her. I always gave everything back." He looked sick. "But I typed it. You don't forget a thing you type for someone like her — she made you type it three times if your finger slipped, so it set. I could — " He swallowed. "I could remember the symbols. Some of them. If it mattered. If you tell me it matters more than it'll cost."

"It matters," Elena said, and hated how steady she sounded, because steadiness was the thing they had taught her sister, the thing the boy on the rocks recited into the dark, and she did not want to be good at it.

Noah set his coffee on the dock rail and looked at the gray door's general direction, two flights up and around, the way you look toward a sound you have spent four years pretending not to hear. "There was one she changed," he said. "After she'd already chosen the symbols. Only one she ever changed. Most of them she gave me cold, like inventory. This one she sat with. I'd typed a — it doesn't matter what I'd typed. She crossed it out herself, in front of me, which she never did, and she chose a new mark for it, and she said something I didn't understand and didn't ask about because you didn't ask her things." He was very pale now, the cold or the memory, she couldn't tell. "She said, *this one I'll carry by hand.*"

"What was the code."

"I don't—" He shut his eyes. "It started with L. They all started with L. I typed hundreds." He opened them. "But I'd know the symbol if I saw it. She drew it twice so the ink would take. It wasn't a letter. It was a little — a window. A square with a line through the middle, like a pane. Like the window. The one that turns gold." He laughed, once, with no humor in it. "I thought it was a coincidence. I thought she just liked the building."

The foghorn again, low and patient. Across the cold water the east wing rose against the white sky, and the single high window in it was dark now, in daylight, the way a lit thing is only ever a lit thing at night.

Elena understood several things at once, and she made herself hold each of them without letting it become the thing she wanted. She understood that there was a concordance, after all — not a list but a strip of symbols in a dead woman's hand, the one key that turned the L-codes back into the people they had unmade, and that it was somewhere the killer's unhurried hour might have missed or might have taken. She understood that one code among all of them had been singled out, recoded, marked with a little gold window and *carried by hand* — pulled out of the cold inventory of the damned and given a private weight by a woman who was running out of time and had decided to undo her own life's work. And she understood, with a certainty she could not yet prove and would not yet name, that she needed to know which subject was the one Vivian Mercer had decided to carry by hand, because a woman did not single out one of her instruments at the very end unless that one had stopped being an instrument to her and become a debt.

*The kind that isn't on any books,* the teller had said. *I'm paying a very old debt.*

"Noah," she said. "I need you to do something that is going to frighten you, and I need you to not tell Reyes you're doing it, and if at any point it feels like it's costing you your life instead of your nerve, you stop, you walk to a public place, and you call me. Do you understand the difference."

"My nerve I can spend," he said. "I've been spending it since the third."

"I need you to remember every symbol on that strip you can. Not here. Not in the building. Write nothing down where the building can see it." She heard herself building him a careful instrument out of his own fear and she hated it and she did it anyway, because the alternative was leaving him standing alone at the bottom of a staircase under a window someone had lit. "And the one she carried by hand. The little gold pane. I need to know what code it was paired with before she changed it."

He nodded, slow. Then he frowned, the way a man frowns when a thing he has carried unexamined turns over in the dark and shows its other face. "There's something I never said," he told her, "because nobody ever asked me a question shaped right to make me say it. The strip wasn't only codes and symbols. There was a column on the end. Just one entry filled in, the rest blank. She wrote a name in it. A real name, block letters, like she wanted to be sure of it. The only real name on the whole strip." He looked at her, and the relief and the dread had burned down to just the dread now. "I didn't know who it was. It didn't mean anything to me. But you're standing here like the name's going to mean something to you, Detective, and I think — I think she'd have known it would."

The water moved against the pilings. Somewhere two streets over a bell on a buoy turned in the swell. Elena did not ask him the name. She already knew she would have to drive somewhere with no signal to hear it said aloud, the way she had driven north to hear a dead woman name a trade, because she was no longer sure there was a single room in this city where her sister's name could be spoken without the lantern hearing it too.

"Tell me where," she said, "and not what. Not here."

And Noah Okafor leaned close, the cold coming off the water between them, and told her where the only real name on the strip had been filed — and the where alone, before she ever heard the what, was enough to stop her breath.
