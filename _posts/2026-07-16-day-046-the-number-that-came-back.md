The sky had cleared sometime after midnight, the first clear sky in five days, and by the time Elena parked two blocks short of the old ferry terminal the cold had gone dry and absolute in a way the snow itself had never managed — a cold with nothing soft left in it, stars still faintly visible over the strait in the last gray minutes before true light, her breath standing in front of her face like something she'd have to walk through twice. The plowed road gave way to unplowed apron a hundred yards out, and she left the car and went the rest of the way on foot, boots punching through a crust that had frozen hard enough overnight to hold her weight for exactly one step before giving.

Adeyemi's car was already there, dark, angled into the shadow of the old freight shed where he could watch the water door without being watched from it. She didn't look at it as she passed. That was the agreement — she went in the way she'd gone in three weeks ago, alone, unremarkable, a woman walking toward a building the city had forgotten it owned.

Noah was waiting just inside the water door, in the same gray half-light, wearing a coat too thin for the morning and a backpack he hadn't taken off, the strap crossed over his chest like a man who might have to run. He looked like he hadn't slept, which she understood; she hadn't either.

"You're early," she said.

"I've been here since five." He said it apologetically, as if lateness and earliness were the same failure. "I didn't want to be the one who made you wait somewhere exposed."

"Nobody followed you."

"I took the long way. Two buses, not one." A short breath that wasn't quite a laugh. "My mother used to say the only trick to not being followed is deciding, ahead of time, which streets you're allowed to be interesting on. I picked boring ones."

The terminal smelled the way it always did — creosote and cold salt and the particular mildew of a building the harbor had given up on decades before the clinic ever existed — and the light coming through the broken clerestory windows overhead had just enough gray in it now to make out the far wall, the rusted ticket grille, the bench nobody had sat on in thirty years except, twice now, the two of them.

Noah unzipped the backpack with hands that weren't quite steady and took out a single sheet, laminated in plastic wrap gone cloudy at the folds, the way a man protects a thing he's carried through weather more than once. He didn't hand it to her right away.

"I need to tell you what it is before I give it to you," he said, "because once you have it, you're going to see the discrepancy before I finish the sentence, and I want you to hear the sentence."

"Go ahead."

"There are two concordance pages. The first one — the one I told you about on the bus, the one I typed three years ago, with the L-14 symbol sitting two lines above the gold-pane mark — that's the current one. The one still in use, near as I know, right up until she died." He turned the laminated sheet over once in his hands, not yet offering it. "This is the other one. Older. I typed it almost five years ago, back when I'd only worked for her a few months, and I forgot it existed until Voss." His jaw worked once. "I went through my own boxes after that. Everything I'd ever typed for her, anything I still had a carbon or a draft of, because I stopped trusting myself to remember what I'd already given away and what I hadn't."

He held it out. Elena took it by the edges, the way she'd learned to take everything now, and angled it to catch what light there was.

It was a column of symbols on the left — the same alphabet of small drawn marks she recognized from Noah's description of the current strip, a gate, a bell, a gold pane like a window with light behind it — each paired with a short alphanumeric code in Vivian's own idiosyncratic shorthand. Her eye went, the way Noah had known it would, straight to the gold-pane symbol two-thirds down the page.

On this page, five years old, the code beside the gold pane was not the same code Noah had described from the current strip. It was a different string entirely. And the code that had sat, on the newer page, two lines above the gold pane — the one identified as L-14, Daniel Voss's — appeared here, on the older page, four lines below it.

"The codes moved," she said.

"The codes moved." Noah's voice had the flat, careful calm of a man who has said a thing to himself so many times in the dark that saying it aloud finally costs nothing. "I thought at first I'd misremembered, that maybe I typed the new one wrong, or misspoke to you on the phone. So I checked it eleven times against what I actually remember typing for the current strip, not what you told me back, because I didn't want to just be agreeing with myself in a circle." He shook his head. "It's not a misprint. Between this page and the current one, somebody reordered the whole concordance. Not just added new names at the bottom, the way you'd expect if she just kept taking new patients. Moved. Existing codes, existing subjects, shuffled into new positions relative to each other."

"Vivian wouldn't have needed to reorder her own system."

"No." He said it immediately, with none of the hedging he usually gave his own conclusions. "She dictated to me for six years. She never once asked me to retype something I'd already typed unless the content changed — a new subject, a discharge, a death. She hated waste. She used to say paper was the one thing in that building she wasn't allowed to be careless with, because paper was how it eventually got you caught." He looked at the sheet in Elena's hands like it had cost him something to hold onto it this long. "She didn't reorder for tidiness. If the order changed, it changed because something about the underlying structure changed. And I never retyped a reordering. Which means somebody did it without going through me."

Elena thought of the gray door, of the key that lived, uncatalogued, on a chain she had watched Reyes touch without seeming to notice he was touching it. She thought of the sealed-records protocol he had cited three nights ago, requiring a second key holder present, a protocol that existed to keep exactly this kind of file exactly this hard to reach on exactly the night someone might have wanted to reach it. She did not say any of that to Noah. She had learned, this far into the case, that naming a suspicion out loud in a cold building at first light was its own kind of leak, even to an ally, even to the only person who had ever tried to hand her the truth on purpose.

"Do you know when the reordering happened?" she asked instead. "Even roughly."

"Not exactly. But the gold-pane subject's code on this old page—" he tapped it without quite touching it, "—doesn't appear anywhere on the current strip at all. Not moved. Gone. Replaced." He swallowed. "Either that subject graduated, in the word she used, and the code got freed up and reassigned to somebody new wearing the old number like a hand-me-down coat — or somebody wanted the original subject harder to find on the current page than they used to be, and buried them by giving their old slot to someone else."

The word graduated sat between them in the cold air, and neither of them reached for it further. Elena had not spoken that word herself since the day she'd first understood what it meant on a dead girl's file, and she did not intend to start now, in a building with no witnesses but Adeyemi three hundred feet away in a dark car.

"I don't know who the gold-pane subject is," Noah said, quieter now, as if apologizing for a debt he hadn't incurred. "I want to be clear about that, because I know how bad I'd feel if you walked out of here thinking I did and was still holding it back. I've typed that symbol probably forty times over six years and never once heard a name attached to it. She was careful about that one specifically. More careful than any other."

"I believe you."

"There's one more thing." He reached into the backpack again, and for a moment his hand stayed inside it, not quite committing. "I've started writing things down. My own version. Not what she dictated — what I remember. Every session I ever typed, everything I can still reconstruct before it goes soft in my head the way memory does. I started the week Voss died, because I realized I'd been treating my own memory like it was safe somewhere, and it isn't, nothing in my head is safer than a drawer, and drawers get opened." He didn't take whatever it was out. "It's not finished. It's not even close to finished. I'm not giving it to you yet, because it isn't ready and because I don't fully trust it isn't wrong in places. But I wanted you to know it exists, in case—" He stopped.

"In case what."

"In case I run out of time to finish it properly, and somebody has to decide whether an unfinished thing is still worth having." He said it lightly, the way people say the truest sentences of their lives, offhand, so the weight of it doesn't have to be acknowledged by either party. "It's at my aunt's. Not on me. I learned that much, at least, from Voss."

She wanted to tell him nothing was going to happen to him, the way she had on the phone, and remembered exactly how that sentence had landed the last time — a promise he had no use for and had told her, gently, to stop making. So instead she said, "Where at your aunt's," and he told her, precisely, the way a man gives an address to someone he has decided, finally, to trust with the shape of his own disappearance, and she wrote it nowhere, committed it instead to the same place she kept the terminal's name and Marguerite's diner and every other true thing too dangerous yet to spend on paper.

They didn't linger. He went out the landward door first, the long way, two buses' worth of caution still in his legs; she gave it four minutes and went out the water door, back toward the plowed road and Adeyemi's dark car, the laminated page zipped now into the inside pocket of her coat, against her chest, the coldest part of her.

It was Adeyemi who found the tracks, stepping out to meet her at the edge of the apron, his flashlight low and businesslike across the crusted snow. Not their two sets, hers and Noah's, printed clean and separate along the approach they'd each taken. A third line, older, already half-refrozen at the edges, coming in from the seaward side where neither of them had walked — from the water itself, or the rock apron below the old dock — and going back out the same way, hours before first light, before either of them had arrived.

"Somebody was here first," Adeyemi said, crouching, not touching the print, his breath fogging low over it like a held word. "Before you. Before Noah. Came in from a direction nobody uses, stood about here—" he swept the light in a small tight arc, "—long enough to lose the heat out of both boots, and left the same way they came, back toward the water, before the two of you ever showed up."

Elena looked out past the rock apron to the strait, black and flat and giving nothing back, and understood, with the particular cold that had nothing to do with the morning, that for the first time since this case began, the watching had stopped living entirely inside a phone.
