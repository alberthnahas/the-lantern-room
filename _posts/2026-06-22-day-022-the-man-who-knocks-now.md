She let it ring four times before she answered it, which was three times longer than she would have let any other caller wait, and she knew exactly why she had waited and hated the knowing.

The Crown Victoria was down out of the firs by then, into the long descending curve where the road gave the strait back to her in pieces between the trees, gray water flickering through black trunks. The frost had not surrendered here either. It lay in the road's north shoulder in a band that the low sun could not reach, and the cold came up off the water and met the cold coming off the windshield and the two of them sat in the car with her like a thing she was ferrying somewhere. On the seat the phone held both messages at once — the warm name, the three cold words beneath it — and she made herself look at the cold ones first. She said it twice. She turned the phone face down on the seat so that only the call remained, ringing, and she answered it because the alternative was to let him wonder why she hadn't, and a woman who did not want to be wondered about did not give people reasons to wonder.

"Elena," Reyes said, and her name in his mouth was the gentlest thing she had heard all morning, gentler than her sister's recorded voice, which had not been gentle at all but small and lost and asking. "I hope it's not too early. I find I'm calling people too early these days. I don't sleep the way I used to, and I forget that other people manage it."

"You're fine," she said. "I'm up. I'm driving."

"Driving." A pause, the exact length of a pause a kind man leaves when he is deciding not to ask where. "I won't keep you, then. I only wanted — Elena, I had a bad night about Vivian, and I find when I have a bad night about Vivian the only person I want to talk to is the one person in this whole business who seems to take her death as seriously as I do. Which is a strange thing to be grateful for. But I am."

She watched the road. She had learned, over twenty days, to hear two channels in everything he said: the words themselves, which were always warm and always plausible and always landed exactly where a hurt person needed to be touched, and the thing underneath, the steady managing pressure, the hand on the small of your back that you mistook for tenderness right up until you noticed it was steering. The trouble was that she could not prove the second channel existed. She had only ever felt it. And a detective who built a man's guilt out of a feeling was a detective the program had already won.

"I take it seriously," she said.

"I know you do." Then, lightly, the way you set a thing down so softly the other person doesn't hear it land: "Have you found anything I should know? You don't have to tell me. I only ask because I'd rather hear it from you than from Sandoval's people, who have started circling the clinic like they think we keep the answer in a drawer."

There it was. He arrived exactly when she had learned a thing. He had a way of asking that made not-answering feel like a small cruelty to a grieving friend, and she felt the pull of it, the genuine pull, because part of her did want to set the weight down in front of someone who had a right to it, who had loved the dead woman, who made the tea himself. She thought of Mara's voice flattening into a script. She thought of the boy on the rocks. She thought: I could tell him the words. I could say them and watch his face. A man who had never heard the catechism would frown and ask what they meant. A man who had written it would do something else, some smaller truer thing, and she would see it.

"I found out your motto is older than your building," she said.

The line was quiet. Outside the window the firs thinned and the first houses of the city's northern edge began, dark wet roofs, a dog standing in a yard watching the car go by.

"Our motto," Reyes said.

"On the door. *We are not what was done to us.* I came across an older version of it. Earlier. Said a different way."

"Did you." His voice had not changed. That was the thing she would turn over later, in the dark, the way she turned everything over: his voice had not changed at all, and a voice that does not change is either a voice with nothing to hide or a voice that has practiced this exact moment so many times it has worn the surprise out of it, and she could not tell which, she could never tell which, that was the whole architecture of the man. "Vivian used to say it the long way around, when I first knew her. *I am not what was done to me.* First person. She gave it to patients like a — like a handrail. Something to hold on the stairs. We shortened it for the plaque because the board thought the singular sounded like a confession." A small sound that might have been a laugh and might have been grief. "She hated the plural. She said *we* let everyone off. She wanted them to have to say *I*."

Elena's hands were very still on the wheel. He had handed it to her. He had handed her the catechism, its origin, its grammar, the whole shape of it, freely, warmly, as a memory of a woman he missed — and either he had done it because he had nothing to hide and the words were just words to him, an old colleague's gentle habit, or he had done it because he had calculated to the ounce how much truth a person will trust you with precisely because you seem unable to keep it from them. He was either the most innocent man in Hartwell Bay or the most finished one. The same evidence fit both. It always fit both. That was not an accident. She understood, descending into the gray city with his voice in her ear, that the case had been built so that every fact about Adam Reyes would fit both, so that she could never make the feeling into proof, so that she would either accuse a grieving friend on a hunch and be destroyed, or trust him and be steered. The trap had no third door.

"I'd like to see you," he said. "Not at the clinic. You hate the clinic, I can see it on you every time. Somewhere ordinary. Let me buy you a bad coffee and you can tell me to my face that I'm circling like Sandoval's people, and I'll deserve it."

"All right," she heard herself say.

They met at a diner on the cannery road, because she chose it, because it was three blocks from the dead-end pier where eight days ago a hooded figure had raised an empty palm at her hidden car, and she wanted to sit in the gravity of that place and watch his face for any flicker of knowing it. He gave none. He was already in the booth when she came in, his good coat folded beside him, two coffees ordered, and he half-rose the way men of his generation did and the gesture was so unguardedly courteous that she felt the familiar lurch of liking him and despised herself for it the way you despise a reflex.

Up close the tiredness in him looked real. The gray at his temples had the deliberate quality Whit had described from the other side of a locksmith's counter — *gray coming in on purpose-looking* — but the shadows under his eyes were not deliberate, or were the finest deliberate thing she had ever seen. He wrapped both hands around the coffee cup the way cold people do, and she watched his hands, because she watched everyone's hands now, because the case had taught her that the hands were where the truth lived when the face had been trained. His hands were a clinician's hands, unmarked, careful, hands that had never been used for anything but talking, and she thought of the splice in the tape shell, the hairline seam where someone with unhurried patience had opened the magnetic stock and cut a name out of her sister's mouth, and she thought: hands like these.

"You look like a woman who heard something she can't put down," he said.

"I hear a lot of things."

"Yes. I imagine you do." He turned his cup a quarter turn on the table, and the gesture went through her like cold water, because it was Gerald's gesture, and Clara's, she'd been told — the small turning of an object that careful damaged people do to give their hands a job. She had not known Reyes did it too. She filed it without letting her face file it. "Elena. I'm going to say something and I need you to let me finish before you decide what it is." He waited until she nodded. "I think Vivian was doing something at the end that frightened her. I think she was trying to undo a thing she'd spent her life building, and I think it killed her — not her own hand, whatever the file says. I've thought it since the morning I stood at that key hook. And I think you think it too, and I think you're carrying it alone because you don't know who in that building you can trust, and the terrible joke is that the answer is *no one*, including me, and I'm the one telling you that, which either means you can trust me or means I'm very good. I know how that sounds. I've thought about how it sounds. I'm saying it anyway because I'm tired of you being alone with it."

It was, she thought, the single most disarming thing anyone had said to her in twenty days, and it was disarming precisely because it disarmed — because it named the trap and then walked her gently past it, the way you walk a frightened animal past the thing it fears by acknowledging the thing exists. *You don't notice the door closing because he's holding it for you.* Noah had said that. A frightened twenty-six-year-old had described this man's whole method in a sentence, and here was the method, operating on her, in a diner that smelled of burnt coffee and bleach, and the worst of it was that she wanted to believe him, that the wanting was real, that her own grief leaned toward the warmth of him the way a cold hand leans toward a flame, and she understood with a sick lurch that her grief leaning was the entire mechanism, that they had not chosen her despite the wound but *for* it, and that the wound was, at this exact moment, working.

"Say it for me," she said.

"Say what?"

"The long way around. The handrail. The way Vivian gave it to patients." She kept her voice even, conversational, a woman asking a colleague to repeat a fond old quotation. "I want to hear it the way she said it."

He looked at her for a moment, and something moved behind his eyes that she could not name and would spend the night failing to name — not alarm, nothing so crude, but a kind of attention, as if a man playing a long game had felt the board shift one square. Then he gave her the gentlest smile and he said it, quietly, across the bleach-and-coffee table, in the cadence of a man reciting a dead friend's favorite line:

"*I am not what was done to me.*"

And then, before she could stop it, before she even knew it was a thing that could be stopped, he finished it — not the plaque, not the public half, but the whole of it, the second clause, the one that was not on any door, the one she had only ever heard in two dead voices on a spliced tape played alone above the strait where no living person could have heard her play it:

"*The water closed and the lantern stayed lit.*"

The diner went on around them, the cook's spatula, a chair scraping, the foghorn somewhere past the breakwater answering nothing. Reyes lifted his coffee and drank, his eyes still on her, kind and tired and waiting, a man who had just recited the second half of a sentence that was not written on any wall she could find, that lived only on a tape in a sandwich bag in a locked drawer in her apartment and in the mouths of two people he had no way of knowing she had heard — unless he had put the words in their mouths himself, and unless he had always known she would come here, to this booth, on this morning, and ask.
