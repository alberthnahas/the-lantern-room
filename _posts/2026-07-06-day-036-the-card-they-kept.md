Adeyemi killed the engine at the foot of the drive rather than pull all the way to the gate, the way he always parked when he wanted a second to think before a building could see him thinking, and for a moment neither of them said anything, watching the east-wing window burn gold above the black water while the wipers, stopped mid-arc, let the first fresh beading of ice gather on the glass between them and it. "We don't have a warrant," he said finally, not as an objection but as an inventory, the way he'd read her the struck-through line on his legal pad four hours ago. "We have a security guard's identification of a man from a six-year-old license photo, in the dark, at forty yards. That's not cause. That's a description."

"It's cause for a welfare check," Elena said. "Community caretaking. We're not searching for evidence of a crime, we're confirming a named, publicly announced, allegedly at-risk person isn't in danger inside a building he was seen entering. Any duty judge signs that in his sleep, if we had time to ask him, which we don't, because the building doesn't have to let us wait for one — it can let us in on its own authority right now, tonight, if somebody who runs it decides to." She was already reaching for the door. "So the only question is whether Reyes decides to."

He decided to before they reached the gate. The guard buzzed the pedestrian door before Adeyemi finished holding up his badge, and Reyes was there in the lobby's flat fluorescent light, still in the good dark coat from the presser, looking like a man who had come home to find his house on fire and was determined to be gracious about the water damage. "I heard on the radio ten minutes ago," he said, already turning to walk them in, not waiting to be asked. "I called ahead. Every ward on this floor knows to expect you, and I've had the overnight administrator paged so nothing waits on a signature. Whatever you need to see tonight, you'll see it tonight." He said it the way he said everything, as if generosity cost him nothing and he wanted them to notice that it didn't, and Elena thought, walking a half-step behind him down a corridor that smelled of floor wax and someone else's dinner, that she had stopped being able to tell whether that was the truest thing about him or the most rehearsed.

They searched the way you search a large building at night with a cooperative host and no legal teeth: fast, thorough, and entirely on his terms. The common rooms, the cafeteria with its chairs already up on the tables, the day wards where a night nurse walked them past sleeping patients with the practiced hush of someone who had done this before under worse circumstances, the service stairwells smelling of bleach and old rain. No Daniel Voss. A tech in the security office pulled the gate log without being asked twice and turned the monitor so Adeyemi could see it: a badge scan at the pedestrian service door, 6:52 p.m., accepted, green light, no alarm — the same forty-minute window the patrol had radioed in from the plaza. "That's not a visitor scan," the tech said, frowning at his own screen like it had contradicted him personally. "Visitor scans throw a yellow flag and page the desk. This came back green. Whoever came through that door had a working badge."

"Whose," Adeyemi said.

The tech typed, waited, typed again, and the frown deepened into something closer to unease. "It's not coming back to a current staff record. It's an old card — assigned, deactivated years ago, never physically collected when the assignment closed out. IT's supposed to purge the credentials on those but nobody ever — " he stopped, aware of Reyes standing two feet behind him, and finished more carefully. "It's an old patient access card. Ward-level only. Should have been shredded."

Elena felt the corridor go very quiet in the particular way it goes quiet when a fact lands somewhere it wasn't supposed to be able to reach. A patient card. Not staff. Not visitor. A man discharged six years ago, walking through a service gate on a credential that should not have opened for anyone, that should not have existed at all past the week it was issued, and the door had gone green for him the way a door goes green for someone it still recognizes. She did not say the word graduate. She had learned, across five weeks and four unknown numbers and a strip of paper she still had not been allowed to see, exactly how much a single unspoken word could cost her, and she filed this one where she filed all the others — not spoken, not written, held.

"What name is it assigned to," she asked, and watched the tech's mouth open, and watched — for the first time since she had sat across from him in a diner three blocks from a cash-drop pier — something move behind Reyes's face that he had not put there on purpose. It was there and gone in under a second, small enough that the tech never saw it and Adeyemi, half-turned toward the monitor, missed it entirely: not alarm, nothing so crude as alarm, but the particular stillness of a man doing very fast, very private arithmetic he had not expected to need tonight. Then it was gone, and he was leaning past the tech's shoulder with exactly the right amount of professional concern, and saying, in exactly the right voice, "That field's usually blank on the legacy cards — we switched systems eight years ago and half the old records never migrated cleanly. I can have Records pull the physical file in the morning." He said it smoothly. He said it fast. Elena had spent five weeks learning the difference between Reyes being helpful and Reyes closing a door gently enough that you thanked him for it, and this had the shape of the second thing wearing the coat of the first.

The field on the screen was, in fact, blank. Where a name should have sat there was a six-character string that meant nothing to the tech and everything to Elena, because she had seen its shape before, on a strip of paper she'd never been shown and a strip of paper she had been shown, on the back of an intake form she hadn't turned over for six years: a letter, then a hyphen, then a number, in the same bloodless clerical format the whole program used instead of names, as though whoever built it had understood from the beginning that names were the one thing you couldn't take back once you'd written them down where somebody might read them after you were dead. L-14. She did not know what it meant. She knew, with the particular cold certainty of a woman who had spent a month learning to recognize a machine by its parts, that it meant something, and that whatever it meant had walked itself, of its own free will, back into this building tonight using a door that should not have opened for a dead assignment, and that the one man standing close enough to read it over her shoulder had, for one half of one second, looked like he already knew.

"I want that pulled tonight," Adeyemi said, "not in the morning," and Reyes agreed instantly, warmly, said of course, said he'd have wondered why they hadn't asked — and then explained, with the same fluent regret he'd used on a stage two hours earlier, that the legacy patient files lived in a records annex behind the east wing that required a second key holder present for any file with a sealed designation, a privacy protocol from Dr. Mercer's era that the Institute's counsel still enforced to the letter even after her death, and that the second key holder was, unfortunately, unreachable at this hour. "I'm not protecting a piece of paper," he said, and Elena believed, watching him say it, that he believed it too, which was somehow worse than watching a man lie. "I'm protecting the Institute from exactly the kind of lawsuit that makes it impossible to help you with anything, tomorrow or the day after. Give me until morning. I will personally walk the file to your desk."

They had the badge log, timestamped and pulled under both their names the way Adeyemi had pulled the notebook pages, and they had a building searched floor to floor with nothing to show for it but an empty patient room whose bed, when Elena finally asked to see it, had been slept in once, briefly, the pillow still dented, and left. No Voss. No blood, no struggle, nothing a photograph could make into evidence — only a warm bed going cold in an empty ward, and a corridor camera outside its door that the overnight tech, checking almost as an afterthought while they stood in the doorway, found had dropped ten full minutes of footage sometime after seven, not from a fault in the system, which flagged faults, but from a gap the log recorded as a clean, deliberate stop and a clean, deliberate restart, the kind of gap a machine does not produce by accident and a man does not produce by mistake.

Ten minutes. Elena thought of a boardinghouse window across the water and a stack of dated notebooks with one page missing, of a thin, careful man who had spent two years writing down exactly this: dark, then something colder and unsteady moving room to room, then dark again, and no one who called the tip line ever calling him back. She had believed, walking into this building an hour ago, that she was looking for a man who might still be found. Standing in a doorway with a cooling bed on one side of her and Reyes's steady, sorrowful, endlessly reasonable face on the other, she understood that she was no longer early enough for that, and that whatever Daniel Voss had walked in here believing he was safer than the street, the building had already had its ten minutes with him, exactly the way it had once had its ten minutes with someone else, in a window she used to watch from the outside and now stood, for the first time, actually inside.
