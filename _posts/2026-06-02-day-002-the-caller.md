The phone was still ringing when Elena reached the bottom of the stairs, and then, as if it had only been waiting for her to arrive, it stopped.

She stood in the wide pale hall of the Mercer house and listened to the silence the way she had listened, upstairs, to the room. Rain combed the windows. Somewhere a uniform shifted his weight and his belt creaked. The house was the kind of expensive that made no sound on its own — thick stone, deep carpet, doors hung so true they sighed shut — and the ringing had carried through it like a struck bell precisely because the house had been built to swallow everything else. Eight rings, she had counted, coming down. Nine. She found the handset on a console table near the kitchen, a sleek cordless thing in its cradle, and crouched to read the little gray screen without touching it.

One missed call. 4:46 AM. And a name, not a number, because the dead woman's phone book had supplied one.

MERCER CLINIC.

Elena read it twice. The medical examiner put Vivian's death between ten and midnight. Whoever had called this house at 4:46 in the morning — call after call, the rings she had heard from two floors up — had been dialing from the clinic across the bay nearly five hours after Vivian Mercer stopped being able to answer anything. She took out her notebook and wrote the time and the words, and under them, *who calls a house at dawn and lets it ring nine times when the news is already out?* The news had been out by then. Patrol had been on scene since half past four. A condolence call did not ring nine times. A condolence call did not come at 4:46. People who wished to comfort the bereaved waited for a decent hour and then could barely make themselves dial at all.

This was not comfort. This was someone checking whether the house would answer.

"Detective." Pollard, in the kitchen doorway, lowering his voice the way people did around her now. "The husband. You said you wanted him before he lawyered up."

"I want him before he calms down," Elena said, and stood, and put the thought of the 4:46 call into the cold drawer in her mind where she kept the things she was not ready to be wrong about.

Gerald Mercer sat at the end of a marble island the size of a small boat, both hands around a mug of tea a kind officer had made him and which he had not drunk. He was a big man gone soft, fifty-eight and dressed in last night's clothes, a good coat thrown over a sweater with a fleck of dried blood at the cuff. The blood, she would confirm, was his own: a long shallow cut across the meat of his right palm where he had reached through the window he'd broken and caught the frame. He had wrapped it in a dish towel that had bled through and dried stiff. He looked up at her with eyes so red they seemed lashless, and the first thing he said was the thing Pollard had warned her he kept saying.

"I should have come up earlier. If I'd come up at one, at two —"

"Mr. Mercer, I'm Detective Vega. I'm sorry for your loss." She said it and watched him receive it, because how a person took those words told her something the words themselves never could. He flinched, which was ordinary. He looked at her hands and not her face, which was less ordinary, and which she filed. "I need to walk through last night with you. Take your time. Start wherever it starts."

It started, he said, with the text. He had not spoken to Vivian in three weeks — they had been living apart for the better part of a year, he in a rented place down on the waterfront, she up here in the house that had always been more hers than theirs. Then at midnight his phone had lit on the nightstand and it was her, and it was strange, all of it strange. He showed Elena the message himself, scrolling to it with a thumb that shook, and she read it on his screen exactly as it had been read to her upstairs by a young officer with an uncomfortable face.

*Tell Clara I tried to undo it. Tell her the lantern is lit.*

"I thought she was drunk," Gerald said. "Or — she'd started taking something to sleep, she told me that months ago, and I thought, she's taken too much of it and she's maudlin. She got like that. Grand. She'd say things that sounded like the last line of a film." His mouth worked. "I texted back. I called. Twice. It went to her voicemail, that voice, you know, that calm — I hung up. I told myself I'd call in the morning." He turned the mug a quarter-turn on the marble, very precisely, and Elena thought of the squared pens on the blotter upstairs, the books ranged by height, and wondered whether order was a thing you married or a thing you caught from someone. "I lay there. And it kept — it wouldn't sit right. The undo it. She doesn't say things like that. She undoes other people. So at half three I got up and drove."

"Why the window?" Elena asked. "You have a key to this house."

A small, terrible pause. "I rang. I knocked. I called the house line from the step and I could hear it ringing inside and no one — and the office light was on, I could see it under the door upstairs, and I just —" He lifted the wrapped hand an inch off the marble and let it fall. "I put the granite frog through the side window. Her mother's frog. Forty years on that step." He laughed, one wet syllable with no humor in it, and pressed the towel to his face. "She'd have hated that."

Elena let the silence do its work, then asked, lightly, as though it cost her nothing, "When you called the house line from the step — what time was that?"

He thought. "Quarter to four. Maybe a little before."

So: a call from Gerald's phone to the landline at roughly 3:45, which she could verify, the husband standing on his own doorstep listening to it ring. And then, an hour later, with the house full of police and Vivian long gone to the morgue, a second call to that same landline — from the clinic — ringing nine times into a house that everyone in Hartwell Bay already knew was a house of the dead. Two callers to a phone no one would ever answer. One of them was sitting in front of her with blood on his cuff. The other was a white tower across the water with a single gold window.

"Mr. Mercer," she said, "had you and your wife been discussing money?"

It was a small question and it changed his face entirely. The grief stayed but something slid in beneath it, quick and ashamed, the look of a man who has been waiting for a particular question and is relieved and frightened to finally hear it. "Everyone's going to tell you about the money," he said.

"I'd rather hear it from you."

"I'm not — I had some difficulty. A development down south, it went bad, I put more in than I should have." He turned the mug again. "I'd asked Vivian for help. She'd said no. Then a month ago she said yes — out of nowhere, like the text, like everything she did lately — she said she'd settle it. We were meant to sign papers next week." He looked up and for the first time met Elena's eyes directly, and she saw that he understood exactly how it sounded. "So no, Detective, I did not want her dead. Her death is the single worst thing that could have happened to me, in every way a thing can be worst, including the smallest and ugliest one. I needed her alive and generous for eight more days. Write that down. It's the truth and it makes me sick."

She did write it down, because it was the kind of motive that worked equally well as guilt or innocence, and a careful person did not yet choose. But she believed the sickness. You could fake grief — she had watched a man weep at his wife's funeral and learned eleven years later he'd drowned her in the bath — but the specific, self-loathing precision of *including the smallest and ugliest one* was hard to manufacture. It was the confession of a coward, and cowards, in her experience, rarely had the nerve to kill.

What money, though. And why now, the same *now* as the text, the same now as a woman who undid other people deciding, all at once, to undo something herself.

She left him with the tea he would not drink and went back up the stairs against the traffic of the gurney coming down, and stood again at the office door while Osei's technician knelt at the deadbolt with a light. The window was off the table — barred from outside by the planting box, the broken glass fallen inward and outward both in a pattern consistent with a frog thrown from the step, and twelve clean feet from the desk regardless. Which left the door. The door that had been bolted from the inside, in a room with one occupant, and that occupant dead.

"Talk to me about the lock," Elena said.

The technician, a patient young man named Briggs, sat back on his heels. "Standard thumb-turn deadbolt, keyed on the outside. Inside you just twist. It was twisted to engaged when the husband broke in — Officer Pollard confirms, I photographed it before anyone touched it." He tilted the light so the brass caught. "Here's the only odd thing, and I want to say up front it's probably nothing." Elena had noticed that everyone in this house kept telling her things were probably nothing. The probably-nothings were beginning to outnumber the certainties. "The deadbolt's stiff. Old mechanism, needs a real push to throw. And the thumb-turn's clean. Wiped, or never touched — no prints, not even partials, not even the doctor's. Everything else in this room has her prints on it. The pen. The water glass. The light switch. But the one thing she'd have had to turn with her own hand to lock herself in to die —" he shrugged, "— is the one thing she never touched."

Elena looked at the small brass turn, innocent and gleaming, and felt the cold find the bottom of the well again.

"Where are her keys?" she asked.

Briggs nodded toward an evidence bag on the floor. "Ring was in her cardigan pocket. House key, car, two clinic keys. I checked them against this lock." A beat. "One of the clinic keys is for a deadbolt of this exact make. Throws this lock fine. But it's not this door's key — wrong house. So the question your face is asking —" he was not unkind about it — "is yeah. How many keys open this room. And the answer is, her ring has the wrong ones, the right one's nowhere in this house yet, and the only door to a locked room was locked by a hand that left no print."

Outside, the rain had thinned to mist and the bay had gone the color of a knife. Through the hall window the clinic stood pale and certain on its bluff, and high in the east wing the single window still burned its steady electric gold, unbothered by the morning, lit the way it was lit every night of the year. Someone in that building had let a dead woman's phone ring nine times at a quarter to five. Someone in this house had wiped a lock that the dead woman's own keys did not fit.

Elena took out her own phone and dialed the clinic's main line — the number that had called at 4:46 — and stood listening to it ring across the water, watching the gold window for any flicker, any shadow, any sign that the building knew she was calling it back.

On the fourth ring, someone picked up. And said nothing. Just an open line, and behind it, faint and unmistakable, the long single note of the foghorn out past the cannery — which meant whoever was breathing into the silence was not in the clinic at all, but somewhere down by the water, close enough to the strait to be standing, as she was, in the same gray morning, listening back.
