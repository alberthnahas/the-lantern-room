The morgue at Hartwell Bay General had no back door, only a loading bay that pretended to be one, and Elena went in that way at a quarter to eight on a Sunday because Adeyemi had told her to come in the back and because she had spent enough of her life walking through front entrances that the habit of a side approach felt almost like rest.

The ramp was wet. Everything in this city was wet, except on mornings like this, when the cold came up off the harbor with a mineral edge — the first real cold of the season, the kind that meant the rain would soon start arriving with something harder folded inside it. November was tipping. She could feel the year leaning the way you feel a ferry change its mind about a current, a long slow pressure that was not yet motion but would become it.

Osei was waiting for her by the steel doors with two paper cups of coffee and the particular stillness of a man who had not gone home. He handed her one without asking how she took it, which was correct, because she did not take it any way at all; she held it for the warmth.

"Adeyemi said before it's filed," she said.

"Adeyemi says a lot of things." Osei pushed the door with his shoulder. "I said I'd show you what I found. The filing's not up to me, and it's not up to you, and we should both remember that when we're standing in there."

The room beyond was the room she had stood in too many times to count and had never once gotten used to, which she counted as a small mercy. The light was the flat shadowless white that made every face look like a question. Vivian Mercer lay under a sheet drawn to the collarbone, and even reduced to this, even with the magnetism switched off and the terrifying perception gone out of her like a current out of a wire, there was something composed about the woman that Elena could not stop reading. She had looked composed in the chair. She looked composed here. Elena had spent six years hating a signature and three days standing over the body that had made it, and she still could not find the seam where the monster met the woman.

"Talk to me about the overdose," she said.

Osei set his coffee down and did not pick up the file, which told her he had it by heart. "The overdose is real," he said. "That's the first thing. I want you to hear that before I say anything else, because I know which way you're already leaning and I'm not going to be the thing you lean on if it won't hold. She had a therapeutic-plus level of a benzodiazepine and a barbiturate in her — pentobarbital — in combination. Either one in that amount is a hard sleep. The two together is a stop. Tox is tox. Nobody faked the chemistry. She died of what the note says she died of."

"But."

"But," Osei agreed, and now he did pick up the file, not to read it but to have something to hold, the way she was holding the cup. "Two things. The first is small and I'd have signed right past it on another day. Lividity."

"She was upright."

"She was upright," he said. "Posture you flagged at the scene — the chair pushed back, the spine too straight. You read it as staged because it looked composed. The body agrees with you, for a more boring reason than composed." He used his own hand to show her, tilting it, letting the blood of the explanation pool toward his fingertips. "When the heart stops, blood settles to the lowest parts of the body and sets there, fixed, within a window. Where it sets tells you the position the body held. Hers set consistent with the chair — buttocks, backs of the thighs, the forearms. She died sitting, she stayed sitting." He turned his hand over. "Except for a band across the upper back and the backs of the shoulders. Faint. Early. Set, then interrupted. As if for the first little while she was lying flat on her back, and then somebody sat her up before the lividity could fully commit to the floor."

Elena did not move. Outside, very far off, the harbor cleared its throat — the foghorn, two long notes and the gap between them she had learned to wait through. "How long," she said. "The first little while."

"That's the part I can't give you to the minute, and you know I won't pretend I can. An hour. Maybe less, maybe a touch more. Long enough to start, not long enough to finish." He looked at her steadily. "Elena. She did not take a fatal dose, lie down on her own back somewhere, change her mind partway into dying, and walk to her chair. The chemistry doesn't allow it and neither does the time. Somebody moved her. After she was past moving herself and before she was cold."

"Into the chair."

"Into the chair. Sat her up. Squared her. Made her look like the photograph everyone wanted to take." He said it without heat, which was worse than heat. "The overdose is real. The pose is a lie. Both of those are true at once and I have spent two nights making sure I believed it before I'd say it to your face."

She made herself drink the coffee then, a swallow of it, because she needed the half-second the cup bought her and because if she stood there with her certainty visibly catching fire Osei would, correctly, stop telling her things. He was right that she was leaning. She had been leaning since the first morning, when the chair had stood eighteen inches too far from the desk for a woman who had reached for nothing. Now the body had leaned with her, and the trouble with being right was that it made you reckless about the next thing, and the next thing was always where the case died.

"You said two things," she said.

"I said two things." He hesitated, and the hesitation was a different texture than the first — the first had been a careful man laying down a careful fact. This was a man who had found something he could not make fit and disliked the feeling. He drew the sheet back from Vivian's left arm and turned it gently, exposing the inner forearm, and pointed to a place near the bend of the elbow where, under the white light, Elena could make out the faintest stipple of a mark. "Injection site."

"She took pills."

"She took pills. The tox is oral — gastric contents confirm it, she swallowed the dose. There's no injectable in her in any meaningful amount." He kept his finger near the mark without touching it. "This isn't the dose. This is something else. One puncture, clean, in good practice — somebody who's done it a thousand times and never once made a patient flinch. No bruise to speak of, which means it was done very well or done very near to death, when the body had stopped defending itself. There's nothing in the blood at that site I can name. Either it metabolized to nothing, or it was something I don't have a panel for, or" — and here he looked up at her — "it was nothing at all. An empty needle. A draw, not a push."

"A draw." The word went into her slowly. "You're saying someone took something out of her."

"I'm saying I have a hole and no story for it, and I'd be lying if I dressed it up as more than that. People draw blood from the dead and the dying for a hundred lawful reasons and half of them don't write it down. But nobody on my team did it, and it isn't on any chart, and it's at a site you'd choose if you wanted a vein and didn't want it seen under a sleeve." He drew the sheet back over the arm with a care that was almost tenderness, the way she had seen him cover dozens of strangers, as though the dead could still be embarrassed. "I can tell you it happened. I can't tell you what it was for. That's the honest end of it."

Elena let the two facts sit beside each other the way she had learned to let facts sit — not forcing them into a shape, just keeping them in the same room until they decided whether they could stand each other. Somebody had moved Vivian after she died, to make her look like a woman who had chosen the chair. And somebody — the same hand, or another — had put a needle in her arm and, perhaps, taken something away. A pose added. A something subtracted. A killer who had not simply walked out of a finished suicide but had stayed, in a dead woman's locked office, long enough to arrange her and long enough to draw on her, and who had then left the room as sealed as the day it was built.

"The room was bolted from the inside," she said, mostly to herself. "Thumb-turn wiped. The office key's gone. And now you're telling me whoever did this had time in there. Time to lift her, time to find a vein." She looked at the sealed line of Vivian's collarbone. "You don't do that fast. You don't do that scared. That's not a man who broke in and panicked. That's somebody who knew they had the room to themselves and knew they could close it behind them."

"That's your half," Osei said. "I'll keep to mine. The post says: overdose by her own swallowing; position not her own; one unexplained puncture. That's what I'll write, and I'll write it plainly, and the day I make it say more than I can prove is the day you should stop trusting me." He picked his coffee back up at last and found it cold and drank it anyway. "Sandoval's going to want this fast and tidy. A note, a dose, a closed door. What I've got is none of those things, and the minute it's filed it stops being yours and mine and becomes a thing other people get to interpret. So you've got it before they do. Do something honest with it."

She almost told him then — about the logbook hand, about the intake sheet in the cold drawer by her bed, about the photograph of her own window and the two words under it. She felt the words rise. She was so tired of carrying it alone that the weight of it had started to feel like a kind of company. But telling Osei meant making him carry the part of it that could end her, and he had just spent two nights making sure he only ever said what he could hold; she would not hand him something he'd have to drop.

So she said, "Thank you," and meant it more than the size of the word, and turned to go back out the way she'd come.

She was halfway up the wet ramp, the cold coming off the water and the foghorn marking its two slow notes behind her, when her phone buzzed against her hip — once, the short hard pulse of a text and not a call. She thought, before she looked, that it would be Adeyemi, asking what she'd seen. It was not Adeyemi.

It was the clinic exchange again, the same string of digits that had sent her window back to her in the dark, and this time there were no words at all. Only an image. She opened it standing on the ramp with the harbor going gray-gold behind the cloud, and for a moment she could not parse it — close, overexposed, a curve of something pale — until the angle resolved and she understood she was looking at the inner bend of an arm, a forearm turned to the light, and the small clean stipple of a puncture near the elbow, photographed in detail no one outside that sealed white room downstairs should have had.

She had walked in the back. She had not signed a log. And somebody already knew what Osei had only just told her — knew it well enough to send it back to her before the ink on his report was dry.
