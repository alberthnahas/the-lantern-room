She did not let her face move.

It was the hardest thing she had done in twenty days, harder than the morgue, harder than her sister's voice flattening into a learned line above the strait, and she did it the way she had taught herself to do hard things: by becoming a person watching herself from a little distance, a cold clerk filing what the warm animal felt. The warm animal wanted to come across the table. The clerk noted the temperature of the coffee, the angle of his cup, the foghorn past the breakwater answering nothing, and wrote down: he said the second clause. He said it unprompted. He is watching you to see what you do.

So she did nothing. She let the sentence sit in the air between them the way you let a wasp sit on the back of your hand — not because you are calm but because the alternative is worse. She lifted her own coffee. It was as bad as he had promised, scorched and thin, and she drank it as if it were the only thing in the diner worth her attention, and over the rim of the cup she gave him the small tired nod of a woman who had asked to hear a dead friend's favorite line and had heard it and been moved, and nothing else, nothing more.

"That's it," she said. "That's the one."

"That's the one," he agreed.

And the moment passed, or was allowed to pass, or was made to pass — she would never afterward be able to say which, and the not-knowing was the wound he kept reopening simply by existing. Because here was the trap with its teeth showing. She now knew, with a certainty she could feel in her back teeth, that Adam Reyes knew words he had no innocent way of knowing. The second clause was on no plaque. It was in no file she had been able to reach. It lived in two dead mouths on a spliced cassette in a sandwich bag in a stiff drawer by her bed, and on a side she had played exactly once, alone, in a turnout north of the city where the cell bars dropped to nothing and no living person had been within four miles of her. There were only two ways he could own that sentence. He had heard the archive — the tapes themselves, which meant he had access to the thing the whole city believed had died with Vivian. Or he had written it — had stood at the head of the long table where the script was issued and put the words into the first mouth himself. Access or authorship. Either one ended him. Either one was the case.

And she could do nothing with it. That was the part that made her want to put her head down on the bleach-smelling table and laugh. To use the second clause against him she would have to explain how she knew it was secret, and to explain that she would have to produce the tape, and to produce the tape she would have to explain that she had received stolen evidence from a hooded stranger at a dead-man's drop, played it off the books, and concealed it for three days from her partner, her lieutenant, and the entire chain of custody on case 11-0419 — while already flagged by Internal Affairs for a conflict she had hidden for six years. The instant she spent the word, she spent herself. He had handed her a knife with the blade welded to her own palm.

"You went somewhere," Reyes said gently.

"I'm tired," she said, which was true, and which was also the kind of truth she had learned to hold up in front of the other kind.

"We're all tired." He turned the cup the quarter turn again, and she watched the small revolution of white china on the laminate and thought of glass cooling, of a frog of granite swung once at a window, of careful damaged people giving their hands a job so the hands would not give them away. "Elena, I want to say one more thing and then I'll let you go back to whatever you can't tell me. You don't have to trust me. I'd think less of you if you did, on this evidence. But I'd ask you to do one thing for both our sakes." He leaned a little forward. "Whatever you've found — the thing that's making you sit here looking at me like I'm a lock you can't read — don't carry the only copy of it. Put a copy somewhere a friend would think to look if you went quiet. That's not me asking to see it. That's me asking you to outlive it."

It was, she would think later, in the dark, where she did all her thinking now, a perfect sentence. It was the sentence a man who loved her would say. It was also the sentence a man who wanted to know whether a copy existed, and how many, and where, would say — phrased so that any answer she gave, even a flinch, even a glance toward her coat where her phone was, would tell him something. She gave him nothing. She had gotten very good, this week, at giving men nothing while looking like a woman giving them comfort.

"I don't have anything that interesting," she said.

"No," he said, smiling, not believing her, letting her see that he did not believe her, which was its own move, the move of a man so sure of his footing that he could afford to be caught not believing you. "No, I don't suppose you do."

He paid. He overtipped, the way he did everything, generously and visibly and in a way that made the waitress's morning and made him, for one more person in Hartwell Bay, the kindest man they'd met that week. He helped her into nothing — she had no coat to be helped into, she'd left it in the car — and the courtesy hung there unused, and he laughed at himself for it, and she almost laughed too, and that almost was the most dangerous thing that happened in the whole encounter, more dangerous than the second clause, because the second clause she could file and the almost-laugh she could not.

On the cannery road the cold had not moved. The sky still had the hard white blankness of the inside of a shell, the strait beyond the gutted buildings flat and gray, and the foghorn went on at its interval as if marking time for someone who had stopped counting. She sat in the Crown Victoria and watched his good coat go up the block and around a corner toward wherever a man like that left a car, and she did not start the engine until he was gone, and then she did not start it for a while after that.

She made herself think it through the only way she trusted, which was on paper she would burn.

The feeling was not proof. She wrote that first, in her head, in block capitals, because it was the rule that kept her alive in this. Every fact about Adam Reyes fit two stories. His grief was the right size or it was the right size on purpose. He pre-managed the key the morning after, before any detective came — protective instinct or guilty housekeeping. He volunteered Daniel Voss as a suspect — a colleague helping, or a man pointing the dogs at a corpse. The initials A. R. on the second specimen draw at 6:14 the Saturday evening before Osei phrased his report — a referring physician's ordinary estate sample, aboveboard because logged, or a credentialed hand reaching into her dead woman's arm to take out whatever the program needed back. He turned his cup like Clara turned glass. He had a clinician's careful unmarked hands, the kind that could open a tape shell along its seam and cut a name out of a reel without a tremor. None of it was proof. All of it was the same shape. The case had been built — she was sure of this now in a way she could not have defended in any room with a window — so that the shape would always be exactly this: a man who fit, perfectly, the silhouette of the thing she was hunting, and who could never be made to hold still long enough to cast a shadow she could photograph.

But the second clause was not a feeling.

That was the change, and she held it carefully, the way you carry the one egg that didn't break. Everything else she had on Reyes lived in the country of impression — the temperature of grief, the timing of a phone call, the quarter-turn of a cup. The second clause lived in the country of fact. It was a discrete, falsifiable, physical thing: a string of words that existed in only a few places in the world, and he had produced it, and the places it existed were a sealed tape and, by inference, the archive and the head of the table. She could not use it. But for the first time she had something about him that was true regardless of how she felt, something that would still be true if she liked him, still be true if she were wrong about everything else, still be true if her grief were leaning the whole time. And a thing that is true regardless of feeling can, eventually, be followed to another true thing. You did not convict a man on a sentence he should not know. But a sentence he should not know told you where to dig: not at the man, who fit everything, but at the words, which had to have come from somewhere. The catechism had a first mouth. Before it was a voice it was a page. Find the page and you found the hand that wrote it, and a hand on a page was the kind of thing a court could hold, in a way it could never hold the look that had moved behind his eyes when she asked him to recite.

She took out her phone. She did not call anyone. She opened the photograph she had taken three days ago, above the strait, of the bare side of the cassette where the obscured hand had written for the audience and, below it, now turn it over — and beside those, smaller, in the corner of the label, a thing she had registered and not let herself dwell on because she had been busy drowning in her sister's voice: a single faint mark she had taken for a smudge. She enlarged it now with two fingers. It was not a smudge. It was a glyph, drawn small and deliberate, four little panes around a center, like a window divided into lights. Like the gold pane Noah had described, the mark Vivian had given the one code she said she would carry by hand. It sat in the corner of the label on her sister's tape like a maker's stamp, like a signature too disciplined to read, and Elena understood that whoever had spliced the name out of Mara's mouth and taped the result under a rotting pier for a detective to find on her knees had also, quietly, in a corner, signed the work — not with a name, never with a name, but with the program's own most private mark, the little lit window, put there for an audience that was meant to learn, eventually, exactly whose handiwork she was holding.

Find the page, she thought. Find the room.

The foghorn went again. Across the gray water, though it was full morning and no window should have shown a light, she could not see the east wing from here and was glad of it, because she did not trust herself, this morning, not to drive toward a lit window like a moth that had decided the flame was a message addressed to her by name. Instead she started the car. She would go to Noah. Noah had built the strip; Noah remembered the symbols; Noah might know whether the little four-paned window had ever stood beside any name but the one the architect had cut away. It was the right move and she knew it was the right move and she also knew, turning the Crown Victoria onto the cannery road with the heater laboring against a cold that had stopped pretending to be weather, that every time she had decided on the right move this week, the right move had turned out to be the one a careful hand had left lit for her to find.

Her phone buzzed once against the seat.

She looked. Not Reyes's name. Not the clinic exchange. Not her sister's resold number. A fourth string of digits she had never seen, local prefix, and beneath it three words, lowercase, patient as a man drinking coffee:

he asked you to make a copy.
