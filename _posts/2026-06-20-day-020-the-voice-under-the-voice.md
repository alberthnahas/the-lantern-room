Gerald brought the name on paper, the way she had told him to, folded twice and pressed flat the way a man folds a thing he is afraid of and wants smaller.

They met at the far end of the ferry parking apron at first light, a Sunday gone the color of a coin, the frost holding now even in the open where the sun should have burned it off, the strait beyond the breakwater flat and gray and breathing its single long note. She had chosen the apron because it was a lot, not a room — no walls, no window, nothing for the watcher's reach to climb into. She had learned that lesson on the strait road two days ago with a tape and a cold engine: the only place that could not be listened into was a place that was not anywhere. Gerald got out of his car in yesterday's cardigan, having plainly not slept, and crossed the asphalt to her with the paper held against his chest like a man carrying something liquid.

"I didn't say it to anyone," he said. "I didn't say it out loud at all. I wrote it in my kitchen with the radio off." He stopped. "I keep thinking that's insane. Writing a name with the radio off."

"It isn't insane," she said.

He gave her the paper and then could not look at it being read, and turned to face the water instead, his hands going into his armpits against the cold. Elena unfolded it. Gerald's handwriting, the ordinary grieving legible hand of a man with nothing to hide. One name, block-printed because he had wanted to be sure she could read it, sitting alone in the white.

She had braced for it to be Mara's. She had spent the drive over not letting herself brace for it, which was its own kind of bracing, and she had been wrong anyway, and the wrongness went through her colder than the name would have.

It was not Mara.

It was a man's name. A name she did know — Gerald had been right about that part for both of them — a name she had read three days ago in a fifteen-year-old drowning file at the Records Annex, on the back of a sheet in a printed admin box, beside a code she had photographed and made herself not underline twice.

The conservatorship Vivian Mercer had taken out eleven years ago, the legal architecture of one human being's entire will, was over the boy who had stood on the rocks in a dark coat and watched Sophie Lund drown. The witness she had stepped between and the police three days after the tide gave the girl back. The one Elena had heard on a microcassette above the strait, reciting a worn-smooth catechism in a taught flat voice. L-9. The first instrument. Vivian had not only reshaped him; she had gone to a court and made herself the legal owner of his choices, and then had the court forget she'd done it.

"You know him," Gerald said. Not a question. He had read her face before she'd schooled it.

"I know what he is to the case," she said carefully, which was the truest thing she could afford. "Gerald. You did the brave thing. Now do one more. Forget you read this. Don't go back to the court, don't tell me you remembered anything else, don't be interesting to anyone. You lit a match in a dark room and now I need you to be a man who was never in the room."

He nodded, slowly. The foghorn went. "There was something else on the screen," he said. "I wasn't supposed to see it, the clerk scrolled past it. The conservatorship had an annex. A medical directive. It named who could speak for him if he couldn't speak for himself." He swallowed. "It wasn't Vivian. Vivian filed it, but the person named to speak for him wasn't Vivian."

"Did you see the name?"

"It went by too fast. But it was a doctor. There was an M.D. after it." He looked at her, and for once his cowardice and his grief got out of the way of something simpler, which was fear. "Whoever that is — they can still speak for him. She's dead and they can still speak for him. Is that — Elena, is that the worst thing? It feels like the worst thing."

It was very close to the worst thing, and she did not tell him so. She told him to go home, to keep the radio off, to call her only if a kind person became interested in him. He went. She stood on the apron until his car had merged onto the bluff road and then she got into the Crown Victoria and sat with both hands on the wheel and did not start it, because she had one more thing to do that could not be done in any room, and she had been putting it off the way you put off the last stair in the dark.

She had the L-9 tape in a sandwich bag in the glovebox. She had played it once already, above the strait, and heard the boy on the rocks become a man who could live with anything. But she had not played the other side.

She had not let herself think there was an other side until last night, turning the case over in the precinct lot while the gold window watched her. Lind shipped sixty tapes to a case; Vivian never reused a tape, never recorded over the old word — reuse is a kind of lying, whatever you put on top, the old word is still under it. But a microcassette had two sides. And the L-9 tape that had been planted for her under the pier, taped to the underside of the decking where a collector would never reach but a detective on her knees might, had been flipped to side A when she found it, the way you leave a thing turned to the face you want read first. Someone had chosen which side she heard.

Which meant someone had chosen what she would hear if she turned it over.

She did not want to turn it over. That was exactly why she was going to. A detective who only played the side she was handed was no detective at all; she had said something like that to herself on the strait road and then proved she half believed it by waiting three days to flip a tape.

She drove out the bluff road past the ferry and kept going, north, the way she'd gone two days before, until the houses thinned and the cell bars on her phone dropped from four to two to none, until there was nothing but the gray water the color of a knife and the gulls hung motionless on the wind and the turnout she had used before, a cold gravel shoulder above a drop to the rocks. She killed the engine. She took the pawnshop transcriber out from under the seat, the cash-bought one with the foam earpiece, the one with no chain of custody and no hands on it but hers. She slid the L-9 tape out of the bag, turned it over, and read the side she had not read.

There was no label on side B. The adhesive strip — Noah's machine, the typed L-9 — was on side A only. Side B was bare plastic, and someone had written on the bare plastic in ballpoint, small, the letters cramped and over-built and then taught to vanish, the same hand she had now seen across two years and four documents and a logbook, the hand Whit had watched pocket three keys, the hand she would not name even alone in a cold car at the end of the road. Two words, in that disappearing hand.

for the audience.

She sat with that a moment. For the audience. Not for the file, not for the world, not on behalf of. For the audience, and she was the audience; the gold window had been lit for her, the case curated for her, her dead sister's name printed in a column no one reads and left for her alone to find, and now a tape flipped to the side they wanted read first with the other side marked for the one person it would unmake. She understood that pressing play was doing what she was built to do, the way the boy on the rocks had recited the line he was built to recite. She understood it and she pressed play anyway, because the alternative was to hand them a detective who flinched, and she had stopped being willing to be handed anything.

Tape hiss. The particular grain of it, the sound of a room being kept. Then a chair. Then a voice, and the voice was not the boy's, was not flat, was not taught.

The voice was her sister's.

She knew it before it formed a word — knew it in her spine, in the place that had been dialing a recycled number twice a year for six years to prove that nothing is held, that no voice survives, that the world keeps no copy of the people it loses. She had built her grief on that proof. She had no recording of Mara. She had a photocopy and a block-capital intake sheet and an assessment in Vivian's hand and a stranger in Bremerton who answered the phone confused, and out of those she had made a sister she could survive. There was no tape of Mara. There had never been a tape of Mara. It was the one mercy the case had left her, the floor under everything, and the floor was not there.

"—don't know why I keep coming," Mara said. Twenty-nine forever. The little upward turn at the end of a sentence she didn't mean as a question, the thing Elena had spent six years failing to remember the exact shape of and now would never again be able to forget. "You make it sound like I chose this. Like there was a door and I walked through it. I don't remember a door, Dr. Mercer. I just remember already being inside."

A pause. The hiss. And then a second voice, low, warm, unhurried, terribly kind, the voice of a woman who had spent her life teaching people to forgive themselves.

"There's always a door, Mara," Vivian Mercer said. "Someone walked you to it. Do you remember who?"

And Mara — Elena's Mara, six years dead, water gone cold around her in a rented room — said a name.

The tape did not let Elena hear it. At the exact instant Mara's mouth made the shape of the name, the recording dropped — not stopped, not clicked off, but dropped, a clean engineered silence punched into the reel where a name had been, a quarter-second of nothing so smooth it could only have been done on purpose, with equipment, by someone who knew precisely which quarter-second to take. Then Mara's voice came back mid-breath, on the far side of the hole, saying, "—and I trusted him. That's the part I can't— I trusted him, and now I can't tell anymore which of the things I feel are mine."

Elena stopped the tape. Her hand was very steady, which frightened her more than shaking would have.

They had given her Mara's voice. After six years of nothing, after the recycled number and the cold drawer and the survivable sister she had built out of paper, they had reached into the one place she had decided was empty and put her sister's living voice into her ear, the upward lilt, the I trusted him, the room being kept — and in the same gesture, in the same engineered quarter-second, they had taken the only thing on the whole tape that mattered. Mara had named the man who walked her to the door. And the audience was meant to hear her sister say it and hear the name removed in the same instant, so that the grief and the absence arrived together, fused, inseparable, the lever and the fog. So that Elena would spend whatever she had left chasing a hole in a tape.

She looked at the bare plastic, the cramped vanishing words. for the audience. She thought about the boy on the rocks who had been made able to live with anything, and about a court eleven years ago handing one human being's entire will to a doctor with an M.D. after their name who could speak for him still, today, with Vivian in the ground. She thought: they did this to Mara. She let herself think it all the way through this time, did not refuse it as the lever, because refusing it had become its own obedience. They did this to Mara. They are doing it to me. The method is the same. They are walking me to a door and letting me believe I don't remember choosing it.

Her phone had no bars. She was off the grid, in no room, where they could not hear. And on the bare plastic of the tape, below for the audience, in the same hand, so small she had not seen it until the gulls shifted and the gray light moved, were three more words she had missed.

now turn it over.

She had played side A first because she was handed it. She had played side B because she chose to. And the tape was telling her, in the killer's own vanished hand, that she had gotten the order wrong — that there was a first side and a second side and she had walked through both doors exactly as built, and the thing she had just heard, the thing that had taken the floor out from under six years, was not the bottom.

It was the side they wanted read last.
