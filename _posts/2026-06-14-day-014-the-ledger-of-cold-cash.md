She did not go in.

Standing on the receiving dock with the gold pane laid over the water and Noah Okafor's upturned face beside her, Elena had felt the whole architecture of the night arrange itself around a single open door, and she had wanted — the way you want a stair you should not take — to climb the back way to the second landing and put her hand on a brass lock the color of fog. But want was a thing this case had been farming in her for thirteen days, planting it and watering it and waiting for the harvest, and she had learned to be suspicious of any door someone else had lit for her. She drove Noah to his bus stop instead, because a man who said the dead woman's name at full volume should not stand alone under a window that had just decided to shine. She watched the bus take him north. Then she went home and did not sleep, which by now was less a failure than a routine.

In the morning she did the unglamorous thing. She filed paper.

Adeyemi found her at her desk before eight, two coffees in a cardboard tray, his tie already loosened on a Sunday he was not supposed to be in. He set one cup down by her keyboard like a man laying a stone on a grave.

"You look like you slept in the river," he said.

"I'm requesting a financial production order on Vivian Mercer's accounts," Elena said. "Joint and personal. Going back two years."

Adeyemi sat on the edge of the next desk and did the thing he did, which was to make himself heavy and slow on purpose, an anchor she could pull against. "Gerald gave us the affair and the debt and the unsigned settlement. The dept already likes him for motive. Why are you going into her money instead of his?"

"Because Gerald watched her accounts for a year and never asked the one question a man asks when he sees money leaving." She turned her screen so he could see the request form, the fields half filled. "She was pulling cash. Eight thousand. Five thousand. Monthly, sometimes twice in a month, for the last year, year and a half. Out of joint accounts where she knew he could see it. She wasn't hiding it from him, Ruben. She was letting him watch her do it and trusting him not to ask. That's not an affair and it's not a debt. That's a woman paying for something on a schedule."

"Blackmail," Adeyemi said, trying it out the way you test a floorboard.

"Maybe she's the one being paid off. Maybe she's the one paying. Either way it's regular, and regular means a relationship, and a relationship has a name and a face on the other end of it." She signed the form. "I want the withdrawals. Dates, branches, amounts. I want to know if a human being stood at a counter for that cash or if a machine spat it into the dark."

He read her a moment longer than the form needed. Adeyemi had been her partner long enough to know the difference between a detective following money and a detective following a wound, and he had spent the last two weeks watching her do the second while calling it the first. He did not say so. He picked up his coffee.

"Sandoval's going to see this request," he said. "Anything with Mercer on it crosses her desk now. You went into a confessional with her last week and gave up your sister. You sure you want her watching you dig into the dead woman's bank account three days later?"

"If I stop digging because IA is watching, I'm exactly the detective they built me to be." She slid the form into the outbox. "Let her watch. Watching me work is the only thing she'll ever catch me doing."

It took the order until Tuesday to bear fruit, and the fruit was strange. The bank produced a clean printout, twenty-two months of it, and the withdrawals were exactly as Gerald had described — but they were not the smooth, sinister metronome Elena had built in her head. They were cash counter withdrawals, every one, from a single branch: First Strait Maritime on Harbor Street, the small old building near the cannery, the one with the ship's-wheel clock and the teller windows with the brass bars. Vivian Mercer, who distrusted keyboards and would not let an assistant make her phone calls, had driven down to the water twice a month for nearly two years and stood at a counter and asked a human being for cash in her own voice.

And on the printout, beside roughly half the withdrawals, the bank's own internal note field carried a code. Not a transaction code. A handwritten initial the teller had keyed in to mark which staff member had served the customer and witnessed the cash count. Elena had asked for those notes specifically, on a hunch she could not have defended, and the hunch had paid: the same two or three teller initials, over and over, twenty-two months deep. Somebody at that counter had watched Vivian Mercer carry away two hundred thousand dollars in cash, a few thousand at a time, and had never once been asked about it by anyone.

She drove to Harbor Street in the afternoon, into a cold that had stopped pretending to be autumn. The snow that had reached the city Saturday was gone from the streets but had settled into the seams of things — the gutters, the north sides of doorways, the brass letters of the bank's old sign where they sat in shadow all day. First Strait Maritime smelled of radiator dust and floor wax and the particular hush of a building that handled other people's fear for a living. She showed her badge and asked for whoever had served the M. account holder most often, and a manager with a careful face brought her, after a phone call to legal and a long minute of his own conscience, a teller named Dolores Pham.

Dolores was fifty, maybe, with reading glasses on a beaded chain and the steadiest hands Elena had seen on this case, which on this case was saying something. She remembered Vivian. Of course she remembered Vivian. You did not forget a woman like that crossing your floor twice a month for two years.

"She always came to my window," Dolores said. They sat in the manager's office with the door open, because Dolores would not have it shut. "If there was a line she'd wait for me even when the next teller was free. People do that — they find a face and they keep it. I thought it was loyalty. Then I thought it was something else." She folded her hands. "She didn't want a new person learning her business twice a month."

"What was her business?"

"Cash. Always cash. Never the same amount, but always cash, always counted twice, always taken away in a plain envelope she brought herself." Dolores looked at the door, at the floor, anywhere but at Elena, in the way of a witness deciding how much of a confidence to keep for a dead woman. "I'll tell you what I told my manager when he asked me an hour ago, which is more than I'd have told you yesterday, because she's gone and the gone don't get embarrassed. She talked to me. Most people at a cash window don't talk; they watch the bills like the count is a magic trick and you might palm one. She talked. Little things. Weather. My grandson. And once — only once — when I asked her, just to be friendly, what kept her so busy, she looked at me over the brass and she said, 'I'm paying a very old debt, Mrs. Pham. The kind that isn't on any books.'"

Elena kept her face level. The kind that isn't on any books. She thought of the L-codes in the margins of the notebooks, letter and number, no name, no time. A ledger only its keeper could read. She thought of a thirty-year archive of voices labeled like specimens, and a number that had stopped appearing — L-9 graduated — and a woman driving to the water twice a month with cash for a debt that lived in no record on earth except the one inside her own head.

"Did she ever say to whom?" Elena asked.

"Never. But she didn't carry that cash home." Dolores took off her reading glasses and let them hang. "I'd watch her out the window — you can't help it, you spend the count looking at someone's hands, you start to wonder about the rest of them. She'd come out, and she wouldn't go to the lot where the doctors park. She'd walk." Dolores nodded toward the front of the bank, toward Harbor Street and the gray seam of the water beyond it. "Down toward the cannery. On foot. A woman in a coat that cost more than my car, walking into the worst part of the waterfront twice a month with an envelope of cash, and coming back twenty minutes later with empty hands and a face like she'd been to church." She put the glasses back on. "I am sixty-one years old, Detective, and I have handed money across a counter for forty of them, and I have learned exactly one thing about it. People who are being robbed look frightened. People who are paying for something they did look at peace. She looked at peace. Every single time. It used to bother me. Now that I know how she died, it bothers me more."

Elena drove back along the cannery road as the light went, the same road that dead-ended at Hartwell Lock & Safe where Whit had asked her where the third key was. She drove it slowly. Somewhere along this water, twice a month for two years, Vivian Mercer had walked an envelope of cash into the dark and walked back lighter, and called it an old debt that was on no books, and looked at peace. The bible of this case — if it had one, if anyone but the dead woman had ever written it down — said that peace was the most expensive thing a guilty person ever bought, and that they always, always paid for it in installments.

She had the dates now. Twenty-two months of them. She could put the next one on a calendar — the rhythm was a rhythm, twice a month, near the first and near the fifteenth — and she could stand on this road on that day with a long lens and a cold flask and find out who came down to the cannery to receive a dead woman's payment, because somebody was still expecting it. The order to resume the tapes had come two days after the death. The light had come back on. The routine had not noticed she died; it just kept arriving. Why should the debt be any different?

She was halfway through the thought — a stakeout, the fifteenth, four days off — when her phone lit on the passenger seat and broke four days of silence.

It was not from the clinic exchange this time. It was from a number she had not dialed in eleven months, that she dialed twice a year from stairwells, that a stranger in Bremerton now owned. Mara's old number. And the screen did not say a name, because the carrier had recycled it twice and the contact was long deleted, but Elena knew the digits the way you know a scar, and the message under them was three words, and they were not in any stranger's voice.

it isn't paid
