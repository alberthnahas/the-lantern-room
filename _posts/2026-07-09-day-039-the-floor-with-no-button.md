Adeyemi drove with both hands high on the wheel, the way he only did when he'd stopped trusting the road, and the road had earned it — the snow that had been merely serious an hour ago had gone to something closer to a wall, headlights swallowed six feet out, the wipers losing the fight in long labored strokes that never quite cleared before the next white sheet came down. Elena had the radio low, dispatch repeating the same three facts because there were no others to give: a disconnected call from the Mercer Institute's east-wing nursing station, patient welfare, placed at 11:47, no answer on the callback line since. Eleven minutes had become twenty by the time they cleared the harbor road. Twenty had become thirty by the gate.

The guard at the service entrance waved them through without asking for badges, which told Elena more about the state of the building than anything dispatch had said. Inside, the lobby's usual hush had a different quality — not the well-mannered quiet of a place that expected nothing, but the held-breath quiet of a place where something had recently happened and everyone still on shift knew it and no one had yet been told they were allowed to say so out loud.

Adeyemi shook snow off his shoulders in the doorway and did not, for once, ask her to wait while he checked in with the desk first. That alone told her something about where his own patience had settled these past two days — since the ice-glazed drive home, since Danvers, since the deadline he'd given her that she'd satisfied on its letter and not its spirit. "We don't stand in a hallway on this one," he said, more to himself than to her, and went first through the inner doors.

The east-wing nursing station sat at the end of a corridor Elena had walked twice now in the dark, once behind Reyes's cooperative tour two nights ago, and it looked, at first glance, like nothing at all — a desk, a bank of monitors, a rolling cart of supplies pushed at an angle that suggested someone had left it in a hurry rather than parked it. Then she saw the phone. The handset lay off its cradle on the desk blotter, the cord stretched to its limit toward the chair, which had been shoved back hard enough to leave two long skid marks in the linoleum. A paper coffee cup had tipped and dried in a brown fan across a clipboard nobody had moved.

"Nobody's touched this," Adeyemi said, not quite a question, and a security officer hovering at the corridor's mouth shook his head fast enough that Elena believed him.

They found the nurse in a supply closet four doors down, sitting on an overturned bucket with her knees drawn up, a name badge that read R. KESSLER, LPN turned backward on its clip as if she'd grabbed for it and missed. She was young — younger than the voice on the recording had sounded, Elena thought, though she hadn't heard the recording, only the transcript of it dispatch had read back twice. Kessler's hands were doing something small and repetitive against her own knee, and it took her three tries to get the first sentence out whole.

"I called it in and then I hung up because I thought — I thought if I stayed on the line he'd hear me on the line," she said. "That doesn't make sense. I know that doesn't make sense."

"It makes sense," Elena said, crouching to her level, keeping her own voice low and even, the register she'd learned over five weeks worked better than reassurance. "Tell me what you saw before you called."

Kessler had been doing a bed check, she said, room by room, the kind of round that happened four times a shift and meant nothing until it meant everything. She'd come around the corner by the service elevator and found a gurney in the corridor where no gurney should have been — sheeted, strapped, being walked rather than wheeled fast, by a man in scrubs she said she'd never seen before, not once in eight months on this wing, and she knew everyone on this wing, she said, she'd have staked her license on it.

"Did you see who was on it?" Elena asked.

"A shape," Kessler said. "Under the sheet. I saw a shape and one hand that had come loose from the strap, hanging down, and I thought — I thought that's not how you strap somebody who's cooperating." She looked at Elena directly for the first time. "You don't leave a hand loose like that unless the person underneath stopped being the kind of problem a strap needs to solve."

"Did the man in scrubs say anything to you?"

"He said it was a protocol transfer. He said it like he'd said it before, easy, not fast, and he had a badge clipped at his hip that he didn't need to show me because I wasn't asking — I was already moving out of the way, because that's what you do, you get out of the way of a gurney — and it was only after he'd gone past that I thought: I didn't recognize the badge photo. I didn't recognize the badge photo and I still moved."

Adeyemi asked which direction the gurney had gone, and Kessler pointed past him, down the corridor, toward the service elevator at its end — the same elevator, Elena registered with a small cold click, that fed the loading dock and, two floors below that on the building's own posted directory, nothing at all.

Reyes arrived while Adeyemi was still writing Kessler's account into his notebook, moving fast for a man who never moved fast, coat still on, a fine rime of snow melting at his shoulders that told Elena he had come straight from wherever he'd been rather than from an office three doors down. He had been at a board budget session in the west building, he said, before either of them asked; someone had run to get him the moment security flagged a welfare call from a wing he personally oversaw. His face did all the correct things — concern arriving in the right order, no hesitation before asking whether Kessler needed a doctor, an immediate offer to pull every camera on the floor himself so nothing sat in a queue behind IT's usual backlog. It was, Elena thought, watching him do it, exactly what a good man would do, performed with exactly the fluency of a man who had done it before, and she had learned by now not to let the second half of that thought change the first.

"The elevator," she said, before he could finish offering. "Where does it stop."

Reyes's expression didn't change, which she noted and set aside, the way she'd learned to set aside everything about him that could be read two ways at once. "Loading dock, this floor, the sub-level utility corridor," he said. "Mechanical spaces. Nothing clinical."

"Show me the panel."

The panel, when they reached it, had four buttons and a keyed slot beneath the lowest one, unlit, the kind of override that needed a physical turn rather than a card. Three of the four floor lights were dark. The fourth — the sub-level, unmarked on the building's public directory, the one Reyes had just called mechanical — was lit steady amber, which meant, the maintenance supervisor confirmed when Adeyemi pulled him in off the floor, that the car itself was down there right now and had been for some time, because the light for a car in transit blinked and this one didn't.

"Bring it up," Adeyemi said.

"It won't come without the key," the supervisor said, and looked, apologetically, at Reyes.

Elena watched Reyes reach into his coat — not the left breast pocket, she noted, the muscle in her jaw tightening at the discipline it took not to stare at that pocket instead, the one Marguerite had watched ride there for three weeks like a stone he couldn't put down — and produce a separate, ordinary-looking brass key from an inside pocket she'd never had reason to watch before. He turned it without a word. Somewhere below them a motor woke.

Adeyemi, waiting, told her under his breath that he wasn't doing this the Danvers way — not standing in a hallway while paperwork or protocol or somebody's careful hand decided how fast they were allowed to move. "If that door opens on something," he said, "we're already moving before anybody has time to arrange it for us." She understood he meant it as a promise to himself as much as to her, the old eleven-day wait he'd told her about on the ice-glazed road two nights ago, worn smooth now into a rule he applied to every closing door.

The car took ninety seconds to climb four floors it had no business needing that long for, the number over the doors counting up through darkness, B to 1 to 2 to 3, and Elena found herself counting her own pulse against it and losing the count twice.

The doors opened on an empty car.

Not entirely empty — the gurney was there, folded flat, straps hanging loose and undone on both sides rather than cut, an IV pole collapsed into its stand still swinging very slightly on its own bracket as though something on the end of the line had been in motion until moments ago. No blood, no obvious violence, nothing the eye could immediately call proof of harm. Only, caught in the fold-hinge at the gurney's foot where a wheel had pinched it, a single torn strip of plastic hospital wristband, the printed strip on it too short to read a name because there had never been a name printed on it at all — only, in small block characters, a code.

L-14.

No one spoke while Elena bent and photographed it without touching it, the flash throwing the elevator's steel walls into a hard flat white that made her think, absurdly, of the annex's cold fluorescent light the morning she'd laid three dead files side by side. Reyes stood very still at the edge of the car, watching her photograph it, and said nothing at all — no explanation offered, no plausible account of an old wristband left behind in an unrelated transfer, none of the fluent generosity he produced for everything else. It was the second time in four days Elena had watched something behind his face go quiet in a way that wasn't composure, and this time he didn't recover it fast enough to hide that he'd noticed her noticing.

The sub-level corridor beyond the open doors ran dark in both directions, the overhead lights out or never installed, snowmelt-cold air breathing up out of it like the inside of a cave that had never once been warm. Adeyemi found the switch by feel and it did nothing. Somewhere down that unlit throat, distinct enough that all three of them heard it and none of them said so, something metal ticked twice against something else metal and then went still, the sound of a large space settling, or of someone in it going carefully, deliberately quiet.

"Flashlights," Adeyemi said, already reaching for his. "Now."

Reyes moved to follow them in, and for the first time in five weeks Elena put a hand flat against his chest and stopped him at the threshold — not an accusation, nothing she could have written down and defended, only the plain fact that whatever waited past this door had waited for her specifically, and she did not yet know what it would do with an audience of two. "Stay with Kessler," she told him, and watched him absorb the small, deliberate insult of being managed for once instead of managing, his face arranging itself, a half-second too late, into agreeable concern.

Elena stepped into the dark corridor first, her light finding nothing but poured concrete and a bank of shut steel doors receding further than the beam could reach, and understood, with the particular cold clarity of a woman who has spent five weeks learning to recognize the shape of a door built to be found exactly this way, that whatever waited at the end of it had been waiting, patiently, for her to be the one who walked in. Behind her Adeyemi came on counting doors under his breath, out loud, the way he counted everything now, so that nothing again would happen to him in a silence he could later claim he hadn't heard.
