She did not drink the tea, and she made not drinking it look like nothing at all.

It was an old trick, older than this case, older than the department — you set the cup down mid-motion, as if a thought had simply arrived and displaced it, and you let the thought be the visible thing instead of the abstaining. Elena let her eyes go middle-distance, toward the leaded window and the rain sheeting down it in long unbroken ropes, and said, "The board wants a name by Friday," as though that were what had stopped her hand, and Reyes said, "Thursday, actually. They moved it up an hour ago," and the moment passed the way a held breath passes, unremarked, and she had her opening and asked him, plainly, where Daniel Voss was currently residing, because if the department was going to be handed a face by the weekend she intended to have looked at that face herself first, in a room with no one else's story already laid over it.

He gave her the address without hesitating — a rooming house on the cannery side of Harbor Street, one of the old boardinghouses built for dockworkers when the dock still worked, gone half to seasonal shelter now — and told her, gently, that Voss did not do well with strangers, and she thanked him, and left before the rain could get any harder, and did not let herself think, until she was three blocks away with the wipers losing the argument against the windshield, about what her hand had actually been doing when it stopped.

She parked above the ferry apron because the turnout was empty and because she needed, for a minute, to not be moving.

The thought, when she let it back in, was smaller and colder than she had braced for. It was not *he knows something he shouldn't.* It was quieter than that, and worse for being quieter: *he reached for the exact word.* Not a near word. Not a kind word that happened to land close. *Steered* — the precise shape of the thing she had never said aloud to anyone but Sandoval's file and her own ceiling at three in the morning, the private architecture of her fear about what had been done to Mara, about what this whole building did to the people it kept — and Adam Reyes, who by his own account had known her three weeks and had every reason in the world to reach for *manipulated*, or *used*, or *targeted*, any of the ordinary words a kind man offers a tired woman, had instead put his hand directly on the one word that was hers. He could not have known it was hers. That was the relief, and she had felt the relief arrive first, honestly, gratefully — and then, a half-second behind it, the second thought had come up through the floor of the first one like cold water finding a crack: a man does not reach for the exact right word by accident. He reaches for it because it is already in his mouth. Because it is how he thinks, all the time, about everyone, and he had simply, for once, let her hear him think it.

She was not ready to call it more than that. She was careful, sitting there with the engine ticking and the strait gone the color of poured lead beyond the rail, to name only what she had actually observed and not what she wanted it to mean: that Adam Reyes was a man whose kindness had aim. That he had been, for three weeks, extravagantly, unstintingly generous to her, and that generosity, examined at the angle she was examining it now instead of the angle of gratitude, looked less like warmth and more like a hand cupped very gently under her elbow, steering her — there it was again, the word, and she let herself have it this time, deliberately — steering her toward doors he had already chosen for her to walk through. The Voss trap handed over so honestly it disarmed the handing. *I think you're the only one in this who isn't being steered.* She turned the sentence over twice and found, on the second turn, that it was the kind of sentence a man says either because it is true or because he needs it believed, and that from where she sat there was no way yet to tell the two apart, and that not being able to tell them apart was itself the entire shape of what this building did to people. She thought of Marguerite's tea, twice in one day, and did not let herself finish the thought.

She was not going to accuse a grieving man of manufacturing his own vocabulary. She was going to do what she had done with everyone else who fit both doors: watch, and not spend it.

It occurred to her, driving the last mile with the wipers slapping their useless rhythm, that she had spent three weeks being grateful to a man for handing her exactly what she needed exactly when she needed it, and had called that grief, had called it collegiality, had called it, once, kindness, and that gratitude and suspicion were built from precisely the same raw material — attention, paid closely enough that it could not be mistaken for anything else — and that she had simply never before had a reason to weigh which one she was standing in. She did not sleep well. She had not expected to.

She drove home in the dark and did not call Adeyemi, because Adeyemi would ask, kindly, why she was going to see Voss alone and off the log, and she did not currently have an answer that would survive Sandoval's file, and some mornings the honest answer — *because if the department gets to him first he will be a photograph before he is a person, and I would like, once, to meet a man before the city finishes deciding what he is* — was not one she trusted herself to say out loud without it also being, a little, about Mara.

The rain had not let up by morning. It had, if anything, found a second register, harder, more committed, running in the gutters along Harbor Street in a brown noisy rush that carried a dockworker's glove and half a paper cup past her ankles as she found the boardinghouse — three stories of gray shingle gone the color of driftwood, a hand-lettered sign in the window that said ROOMS in a font from another decade, a smell in the stairwell of instant coffee and wet wool and something institutional under both, bleach maybe, or the particular disinfectant of places that had once been inspected and no longer expected to be.

Daniel Voss opened the door on the chain first, the way people who had learned to be afraid always did, and looked at her badge for longer than most people looked at badges, reading it the way you'd read a warning label, and then let the chain off.

He was not what the city had built. That was the first thing, and it did something to her chest she had not prepared for. He was thin in the specific way of a man who forgot to eat rather than a man who couldn't afford to, gray coming in early and unevenly at his temples, a flannel shirt buttoned to the throat against a room that did not need it, and eyes that would not hold hers for more than a second at a time — not evasion, she understood almost immediately, but a kind of pain at being seen, the wince of a man who had had his face read wrong by strangers so many times that he now flinched from the reading itself.

"You're the one from the paper," he said. Not a question.

"I'm the one who isn't," she said, and something in his shoulders came down half an inch.

His room held a single window that looked, she realized with a small cold drop in her stomach, straight across the water at the Mercer Institute's east wing — not by accident, she understood, once she'd seen the angle of the chair pulled up close to the glass, the two years of a body's weight worn into that one spot on the sill. He watched the lantern window. Every night, he told her, not ashamed of it, the way a man isn't ashamed of breathing. He had watched it for two years, since they discharged him "not because I was better," he said, "because I stopped being useful to watch," and something in the phrasing snagged in her like a hook finding cloth, useful, the same word the file had used about Mara, and she filed it and did not let her face move.

There was a shelf under the window, and on it, arranged with a care that told her more than anything he'd said so far, a row of small spiral notebooks, each one dated on the spine in a careful draftsman's hand. "Two years of it," he said, when he saw her looking. "The nights it's gold. The nights it's dark. The nights somebody forgets and leaves the hall light burning till dawn. Nobody ever asked to see them. My caseworker calls it fixation." He said the word the way a man repeats a diagnosis he has decided not to fight anymore, no heat left in it, only the shape of something worn smooth by handling. Elena asked if she could look, and he hesitated — the first flare of the old wariness — and then, slowly, as if testing the weight of the decision before he committed to it, slid the most recent notebook two inches toward her across the sill, no farther, the way you'd offer a hand to something that might still bite.

She did not open it. Not yet, not in that room, with him watching her decide what kind of person she was going to be about it. She only laid two fingers on the cover, an acknowledgment, and told him she'd want it eventually, properly, logged and witnessed so it could never be waved off as a stalker's diary in a courtroom, and watched something in him unclench half a degree at being told the truth of what it would cost rather than a comforting lie about how easy it would be.

"The night she died," Elena said. "Did you watch it that night?"

He nodded, once, and his hands, folded in his lap, tightened on each other. "It went dark early," he said. "Not at the regular time. And then — for a few minutes, maybe ten — something came on that wasn't the lantern color. Colder. Like somebody carrying a flashlight room to room, not the lamp they always leave. Then it went dark again. All the way dark. Darker than I ever saw it before, because usually there's a nightlight in the hall window even when the big one's off, and that night there wasn't even that." He looked up at her, finally, and held it. "I called the tip line. Nobody called back. I called again. Somebody wrote it down and never called back. I know how it looks, a man like me calling in tips about a dead woman. I know what I look like on paper."

"You look," Elena said carefully, "like the only witness anyone's actually asked."

He almost smiled, and it cost him something to do it, and she understood before she left that whatever else this man was, he was not the thing the board wanted delivered by Friday — Thursday, an hour moved up, a city assembling its monster out of a face and a coincidence of geography — and that the tenderness she felt toward him, sitting in that cold clean room being believed for the first time in two years, was its own kind of danger, because it was so much easier to be kind to a man once you had already decided he was safe to be kind to.

She was three blocks from the boardinghouse, rain drumming the roof of the car in that same second harder register, when the phone buzzed once against the passenger seat. Not Adeyemi. Not Sandoval. The old clinic exchange, silent since the taillights on the headland, four words lit against the gray morning like something laid down on a table between them:

*You found him first.*
