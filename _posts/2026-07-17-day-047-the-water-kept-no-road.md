The footprints ended where the rock apron met the tide, and that was the first wrong thing about them.

Elena crouched at the last clean print, gloved fingers spread above it without touching, and watched her own breath drift sideways over the crust of refrozen snow. The cold had teeth in it this morning, a dry, painless cold that didn't announce itself so much as arrive already finished, and the sky over the strait was the flat hard blue of a held note. Behind her, Adeyemi worked a slow arc along the waterline with his phone flashlight, though there was more than enough light now to see by; it was habit, she thought, the way some men still reached for a gun after they'd already cleared a room.

"Nothing past this," he said. "No drag mark, no scuff, nothing that says a boat came up on the rocks. It's like he stepped into the water and kept walking."

"Or he didn't step into the water at all." Elena straightened, following the last four prints with her eyes instead of her feet. They came in evenly spaced, unhurried, and stopped a full stride short of the tideline, as if whoever made them had known exactly where the water would be at that hour and had no intention of getting his boots wet finding out. "Somebody who reads a tide table the way I read a case file."

Adeyemi crouched where she had been and turned his light flat along the last print's edge, hunting for a heel brand, a wear pattern, anything that would let a shoe go into evidence instead of a photograph. "Government-issue winter boot, common tread, half-size range at best. We're not walking this into a courtroom."

"I know." She said it without heat. Five days ago she would have argued the point out of stubbornness alone; this morning she just filed it, the way she'd learned to file most things this case handed her — true, useless, kept anyway.

She was turning back toward the rock apron's high side, meaning to start the tedious work of photographing the whole line before the thaw or a second tide erased it, when something in the frozen tide pool nearest the last print caught the light and held it, a small hard gleam under an inch of skinned ice.

She went down on one knee again, careful of her weight on the rock, and worked the ice loose with the edge of a key rather than a boot heel. Underneath, sitting in a half-inch of standing water gone solid overnight, was a lozenge of glass no bigger than a thumbnail, cobalt-blue, its edges rounded in a way that looked at first glance like ordinary sea glass — the harbor was full of the stuff, ground smooth by decades of tide against broken bottle glass from the cannery years. But sea glass came frosted, matte, its surface pitted by sand and salt until it looked like a held breath. This was warm-looking even frozen, its surface faintly glossy, and on the flat underside, when she tilted it to the light, she found the ghost of a whorl pressed into it — not a scratch, not wear, but a print, small and deliberate, the kind of mark a thumb leaves in something that was still soft enough to take one.

"That's not beach glass," Adeyemi said, over her shoulder now.

"No." She turned it once more before she bagged it, unmarked, dated, her own initials on the seal. "Beach glass doesn't remember who held it before it set."

She didn't say the other thing she was thinking, which was that she had seen glass worked soft and marked like that only once before, in a photograph on a gallery invitation Reyes had left face-up on his desk three weeks ago and never mentioned again — Clara Mercer's name under it in a thin, elegant type, a woman Elena had not yet spoken to, had not yet been given a reason to speak to, and did not, standing on a frozen rock apron with a stranger's fingerprint fused into blue glass, feel ready to invent one.

It could have come from anywhere. A child's marble, a costume-jewelry bead, a decade of harbor debris finally working its way to the surface the way the water gave everything back eventually, on its own schedule, indifferent to what it cost the person waiting on the shore. She told herself that twice, in the flat, procedural voice she used for evidence she hadn't earned the right to interpret yet, and bagged it anyway, because the flat voice had been wrong about smaller things than this before.

They worked the line for another forty minutes without finding anything else — no cigarette ends, no dropped glove, no second print pattern that might have argued for two people instead of one — and then the cold won, the particular cold that stopped being weather and became a decision your body made for you. Walking back up toward the cars, Adeyemi was quiet in a way she'd learned to read as a man doing arithmetic he didn't like.

"Sophie's recital's Tuesday," he said finally, not looking at her. "The dance one. Fourth year running I've told her mother I'd try."

"You should go."

"I'm going." He said it flatly, like a verdict already returned, and she understood this was not a question he was asking her permission for. "I sat on my hands eleven days once because the paperwork said wait, and there wasn't anybody left to find in time. I'm not doing the version of that where I miss my own kid's recital because a case that isn't mine to save alone needs me at six o'clock on a Tuesday." He glanced at her sideways. "That's not me stepping back. I've got someone I trust watching Okafor's block in the evenings, off the books, nobody Sandoval's heard of. It's not much. It's what I've got room for."

"It's more than he's had." She meant it, and something in her chest loosened half a degree at the thought of it — one more set of eyes on Noah that wasn't hers, wasn't Reyes's, wasn't a number with no name behind it.

"Don't thank me yet." Adeyemi shoved his hands deeper into his coat. "I've been wrong about how much I could cover before. I told myself Danvers just needed one more day of patience, and one more day turned out to be the exact day it couldn't afford. I'm not running that experiment twice on a kid who does data entry for a living." He said it without self-pity, the way he said most true things about himself, like a man reading off a gauge rather than confessing to one. "You keep doing what you're doing. I'll keep doing this. Between the two of us maybe nobody has to find out the hard way what I already know about waiting."

Cell coverage found them again at the top of the rise, three bars climbing to four, and Elena called Noah before the cold could talk her out of it.

He picked up on the second ring, his voice pitched low, a kettle or a tap running somewhere behind him. "I'm okay," he said, before she'd asked. "I know that's not what you called to hear, but I am."

"Talk to me about the notebook."

"Two more nights. Maybe three." A pause, the water sound cutting out — he'd have stepped away from the sink. "I keep thinking I've got it all and then I remember another session, another line she made me type twice because the first time I got a comma wrong, and I have to go back and fit it in where it actually goes instead of just where I remember it. I'm not going to hand you something with holes in it if I can help it."

"I don't need it perfect. I need it somewhere safer than your aunt's spare room."

"It is safe." He said it too fast, the way people said things they'd already argued themselves into believing. "Nobody knows where she lives except you, and you didn't write it down."

She let that sit, unwilling to spend the third set of footprints on him yet, unwilling to hand him one more thing to carry through a cold night alone. "Has anything changed? Anybody near the building, a car that doesn't belong?"

The pause this time was longer, and she heard him decide, in real time, whether to tell her. "There was a car outside last night. Sedan, gray, maybe dark green, engine running the whole time I was watching from the window — I couldn't get the plate, the angle's wrong from up there, and by the time I went down for a better look it was pulling off the corner. Could've been anybody waiting on somebody. Could've been nothing."

"Could've been," Elena agreed, and did not believe it, and did not tell him so, because there was nothing useful two more nights of fear would buy either of them. "Tuesday. First light, same as before, or sooner if anything at all feels wrong — you don't wait for the appointment if the ground moves under you."

"I know how this works now," he said, and there was something almost gentle in it, a boy who had learned, the hard way, to stop being the one who left a room looking untouched. "I'll call."

She was three steps from the car when her phone buzzed again, and for one stupid, hopeful half-second she thought it might be Adeyemi, already at the wheel, telling her to hurry against the cold. It was Reyes.

*Heard there was some business down at the old terminal this morning. Hope everyone's all right — that stretch of rock gets treacherous below the tide line. Let me know if the Institute can help with anything.*

She stood with the phone in her bare hand, the cold biting at her knuckles, and read it twice. Adeyemi hadn't called it in yet — she'd watched him decide, on the drive up, to log it quietly through channels that wouldn't reach a clinic liaison line for hours, if at all. Sandoval didn't know. The department dispatcher who'd take the eventual report didn't know. There was no version of this morning that should have reached Adam Reyes before it reached her own captain.

She typed nothing back for a long moment, turning the little glass lozenge over in her coat pocket without meaning to, feeling its rounded edge through two layers of wool. Somewhere in the last six weeks she had begun keeping a private ledger of every time Reyes knew a thing before he had any honest way of knowing it, a column that did nothing but grow, currency she couldn't yet spend on anyone who'd believe her.

*We're fine,* she wrote back. *Nothing the Institute needs to worry about.*

She was reaching for the door handle, telling herself the cold was making her careless, when the phone buzzed a third time — not Reyes's contact card this time, but the flat gray unknown-number field she'd learned to read the way other people read a held breath.

*Tell the tech to log it as beach glass. It isn't.*

Elena stood very still on the frozen rock apron with her hand on the cold door handle, the little cobalt lozenge riding in her pocket like a coal, and understood that she had told no one — not Adeyemi, not the evidence log, not even her own voice out loud — what she had found in that tide pool or what she meant to call it when she wrote it up. She had thought the word *beach glass* exactly once, alone, in her own head, deciding what lie to tell the report.

Somebody had been standing close enough to hear her think it.

She did not look up at the water. She already knew there would be nothing on it to see.
