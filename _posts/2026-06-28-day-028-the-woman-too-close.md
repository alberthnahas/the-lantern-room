The cold had a new manner to it on Friday morning, a stillness that was almost courteous, the way a room goes quiet when someone in it has decided something. Elena stood at her kitchen window in the Heights with the blinds half-down and watched the strait lie flat and gray past the rooftops, frost set hard in the window seams like something poured and left, and she did not think about the gray door, or the coat, or the weight in the left breast that did not move when the man moved. She thought about Marguerite, because Marguerite was the problem now, and the problem had a shape Elena had seen twice already and had not been able to stop either time.

It was the shape of a person who knew too much and was not being watched closely enough yet to be afraid the way they should be.

She had not slept. She had lain in the dark with her phone face-down on the nightstand beside the stiff, un-oiled drawer that held the photocopy of Mara's file, and she had run the arithmetic of the last three weeks the way you run a column of figures you already know will not balance. Noah had given her the gold-window glyph and the where of the gray door, and Noah was, by the bible of this thing, the next name on a list she could not see but had begun to feel the edges of. And now Marguerite — nineteen years at the desk, a witness who would swear to the vanishing hand in any room they put her in — had stood up. Standing up was the dangerous part. In Hartwell Bay, in the orbit of the Mercer Institute, people who stood up tended to be found sitting very still.

So she would not let it happen the way it had happened to the others, whatever the others turned out to be. That was the resolution she carried out of the sleepless night and down to the car: do not chase the coat. Do not chase the room. Keep the woman alive.

She called Marguerite from the parking apron above the ferry slip, where the signal was thin but the harbor could not see her, and she did not say any of the words that mattered. She said she had a question about visiting-hour procedure, about the soft traffic through the bronze door, the kind of administrative nothing a detective might genuinely ask, and Marguerite, who had spent nineteen years learning which questions were the real one wearing a coat, answered the surface of it and waited.

"There's a thing I want you to do for me," Elena said, "and it's going to sound like the opposite of what you'd expect."

"I'm listening."

"I want you to stop reading the book." She watched a gray ferry pull off the slip below her, slow as a thought. "Whatever you found yesterday — you found it. It's safe. It's in me now, and I can carry it, and you cannot carry it and stand at that desk smiling at him every morning at the same time. So you're going to put it down. You're going to be the woman who keeps the logbook and notices nothing, the way you were three weeks ago, and you're going to let me be the one who knows."

There was a silence on the line that was not the foghorn and not the wind. It was the particular silence of someone being asked to give up the one brave thing they had ever done.

"You think I'm the next one," Marguerite said. Not a question. She had done the same arithmetic, alone, at the desk, in the small hours when the lantern window across the water came back gold.

"I think you're close enough to the body of this that I don't want to test the theory." Elena chose the word body on purpose and heard Marguerite hear it. "You watched his coat for three weeks. That means three weeks of standing within reach of the thing he's afraid to set down, and being kind to him, and him being kind back. The kindness is how you'll know it's working — that's the part I need you to believe. When he's gentlest with you is when you should be most afraid. Not because he'll be cruel. Because he won't have to be."

"He brought me tea this morning," Marguerite said, very quietly, and the line went cold in a way the weather could not account for.

Elena closed her eyes. "Then it's already started. Put down the book. Notice nothing. If he asks you anything — anything at all, even the time of day — you give him the surface and nothing under it, and you call me from a phone that isn't the desk line." She made herself say the next part gently, because it was the cruelest thing in the whole conversation and the truest. "You did the brave thing. You don't have to do it twice. Brave twice in a place like this is just a slower way of being found."

When she hung up she sat for a long moment with both hands on the wheel and understood that she had just done to Marguerite exactly what the watcher had done to her — narrowed the woman's world to a single safe room and told her to stay in it for her own good. The difference, she told herself, was direction. She was closing a window to keep someone alive; the listening closed them to keep someone useful. But she sat with the discomfort of how alike the two gestures looked from the outside, and she did not let herself off it cheaply.

She drove north afterward, out past the last of the coverage, to the dead-signal turnout above the rocks where she had first played the L-9 tape, because she had one move left that did not put a living person in a chair, and it was the oldest move she had: she went back to the dead.

Not the tape. She had wrung the tape. The thing she had not done — the thing she had been circling since the margins of Mara's and Sophie's files first showed her the program's grammar — was to test whether the grammar was only those two girls, or whether it was a language. Two notations in one obscured hand was a coincidence a defense lawyer could drown in an afternoon. A third instance, in a wholly separate place, kept by a person with no stake, was a pattern. Marguerite had given her the third instance of the hand. But the hand was not yet the same as the script. A man could sign keys out in the dark for twenty years and still be only a thief. What she needed was the proof that the words in the margins — she is ready to be useful, the lantern is ready to be lit — were not a private habit but an issued language, said about more than one finished person, on a schedule, like a stage direction printed in more than one playbook.

She had a way to test it that cost no one anything. Acheson.

Old Doctor Acheson had signed the typescript on Sophie Lund's drowning fifteen years ago — no evidence of third-party involvement — the same sentence Vivian had later set over Mara, and Acheson was eighty-one now and retired to a care home on the dry side of the peninsula and had answered, when she'd first found him while pulling Sophie's file, with the loose unguarded candor of a man who had outlived everyone who could be hurt by what he remembered. He was not a Lantern figure. He was a coroner's physician who had signed what he was handed. And men who sign what they are handed sometimes keep, out of a craftsman's tidiness, the carbons of what they signed.

She reached him by the care-home landline from a payphone in the dry town beyond the dead zone — a payphone, because she would not carry this thread on a tower the watcher could climb — and Acheson was glad of the call the way the very old are glad of any voice. She did not mention Sophie. She did not mention Mara. She asked him, with the dull patience of an administrator clearing a backlog, whether in his coroner's years he had ever noticed referral language repeating across unrelated files — boilerplate, she called it, the way departments reuse a phrase — because the county was, she said, auditing old templates.

Acheson laughed, a dry rustle. "Templates. We never had templates worth the name. But I'll tell you the strange thing, since you ask, and nobody ever has." He took his time; she let him. "There was a turn of phrase used to come over with the Mercer referrals. Always the Mercer ones. Said a person was 'ready to graduate.' I thought it a kindness at first — hopeful, you know, like a child finishing school. I had three or four of them cross my desk over the years. Drownings, a fall, the bath girl —" he caught himself, gentle, not knowing what he was holding, "— sad cases, all of them, and every one with that same little word tucked in the margin where the family would never see it. Ready to graduate. Graduated. I asked Vivian about it once, at a dinner. She smiled at me the way she smiled and said it was an old teacher's habit, and I let it lie, because she was Vivian and you let her things lie." A pause. "Why do you ask, dear? It's nothing. It's an old woman's word."

Elena held the payphone receiver so hard the cold of it went into her hand. Three or four of them. Over the years. The same word, the same margin, the same small mercy of language laid over the same kind of death — and an old, honest man who had read it as kindness and let it lie, because she was Vivian and you let her things lie, which was the sentence the whole town had been signing for twenty years.

"It's nothing, you're right," she said, and made her voice a clerk's again, because his safety depended on his believing it was nothing. "Boilerplate. I'll close the audit note." She thanked him and asked, lightly, whether he still kept any of his old carbons, and Acheson said he'd burned the lot when he moved, doctor's duty, and she was glad, fiercely and privately glad, because a man with no carbons was a man with nothing worth coming for.

She hung up and stood in the cold of the dry town with the dead phone in its cradle and felt the pattern lock its third corner into something that was no longer a triangle but a room she could almost stand up in. The margins had given her two. The logbook hand had given her continuity. And Acheson — a witness who had never met the Lantern Room, who thought he was discussing paperwork — had just told her the word was not Mara's and not Sophie's. It was a vocabulary. Graduate. Ready. Three or four over the years, and those only the ones whose deaths reached a coroner's desk. The script was not a wound worn by two girls. It was a curriculum, and it had a faculty, and the faculty kept the kind of records you signed for in the dark.

She did not name the faculty. She had reached the man's face again and turned from it again, because the case had been built to hand her that face when she wanted it most, and wanting was the lever. She wrote in her notebook, small and flat: Acheson — "ready to graduate" across 3–4 Mercer referrals, years apart. Coroner read as kindness. Word, not wound. She underlined it once and put the pen away.

It was nearly dark when she drove back into coverage, the strait gone the color of a coin, the gull-less sky lowering toward the first real weather in days. Her phone found the towers on the climb above the cannery road and shivered awake with the small electric hum of messages arriving all at once, and she let them come, because she had learned that the order they arrived in was sometimes the only thing the watcher could not stage.

There was nothing from the clinic exchange. Nothing from the pier's third number. Nothing from the fourth, or the fifth. The silence of all of them at once was its own kind of noise; the listening was loudest, she had learned, in the rooms it did not bother to enter, and tonight it had not bothered, which meant tonight she had moved somewhere it could not yet reach — a payphone, a dead old man's burned carbons, an eighty-one-year-old who thought he'd been talking about templates.

Then one message, from a number she did know, the number she had taken on Day One and used only once. Marguerite. Sent, the timestamp said, four minutes ago, in the last of the light, from a phone that was not the desk line.

She did not put down the book.

Three words, and beneath them, where the older woman's careful thumbs had added a fourth line she had clearly sat with before sending, one more:

He just asked me where you've been all day.
