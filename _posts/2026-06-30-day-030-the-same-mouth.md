The records annex was colder than the morgue, and Elena had come to prefer it for that. Cold rooms kept their counsel. She let herself in at first light on Sunday with the spare badge-key Adeyemi had signed over to her two cases ago and never asked back, and she did not turn on the overheads, only the green-shaded lamp at the far carrel where the un-oiled bottom drawer waited, the one whose runners she'd left dry on purpose so that no one could open it quietly, not even her. The drawer shrieked when she pulled it. That was the point. In a building this still, she wanted to be the loudest thing in it, because the loudest thing in a room is the one with nothing to hide.

Outside, the first real snow of the year was trying to happen and failing, coming down as a wet gray suggestion that melted on the glass before it could mean anything. Autumn had finally given up the coast. She'd watched the season turn from a payphone and a diner booth and a windowless cabinet, and now it had turned all the way, the strait past the annex window the color of a coin left out in the rain. Thirty days. A month ago Vivian Mercer had been alive and lying about a girl who drowned, and Elena had believed she understood the shape of her own grief. She no longer believed she understood anything except the work in front of her, which was the only mercy left, and she bent to it.

She had three folders, and she had told no one she had them.

Mara's, the photocopy she'd carried for six years and only learned to read three weeks ago. Sophie Lund's, pulled fifteen years late, flagged the instant she'd signed for it, Sandoval watching the pull from two floors up. And the third — the third had cost her the most to get without lighting her badge, and she'd gotten it the old way, the only way anything moved in Hartwell Bay that wasn't supposed to: by asking a frightened person to be brave once and promising never to ask again. Earl Pruitt, the cannery groundskeeper who'd minded the rocks the morning Sophie went into the water, had a nephew who'd kept the institute's basement files in the lean years before Noah, and the nephew, for the price of being left alone forever after, had pulled one drowned boy's intake from a dead box marked only with a year. Not L-9. A different graduate — one of the names Acheson had handed her without knowing it: the bath girl, he'd said, and a fall, and a drowning or two, three or four over the years, every one with that same little word tucked in the margin where the family would never see it.

She had found the fall. A man named Tomas Reyland, twenty-six, who had gone off the cannery catwalk eleven years ago in a way the file called consistent with intoxication and lack of caution, signed by a coroner who was not Acheson and who had retired to Arizona and died there. Tomas Reyland had been a Mercer patient for two years before the catwalk. And on the back of his intake, in the box Elena now knew to turn over, in the cramped self-drowning hand she could have picked out of a thousand now, was a letter and a number she did not let herself read aloud.

Three folders. She laid them across the carrel in the lamp's green circle, spines toward her, and she opened each to the page she wanted and turned each face-up, and she set them side by side, edge to edge, the way the dead woman at the diner had named it for her without knowing — the two margins set against a third in a single frame, words side by side where no defense could call them coincidence — except now there were three frames, three mouths, and she made herself read them not as a sister or a witness or a woman who needed something but as a clerk, a stranger, a juror who had never heard any of these names before in her life.

Sophie Lund. Age fifteen. In the obscured hand, in the margin of the third session sheet, dated eleven days before the water: the lantern is ready to be lit.

Tomas Reyland. Age twenty-four at intake. In the same hand, the same place on the page, dated — she checked it twice — nine days before the catwalk: the lantern is ready to be lit.

Mara Vega. Age twenty-nine. In the same hand, the same margin, the same fold of paper where the eye slid past it: she is ready to be useful.

Two of three. Not three of three. She sat with that, because it mattered, because the difference between two and three was the difference between a phrase and a fingerprint, and she would not let herself round up the way grief wanted her to. Sophie and Tomas had the lantern. Mara had the use. Close, a defense would say. Suggestive. A doctor's private shorthand, a teacher's habit, the way Acheson had let it lie because she was Vivian and you let her things lie.

So she went deeper. She turned past the margins to the body of each file, to the session transcripts Vivian had kept in her own clean dictated hand, and she read down the recited portions, the places where a patient's words were set in quotation and a clinician's in plain type, and she ran her finger down all three the way she'd watched Marguerite read pressure and slant down nineteen years of borrowers, looking not for the margin's instruction now but for the patient's own voice, the thing the program put into the mouth and called recovery.

And there it was, in Tomas Reyland's transcript, four sessions before the catwalk, in quotation marks, attributed to the patient as a sign of progress: I am not what was done to me. The water closed and the lantern stayed lit.

The same words. Not the margin's grammar this time — the catechism. The exact line she had heard in a dead boy's prayer-flat voice on a tape played alone above the rocks, the exact line she had heard her own sister recite in the same taught flatness, the exact second clause that lived on no door and no wall and that Reyes had finished for her over bad coffee as if it cost him nothing. Tomas Reyland, dead eleven years, a man she had learned existed yesterday, had said it too. Had been made to say it. Had it written down as the moment he got well.

She found it in Sophie's transcript next, two weeks before the water, the fifteen-year-old reciting it in the same quotation marks, the same approving plain-type clinician's note beside it: subject integrates the frame. And she found, in Mara's, the line her sister had spoken on side B of a tape with a name cut out of it, the line that had detonated six years of Elena's understanding — I just remember already being inside — and beneath it, two sessions on, in quotation, in the taught flatness, the catechism entire.

Three mouths. Three files that had never touched, kept in three boxes in three years, signed by coroners who had never met, read by families who saw the surface and grieved the surface and buried the surface. A fifteen-year-old girl who drowned. A man who fell. Her sister, in a bath gone cold. All three of them, before they died, taught to say the same sentence and praised in the margin for learning it, and all three of them carrying, in the same vanishing hand, the same little stage direction about a lantern being ready, written by someone who had decided when each of them was finished.

It was not a phrase. It was a script. It was not a symptom. It was a curriculum, and it had a faculty, and the faculty kept attendance.

Elena put her hands flat on the carrel the way Marguerite had laid hers on the diner table, to stop them, and found they did not shake. That frightened her more than shaking would have. She had wanted Vivian to be guilty for six years and the wanting had blinded her — she'd said it to herself a dozen times like a penance — but this was not wanting. This was three pieces of paper that did not care what she felt, laid in a row under a green lamp, saying the same thing in the same hand to a stranger who had never loved any of these people. A juror would see it. Sandoval would see it. The words were side by side now, and no one in any room could call it coincidence, because coincidence does not recite.

She photographed them. Carefully, squarely, each margin and each transcript line, case numbers in frame and dates legible, the way you photograph a thing you mean to be believed about. And then she did the disciplined thing that was costing her more by the day: she did not write down where it pointed. She had the hand from Marguerite's book, the vocabulary from Acheson, and now the script itself across three unrelated dead — the program-proof she'd been refusing to reach for thirty days, because reaching too soon was how they'd planned to break her. She wrote, small and flat: Catechism + margin-instruction identical across Lund / Reyland / Vega. Three files, three deaths, one hand. Not a symptom. Issued. She underlined it once and did not underline it twice, and she did not write the name the underlining wanted, because the name was still a thing she'd reached for in want and could not yet hold in proof — and the whole machine had been built to feed her exactly that name at exactly this hour of exhaustion.

She closed the three folders. She slid them into her coat, all three, because they were not safe in any drawer in any building connected to the institute, and the shriek of the empty runners as she shut the drawer was the loudest honest sound she'd made in days.

It was full light when she came out, the failed snow turned to a thin cold rain, and her phone found the towers on the annex steps and woke with the small electric shiver she had come to read like weather. She let the messages come in their own order. Nothing from the channels. Nothing from the pier, the fourth number, the fifth. The silence of all of them at once, which she had learned meant she had been somewhere they could not reach — the cold annex, the dead box, the nephew who would now be left alone forever.

Then one. Not the clinic exchange. Not Mara's recycled number. A sixth number she had never seen, local prefix, and the message was not three words this time but a photograph, and the photograph was a page.

It was a transcript page. The taught flatness, the quotation marks, the catechism set in a patient's mouth and praised in the plain-type margin — she knew the layout in her body now, she had been reading nothing else for three hours. But it was not Sophie's page, and it was not Tomas's, and it was not Mara's. The hand in the clinician's margin was the same vanishing hand. The case number across the top had been thumbed away to a gray smear. And the date in the header had not.

The date was twelve days ago. November, this November, the one she was standing in the first cold rain of — and twelve days ago, after Vivian Mercer was already in the ground, after a tape on which the dying woman swore she meant to put the lantern out herself, someone in the same vanishing hand had sat across from a living patient and praised them, in the margin, for learning to say I am not what was done to me. The water closed and the lantern stayed lit.

The faculty had not buried its dead and walked away. Vivian was in the ground and the curriculum was still in session. Someone was still teaching the sentence. Someone had just sent Elena the proof, squarely framed and legible, the way you send a thing you mean to be believed about — and under the catechism, in the corner of the page, beside the gray smear where a case number should have been, was a small four-paned mark she had stopped mistaking for a smudge.

The gold window. The maker's mark. Signed, twelve days ago, on a graduate the program had finished while she was learning to read her own dead, by the same hand that had cut a name out of her sister's tape and laid a lantern in three margins and was telling her now, with no words at all, that thirty days of proof had only caught her up to where the work already was.
