The proof-of-delivery came up from Tacoma by fax, because Oskar Lind trusted email about as far as Vivian Mercer had trusted keyboards, and Elena stood over the squad-room machine on Saturday morning watching the page assemble itself line by line, like something surfacing through gray water. One case, sixty units. Shipped Monday the ninth. Delivered Tuesday the tenth, 9:41 a.m., MERCER INSTITUTE, EAST WING RECEIVING — HOLD FOR V.M. And in the signature field, round and open and entirely legible, a name: N. OKAFOR.

Thirteen days of this case had taught her that handwriting was a kind of weather, and every hand the case had shown her so far had been hiding something — a signature taught to disappear, block capitals reached up out of a grave, initials too clean to argue with. This one hid nothing. It was the writing of somebody young enough to believe a name was only a name, looped and earnest, the O of OKAFOR drawn as fat as a porthole. The first legible signature the case had volunteered. She distrusted it on principle, was briefly ashamed of the principle, and drove out to find its owner.

The snow had crossed the water in the night. The city woke to its first real dusting — a white film on roofs and windshields that the morning peeled off the asphalt by nine and left in the shadows of north-facing walls, where it meant to stay. The cold had stopped being a kind of weather and become a kind of architecture.

The clinic on a Saturday was a building being honest. No visitors, no performance; a skeleton staff and the heating ticking in the walls. Marguerite was at the front desk with the month's filing squared in front of her, and she looked up at Elena with the particular calm of a woman who has already decided whose side she is on. Elena said the name on the fax, and Marguerite's face did something small and protective.

"Noah," she said. "Dr. Mercer's research assistant. Four years. He's in most Saturdays — they don't pay him for it, he comes anyway." She straightened a stack that was already straight. "He's the only person left in this building who says her name at full volume. Everyone else has started dropping it, like a hot dish." She told Elena how to find receiving — out the side, down the service drive, the water side of the east wing — and then, quietly, to Elena's back: "He carries things, Detective. He's been carrying something since the third."

The receiving dock was a place the institute did not photograph for its brochures. A concrete ramp sloped down between the east wing and the seawall, ending at a rolling steel door with a person-door cut in beside it and a buzzer wired on crooked. The tide was out; the air smelled of creosote and iodine; somewhere up the strait the foghorn was keeping its slow appointment. Elena stood at the bottom of the ramp and looked up before she rang. From this side, the famous east-wing window was just glass — high, dark, daylight sliding off it. The city had named the building a lighthouse from across the water. From directly underneath, it was only a wall with a door in it, and the door was the color of the sky.

Noah Okafor opened the person-door before her finger reached the buzzer, which told her he had been watching for her through the wire glass. He was twenty-six and built like an apology, tall and slightly folded, a lab coat over a sweater over another sweater, a lanyard around his neck carrying more keys than a man of his rank should need. He had been crying at some point in the last day, and had stopped being embarrassed about it.

"You're the detective," he said. "Marguerite called down. I thought — can we talk in here? This is where it happens. The part you're going to ask about. I'd rather be standing in it."

Receiving was a cage of steel shelving, a package table, a clipboard hanging from a string. Their breath showed. He stood beside the table the way other witnesses sat behind lawyers.

He had signed for the tapes since his first month, he said. Four years, a case every eight weeks, twenty-six deliveries. Before him some other assistant had signed; before that, another; the routine was older than anyone's employment. Dr. Mercer had given him the protocol in person, in his first week, exactly once, and never referred to it again. When the case comes, sign for it. Don't open it. Don't enter it in stores — it isn't stores, it's mine. Carry it up the back stair to the second landing and leave it on the table outside the gray door. Then come down. Don't wait.

"And by morning it would be gone," Elena said.

"By morning it was always gone." He picked at the clipboard string. "Four years. I never saw that door open. I never met a single person on that stair. You start to feel like you're leaving food out for something. You laugh about it, and then you don't."

The gray door, he said, had no nameplate and no room number, paint the color of fog on fog, and a brass lock that matched no key he'd ever been issued — and he had been issued many; Vivian kept giving him keys the way other supervisors gave out praise. He had tried the door once. Once, his first year, eleven at night, the landing empty, just a polite, curious quarter-turn of a wrist. It hadn't moved. And the next morning Vivian Mercer had said, without looking up from her desk, "Curiosity is a fine quality, Mr. Okafor. So is knowing when to retire it." He never found out how she knew. He never tried again.

"She gave me her slips to type, sometimes," he went on. "Labels. She'd write them by hand and I'd type them onto the adhesive strips, because she said her handwriting was a liability she had no intention of correcting. Never names. A letter and a number. L-7. L-12. L-31. I typed hundreds of them." He looked at the shelf where the cases had sat. "Once a number stopped coming. L-9. Two years of L-9 and then nothing, and I asked her — joking, you understand, the way you test ice — what happened to L-9. And she said, not looking up, she never looked up: 'L-9 graduated.'" He tried to smile. "I thought it was wit. Doctor humor. It's been thirteen days and I can't get the word out of my mouth. Graduated."

Elena wrote the word down and boxed it, and did not let herself underline it twice.

"Tell me about October," she said, the way she had said it to Lind, and got the same result: he didn't ask which part.

In the middle of October, for the first time in four years, Vivian Mercer had come down to receiving herself. Stood where Elena was standing now, in her good coat, and looked at the cage and the table and the clipboard like a woman inspecting a wound. She had asked him one question — how many cases were in transit — and he'd told her none; the next wasn't due until winter. And then she'd said: "When the next case comes, Mr. Okafor, you bring it to me. Not the door. To my hands."

"I was glad," he said, and his voice cracked on the middle word. "God help me, I was glad. Four years of leaving boxes for a door, and she was finally going to let me hand her one like a person." He pressed the back of his wrist to his mouth, briefly, and took it away. "The next case came on the tenth of November. She'd been dead a week."

He had stood in the cage with it for twenty minutes, he said. The carrier gone, the case on the table, HOLD FOR V.M. on the label in a font that didn't know anything. Her instruction said bring it to my hands, and there were no hands. The old instruction said the door. "The routine didn't notice she died," he said. "It just kept arriving. And I didn't want to be the one who broke it — does that make sense? It was hers. It was the last thing of hers still running." So he had carried it up the back stair to the second landing, set it on the table, and walked down without waiting, four years of obedience driving his legs. Wednesday morning he had made himself go up and look.

The table was empty.

Elena kept her face level while the fact walked through her with its cold feet. Sixty hours of blank tape, swallowed by a gray door, eight days after the woman it was held for stopped having hands.

"Who else knows the routine?" she asked. "Anyone. Carriers, stores, custodians."

"Nobody. The carriers think it's office supplies. Stores has no record — she built it that way. It was me and her." He heard the sentence finish itself and went a shade grayer. "Me and her. And whoever opens the door."

There was one more thing, offered last because it cost him most. Late in October — after her visit to the cage, before the third of November — he had twice seen Dr. Mercer leave through receiving instead of the lobby, after dark, her bag on her shoulder. The bag had been heavy. Square-cornered. "Four years," he said, "the building only ever swallowed. Things went up that stair and never came back down. That was the first time I ever saw it give anything back. Both nights, maybe a dozen tapes' worth — a bag, not a library." He had assumed homework. He no longer assumed.

"Has anyone else asked you about this?" Elena said. "The tapes. The door."

He nodded slowly. "Dr. Reyes. Wednesday. He found me in the annex and made the tea himself — he's kind like that, everyone says how kind —" Noah stopped, reorganized, started again. "He asked if Dr. Mercer ever had me transcribe. Listen to tapes. Type from her voice. Twice he asked it, two different shapes. I said no, which is true. Labels aren't listening. And when he left he said if the police came I should route it through him, so I — so I 'wouldn't have to carry it alone.'" He looked at her, and there it was under the fright, a stubbornness she recognized from a key cabinet and a Tacoma workbench. "And then you called, and I came down here anyway. Because he asked what I'd heard. She would have asked what I'd seen. He doesn't know the difference."

Elena wrote that down too, once, and put the pen away before her hand could editorialize.

It was four-forty and already dying toward dark when they stepped out onto the dock. The tide had turned; the water was coming back in over the stones with a sound like slow applause. She asked her last question — who held keys to the back stair besides him — and Noah was lifting a hand to count names on his fingers when the light came on above them.

Gold. High up on the east wing, laid out over the dock and the seawall and the incoming water, the color every soul in Hartwell Bay would have known with their eyes shut. Noah went still with his hand half-raised, his face turned up into it like a man at a graveside watching the ground move.

"It's been dark," he said. "Since she died. I check every night from the bus stop. Eleven nights, dark."

Elena stood in the spill of it and did the arithmetic she didn't want. Sixty fresh tapes through the gray door on Tuesday. First light on Saturday. Her phone sat silent in her coat pocket, four days silent now, and standing under the lit window she finally understood the silence: the watcher had stopped texting because the next message was a window. And the light was not a slip, and it was not surveillance. It had been turned on the way you light a lamp in a window for someone you are expecting — and Elena, counting the floors to the gold pane with her heart going slow and loud, knew exactly who was expected.
