The trouble with a warning, Elena had learned, was that it told you as much about the one who sent it as the one who received it. *Don't play it where they can hear.* She had sat with the sentence all the way home from the cannery the night before, and again at three in the morning when the heat ticked in the pipes and the L-9 tape lay on the kitchen table inside a sandwich bag like something she had taken off a body. *They.* Not *I*. The watcher who had texted her from a third number had set themselves outside the danger — had pointed at a room and said, in effect, that the room had ears, and that those ears were not theirs. Which meant either it was true, or the watcher wanted her to behave as if it were, and she could not yet tell the difference, and the not-telling was, she suspected, the entire design.

So she did not play it at the precinct. She did not play it at the morgue, or at her desk, or in her own apartment where a photograph of a turned-away lamp had once arrived through rain-beaded glass. She drove instead, on the morning of the nineteenth of November with the frost no longer surrendering to the sun, north out of Hartwell Bay along the strait road until the city let go of her, past the last gas station and the boat launch and the place where the cell towers thinned to one bar and then to none. She had thought about this the way Vivian Mercer thought about everything: *the most dangerous thing in any building is a record.* If the watcher had reach inside the investigation, inside the ME's pipeline, inside the clinic exchange and somehow inside the dead recycled number of her sister, then the only safe room was no room at all. A car, cold, on a turnout above the water, where the only thing that could hear her was the strait.

She had bought the player in cash at a pawnshop two towns over, an old handheld microcassette transcriber with a foam earpiece, the kind a court reporter might have carried in 1994. She had not used the desk recorder from Vivian's office, which sat in evidence under case 11-0419, because evidence had a chain and a chain had hands and hands, she now understood, were the whole problem. She fit the L-9 tape into the player's jaw and felt the little resistance of the spools and did not press the button.

The water below the turnout was the color of a knife. A ferry crossed the middle distance, lit windows in the gray, carrying people who had never heard of a lantern room. Elena thought of Oskar Lind in his end unit off the port road, the old machinist saying *reuse is a kind of lying* — Vivian's words in his mouth — *whatever you put on top, the old word is still under it.* Ten thousand tapes in thirty years and never one reused, because a woman who reshaped people could not bear, apparently, to write over a single hour of the evidence of it. And here in Elena's hand was one hour she had been *meant* to find. That was the thing she kept circling. The figure on the pier had not dropped it and run. It had staged an empty seam and a missed payment and a palm held open, *I am holding nothing*, so that Elena would kneel and find this. A graduated subject's voice, planted like a clue in a game whose rules she did not have.

She pressed the button.

Tape hiss first — the long, soft static she had come to know, the sound of a room being recorded before anyone in it spoke. Then a chair. Then Vivian Mercer's voice, and Elena's stomach did the thing it always did, because she had now heard this voice enough to know it the way you know a smell from childhood, and the voice belonged to the woman who had signed her sister into the ground.

"All right," Vivian said, warm, unhurried, a voice that made you want to tell it things. "We don't have to begin where we left off. We can begin anywhere you like."

A silence. The hiss. Then a second voice, and Elena leaned into the foam earpiece without meaning to.

It was a man. Young — younger than the gravity of the room, a voice with the careful flatness of someone who had been taught, somewhere, to keep the weather off his words. "I keep coming back to the water," he said.

"Tell me about the water."

"It's not — it's not a memory. That's what I can't make you understand. It's not that I remember it. It's that I was *there*, and then I wasn't, and the part in between is yours now. You have it. I gave it to you and now I can't get it back."

"Whose water is it, when you stand in it?"

"Hers." A breath. "Sophie's."

Elena stopped the tape.

The turnout was very quiet. The ferry had nearly crossed. She sat with her thumb on the cold button and made herself breathe, because the name had gone into her like the cold off the water, like a hand laid flat against the glass. *Sophie.* The girl in the sun-faded photograph on Reyes's credenza, the patient who had drowned fifteen years ago, the name Vivian *kept there because it kept her honest*. The name that had been typed, clean and dead, beneath an outside-referrer stamp on the intake that steered Vivian's death toward Elena's unit. A girl fifteen years drowned, surfacing now in the voice of a stranger called L-9, a stranger who said *whose water it was* as if drowning were a thing one person could hand to another.

She pressed the button again.

"You didn't put Sophie in the water," Vivian said. Gentle. Certain. The voice of someone laying a stone exactly where she wanted it.

"No."

"Say the rest."

A long pause. The hiss. And then the young man said, in a cadence that was not quite his own, that had the worn-smooth quality of a thing said many times, of a prayer or a password: "I am not what was done to me. I am not what was done to me. The water closed and the lantern stayed lit."

Elena's hand was shaking and she set the player on her knee to keep it still.

*I am not what was done to us.* The bronze plaque on the clinic door. *The lantern stayed lit.* The word in Vivian's own embossed hand on a notebook page, pressed hard enough to mark three sheets down: *The lantern is lit.* The line in the midnight text to Gerald: *Tell her the lantern is lit.* The gold window across the black water. And now a graduated subject, on a tape buried under a pier for her to find, reciting it like catechism — *the lantern stayed lit* — and the woman who built him answering with a satisfaction so quiet it was almost tenderness.

"Good," Vivian said. "That's very good."

"It doesn't feel like mine."

"It isn't yours yet. That's all right. We say it until it is. Do you remember what we call this part?"

"Graduating," the young man said.

The word landed in the cold car and sat there. Elena thought of Noah Okafor at the receiving dock, asking what had happened to a code that stopped appearing, and Vivian not looking up: *L-9 graduated.* She had thought it a wry clinical joke, a discharge, a recovery. She understood now that it was the opposite of recovery. It was the moment the new thing finished setting, the way Clara Mercer's glass — Elena had read the file, the gallery, the kiln — the way molten glass stopped being liquid and became, forever, the shape the heat had decided. *Graduating.* Not the end of treatment. The end of the person who had walked in.

She let the tape run. The young man spoke about the water and Sophie and a feeling he could not place, and Vivian moved him through it with the soft, relentless patience of a tide, and twice more the recited line surfaced — *I am not what was done to me, the water closed and the lantern stayed lit* — smoother each time, more his, less his, until Elena could not have said whether she was listening to a man being healed or a man being written. She thought: *this is what they did to Mara.* And then she made herself not think it, because she did not know that, because wanting it to be true was exactly the lever someone had built this whole machine to pull, and a detective who knelt where she was told and heard what she wanted was no detective at all.

The session wound toward its end. "We'll stop there," Vivian said. "You did the hard part today."

"Will I remember this?"

"You'll remember exactly as much as you need to."

And then, after the chair, after the sound of a man standing, Vivian's voice changed. It dropped the warmth the way you set down a tool you no longer need, and she spoke not to the patient but to the machine, low, close to the microphone, the way Elena had heard her speak on the October 22 tape — a woman dictating to the only confessor she trusted, which was a spool of magnetic tape.

"L-9," Vivian said. "Third year. He is nearly finished, and I cannot decide whether finishing him is the kindest thing I have ever done or the worst. He came to me unable to live with what he watched happen to that girl. He will leave me able to live with anything. That is the trade. That is always the trade." A pause; the hiss; the distant foghorn even there, even on a tape years old, as though the horn had been sounding under this whole story from the start. "If anyone ever hears this who was not meant to — and I have spent my life making sure no one would — then the work has failed in the one way I always feared, and it has failed into the right hands. Find the others. They don't know what they are. God help me, most of them don't even know they graduated."

The tape clicked off.

Hiss. Then nothing. Elena sat with the dead player on her knee and the strait gone flat and metal below her, and she did not move, and the cold she had been keeping out of the engine all morning came in now and settled in her chest and stayed.

*Find the others.* As if Vivian had recorded it for whoever would one day kneel on a freezing pier. As if the woman had known, years back, that she would not be the one to undo this, that the undoing would fall to a stranger she had wronged, and had left, deliberately, in a voice across time, the one instruction that mattered. Elena thought of the codes in the black notebook — L-7, L-12, L-3, L-31 — a column of letters and numbers that were not names because the names had been the point of erasing. *Find the others.* They were out there in the city right now, on the ferries and behind the brass-barred teller windows and in the cannery vests, people carrying a recited line where a memory used to be, people who did not know what they were.

She rewound the tape one inch and listened again to the part she could not let alone. *He came to me unable to live with what he watched happen to that girl.* Watched. Not did. L-9 had *watched* Sophie Lund drown — and could not live with it — until Vivian made him able to live with anything. Which meant L-9 had been there, fifteen years ago, when a girl went into the water. Which meant the first sin of the lantern room had a witness, and the witness was alive, and somewhere in Hartwell Bay a man was walking around with the truth of Sophie Lund sealed under a sentence he repeated like weather.

Elena's phone, when she finally looked at it, showed no signal and no message — out here there was no one to reach her, which was the entire reason she had come. But she understood now that the silence was borrowed. The moment she drove back into the one bar, then two, then the full grid of the city's listening, the watcher would know she had played it. *Don't play it where they can hear.* She had obeyed. And obeying, she realized as she turned the key and the cold engine caught, was its own kind of graduating — a thing you did until it was yours.

She put the car in gear. Below her the ferry finished its crossing and let out its passengers into the gray, and Elena drove south toward the city and the gold window and the man with the steady hands and the dead girl's name typed clean beneath a stamp, carrying in a sandwich bag on the seat beside her a voice that had told her, fifteen years too late, that someone had watched Sophie Lund die and been taught to call it peace.
