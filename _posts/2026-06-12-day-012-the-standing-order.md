The snow began forty minutes south of Hartwell Bay and could not commit to itself — a thin, gray spitting that died on the windshield and lived on the firs, so that the highway ran dark and wet through a country slowly turning white at the edges. Elena drove with the radio off. She did not need it. She had a tape playing in her head that no one could subpoena: a dead woman's voice saying she had decided to put the lantern out herself, two knocks, a door opening, and that dry, intimate greeting — Well. You knock now? — and then the click, which was the loudest part. She had listened to the original exactly twice before logging it into evidence, and it had been playing ever since.

Gerald had given her the thread without knowing he held it. She bought tapes by the case from a man in Tacoma, because nobody makes them anymore. There had once been four magnetic-media suppliers in Tacoma. The phone book and three disconnected numbers had reduced them, like everything else in Vivian Mercer's world, to exactly one.

LIND AUDIO — MAGNETIC MEDIA, SERVICE & TRANSFER occupied the end unit of a strip of light industry off the port road, between a powder-coater and a place that rebuilt forklift batteries. Inside, the shop smelled of solder, ozone, and warm dust, and the shelves ran floor to ceiling with the corpses of formats: reel-to-reel decks with their mouths open, dictation machines, answering machines, a wall of cassettes in every size the century had tried and abandoned. A workbench under a magnifier lamp held a disemboweled recorder no bigger than a deck of cards, its parts laid out on white cloth with surgical neatness.

The man who came out of the back was somewhere in his seventies, with a machinist's forearms and a loupe pushed up on his forehead like a second pair of eyes that had seen finer things. The stitching on his shirt said OSKAR. Elena showed him the badge and said the name Mercer, and Oskar Lind did not look surprised. He looked like a man whose appointment had finally arrived.

"I read the papers," he said. "I've had her file sitting out since the Thursday. I wondered which of you would come, and how long it would take." He glanced at the gray window. "Twelve days. Not bad, for a woman who paid cash."

The file was a manila folder soft with age, and the account inside it was older than some detectives Elena had worked with. One case of microcassettes — sixty tapes — every eight weeks, on a standing order, for a few months short of thirty years. Never a lapse. Never a complaint. Never, Lind said, a reused tape, and he said it the way other men report miracles.

"Everybody reuses," he said. "Tape's dear now. You record over, you record over again. Not her. I asked her once why, when she was buying old stock off me at collector prices, and she looked at me the way I imagine she looked at her patients and said, 'Reuse is a kind of lying, Mr. Lind. Whatever you put on top, the old word is still under it.'" He closed the folder gently. "She's the only customer I ever had who was afraid of what a tape remembers."

Elena did the arithmetic in the margin of her notebook because her hands wanted the discipline. Six cases a year, give or take. Sixty tapes a case. Thirty years. The number came out a little over ten thousand, and she sat with it the way she had sat with the lividity photographs — letting it become a fact instead of a feeling. Ten thousand tapes. An hour of voice on each, give or take. A woman who labeled the margin of her appointment book L-7, L-12, L-3, and never wrote a name, had somewhere built a library of other people's insides ten thousand hours deep, and the office on the Heights had been emptied of perhaps a pocketful of it.

"Where did the cases ship?" she asked. "The house on the Heights?"

Lind turned the folder back around and put one blunt finger on the address line, and Elena copied it exactly, because exactness was all she had left: MERCER INSTITUTE, EAST WING RECEIVING — HOLD FOR V.M. "Thirty years," Lind said. "Never the house. Not once. I asked her about that too, back when I still asked her things. She said records live where records live."

Elena kept her face still while something cold walked through her. The east wing. The single window that burned gold over the water every night of her surveillance of her own grief, the window the whole city had nicknamed without knowing it was naming something. She wrote EAST WING in block capitals, looked at her own block capitals for a moment, and closed the notebook.

"Tell me about October," she said.

He didn't ask which part of October. That, more than anything, told her he had been waiting. "She called on the sixteenth. A Friday. She always called herself — no assistant, no email, she didn't trust a keyboard as far as she could throw it. I thought it was the renewal. It wasn't." He took the loupe off his forehead and set it on the bench, a man clearing the decks. "She canceled the standing order. Thirty years, and she canceled it in one sentence, like canceling the paper. And then she asked me a question nobody ever asks me, in here, surrounded by all this." He gestured at the wall of salvaged voices. "She asked how you destroy a tape so that it stays destroyed."

"What did you tell her?"

"The truth. Burning's certain but it's smoke and witnesses. Recording over is worse than nothing — it's a lie with the truth under it, her own words back at her. If you want it gone, you degauss. A proper deep-field bulk eraser, rated for the coercivity, both orientations, twice. Done right, there is no laboratory on this earth that gets it back. Done wrong —" he shrugged — "you've scrambled the surface and left the body."

"And what did she say?"

Lind looked at her, and for the first time the craftsman's detachment slipped, and underneath it was something that had been sitting on him for a month. "She said, 'Properly, then, or not at all.'"

Elena heard the embossed page before she could stop herself — the pen pressed hard enough to leave the sentence three sheets deep: Undo it properly or not at all. The same vow, made twice, to a notebook and to a stranger in Tacoma, by a woman twelve days from a staged chair. She kept her voice level. "Did she buy one? An eraser?"

"My bench unit. Reconditioned, rated for exactly her stock. I shipped it on the twenty-fourth." He found the carrier slip without hunting for it. "Delivered the twenty-seventh. The house this time, not the clinic. Signature on file — hers. First and only thing I ever sent her at home."

Elena stepped out into the cold and called Pollard from beside her car, and then the evidence unit, and made them both walk the inventories while she watched the snow fail to land on the wet asphalt. The answer came back twice, identical. There was no bulk eraser logged anywhere in the Mercer house — not the office, not the garage, not the basement. A machine the size of a breadbox, eleven pounds, delivered and signed for eight days before Vivian Mercer died, was not in the house where she had signed for it. The tapes were gone, and so was the means of unmaking them. Whoever had spent that unhurried hour in the sealed office had taken the library and the match.

Back inside, Lind had put two paper cups of coffee on the bench, which was his way of saying he knew she wasn't finished. She asked the question she had been keeping in her coat. "How did she sound? On the sixteenth."

He thought about it with the seriousness he would have given a bad capstan. "Tired. Not sick-tired — I don't know that. Final. Like a woman closing up a summer house for winter, going room to room. She thanked me." He turned his cup a quarter-turn and didn't drink. "Thirty years, and that was the first time she thanked me. You hear a thing like that, in a voice you've serviced that long, and you start keeping the file out."

On the drive up Elena had told herself she would not do the other thing, and standing in the shop she did it anyway, because the wall of answering machines had been speaking to her for an hour. Somewhere behind one of those little plastic doors was somebody's mother saying leave a message after the tone, outliving herself at thirty-nine cents a minute. Elena had a number like that. She had been calling it for six years, from parking lots and stairwells and once from the morgue loading bay, never at home, never where it could become a habit with a witness. Mara's old carrier had recycled the number twice; some stranger in Bremerton had it now, and still, twice a year, Elena dialed it the way you press a bruise — not to hear her sister, who was not there, but to hear the proof that nothing is held. Vivian Mercer had understood something Elena hated her for understanding: voices don't keep unless somebody chooses to keep them. Somebody had chosen, ten thousand times. Elena had a photocopy and a wrong number.

She was zipping her coat to leave when she asked the routine question, the one you ask the way you check a door you know is locked. "The account's closed now, of course. The cancellation stood."

Oskar Lind became very still, and the stillness was the answer arriving ahead of the words. "I need to show you something," he said. "And I need you to tell me I didn't do anything wrong."

He brought out the order book first, the old kind, carbons, and then an envelope, and from the envelope a single card, typed — not printed, typed, the letters struck unevenly the way only a machine with thirty years of one person's force behind it strikes — and Elena read it through twice before she let herself understand it.

RESUME THE STANDING ORDER. SHIP AS ALWAYS. — V.M.

"No call," Lind said. "No signature, just the initials, but the account number was right, and the form of it — that's how she did her renewals in the old days, before I knew her voice. A card in the mail. I thought —" He stopped and started again, honestly. "I thought the practice was carrying on her files. Clinics do. The paperwork outlives everybody, that's the whole point of a clinic."

"When," Elena said. It did not come out as a question.

He turned the envelope over and laid it beside the card, and the postmark was Hartwell Bay, and the date was the fifth of November. Vivian Mercer had died in the first minutes of the third. The card resuming her tape order had been typed and mailed while the fingerprint dust was still settling on her banister — two days after her death, in her own format, over her own initials, by a hand that knew her account number, her habits, and the address where records live.

"And you shipped," Elena said.

"Monday." His voice had gone small in his big chest. "Sixty tapes. East wing receiving, hold for V.M. Same as thirty years." He looked at her across the bench, an honest craftsman standing at the exact edge of something he could feel and not see. "Detective. Who's still recording?"

Elena looked past him, north, as if the wall of dead machines were a window and the window gave onto the water and the one gold light across it. She had spent twelve days learning what the lantern was fed with. Somebody across that water had run out of tape, and ordered more.
