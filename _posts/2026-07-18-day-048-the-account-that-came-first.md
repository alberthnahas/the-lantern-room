The property room at 6:50 a.m. smelled the way it always did in hard cold — like cardboard and gun oil and the particular mineral smell of the building's ancient radiators fighting a losing war against a night that had dropped, according to the dash display on Elena's car, to eleven degrees. She signed the glass lozenge out of overnight holding with her own initials next to Adeyemi's, watched the clerk seal it into a padded mailer bound for the state crime lab's materials unit, and did not let herself think the two words she had refused to write in her report twenty-four hours ago. She had learned that lesson. She had learned it in four words, texted to a phone she'd been holding in her own hand at the time: *Tell the tech to log it as beach glass. It isn't.*

She had not slept so much as lain in the dark rehearsing the tide pool. The wind off the strait. Adeyemi twenty feet up the rock apron, photographing the third set of prints where they stopped short of the water. Her own gloved fingers closing around something small and cold that did not belong among the true sea glass scattered along that shoreline — too glossy, too round, a ghost of a thumbprint pressed into it while it was still soft. She had thought, and only thought, *I'll log this as beach glass until the lab says otherwise.* She had not said it. She was almost sure she had not said it.

Almost.

That was the word that had kept her awake. Not *sure*. *Almost.*

She carried a paper cup of coffee she wasn't drinking down to the digital forensics bay, a windowless room two floors below the bullpen where a technician named Marcus Choi kept eleven monitors and the department's only working understanding of how modern phones actually behaved when nobody was looking at them. Choi was younger than her by a decade, wore a cardigan over his uniform shirt because the servers ran the room too cold, and had the specific, unhurried patience of a man who had long ago stopped being surprised by what detectives brought him.

"I need my own phone looked at," Elena said, and set it on the desk between them, screen down, like something that might still be listening.

Choi didn't ask why, not at first. He plugged it into a gray box the size of a hardback book and watched a bar creep across his own screen. "Personal or issued?"

"Issued. Two years old. I haven't rooted it, haven't jailbroken it, haven't given it to anyone outside the building."

"Everybody says that." He said it kindly, not as an accusation. "Most of the time it's true. Doesn't matter, though — issued phones get MDM'd, they get pushed updates nobody reads, half the apps on here came preloaded and nobody in this building could tell you what they do." He scrolled. Stopped. Scrolled back. "Huh."

Elena had spent eleven years learning to read the backs of other people's heads for that exact syllable. She didn't say anything. She let him get there on his own, the way she'd let Osei get there on the autopsy table, the way she'd let Whit get there over the key receipt. People told you more when they thought they were discovering it instead of confessing it.

"You've got a voice-memo utility running in the background," Choi said. "Not the stock recorder — this is a dictation-and-transcribe app, the kind that turns speech into searchable text automatically. It's not unusual for these to ship preinstalled and disabled. This one's not disabled."

"How long has it been active?"

He pulled a log. The cursor blinked over a column of timestamps going back weeks — small, ordinary-looking entries, none longer than a few seconds, none she remembered ever choosing to make. She made herself read them instead of asking him to summarize. Nov 30, 11:14 p.m. Dec 2, 4:51 a.m. Dec 3, 6:09 p.m. — the six o'clock presser, she realized, her own voice caught murmuring something to herself in a crowd of reporters she'd assumed was too loud to hear anything at all. And at the bottom, newest, unmistakable: Dec 6, 8:41 a.m. Eleven seconds long.

"Play it," she said.

Choi hesitated, the first hesitation he'd shown all morning, then tapped the file. Her own voice came out of the little desk speaker, flattened and tinny and unmistakably her own, saying four words she had been certain, absolutely certain, she had only thought: *log it as beach glass.*

She sat with that for a moment. Not surprise, exactly. Something colder and more specific than surprise — the particular vertigo of learning that a private room in your own skull had a window in it, and that the window had been there long enough that you'd stopped noticing the draft.

"I talk to myself at scenes," she said finally, because it was true and because saying it aloud in this cold gray room felt like handing Choi a piece of herself she hadn't meant to give him. "Cataloguing things. Under my breath. It's — " She stopped, started again, chose the honest version instead of the professional one. "I started it the year my sister died. Working alone at night. It kept the quiet from being total." She had never told Adeyemi that. She had never told anyone that. She had told a phone, apparently, for six years, at scenes and in cars and standing over bodies, and had believed the only audience was the dark.

"Whoever built this app didn't build it to spy on you specifically," Choi said, careful now, watching her face the way people had started watching her face lately. "These things exist for people who want hands-free notes. The setting's probably three taps deep in a menu nobody reads."

"Then how does the audio leave this phone." It wasn't a question by the time it came out of her.

He turned back to the screen, and this time he didn't have an easy answer ready, which told her more than the log had. "It's set to sync. Cloud account, auto-upload, standard stuff — except the account it's syncing to isn't yours. Isn't tied to your department email, isn't tied to any personal address I can find on file. It's a bare username. No display name, no photo, no linked recovery number that resolves to anything." He clicked through three more screens, and his patience visibly thinned into something closer to unease. "I can pull the account creation date, though. That much the metadata keeps even if everything else about it is scrubbed clean."

"Pull it."

He did. Elena watched the date resolve on the screen and made him say it out loud anyway, because some part of her needed to hear it in a human voice rather than read it alone.

"October 21st," Choi said. "Two weeks before Dr. Mercer died."

The coffee had gone cold in her hand. She did the arithmetic she didn't want to do and it came out the same way twice. October 21st was nine days before Vivian Mercer's body was found sitting upright behind a locked door. It was before the case existed. It was before Elena had gone to her captain and asked, quietly, to be the one who caught it if the call ever came — a request she had made off the record, on a Tuesday, to a man who owed her a favor and had never written it down.

"That's not possible," she said, not because she doubted the timestamp but because she needed to hear herself argue with it. "I wasn't assigned this case until the morning after. Nobody could have known I'd be the one standing in that office."

"I'm not going to tell you what it means," Choi said. "I can tell you what the log says. The account existed nine days before the date you've got listed as day one of this case, and it's been quietly collecting audio off your government-issued phone every day since, filtered — near as I can tell — for anything the app's speech model flagged as containing a proper noun or what it read as 'notable content.' It's not recording everything. It's recording the parts a person would want."

Elena thought about eleven years of pattern recognition, about a lifetime of believing that if she was careful enough, thorough enough, alone enough, she could keep the things that mattered to her private by simple force of discipline — and understood, sitting in a cold room two floors under the bullpen with a paper cup going lukewarm in her hand, that she had never once been the only person listening to herself think.

"Can you trace who set it up?"

"I can try. It'll take days, maybe longer, and whoever built this wasn't sloppy — the account creation routed through a chain of relays that'll cost the department money to unwind, if it unwinds at all." He looked at her directly for the first time since she'd sat down. "Detective. Whatever this is, it predates the case by nine days. I don't know what that means for you. But I know it's not supposed to be possible, and I know I'm required to log this as a potential evidence-tampering and officer-safety issue the second you walk out that door, which means Sandoval's going to have it by lunch whether either of us wants that or not."

"Log it," Elena said. She meant it. She had spent six weeks keeping too many things off paper, and paper, this once, was the only thing that might actually protect her.

She left the forensics bay holding her phone the way she might have held something that had bitten her once already — not afraid of it exactly, but aware, now, of exactly where its teeth were. In the stairwell she stopped, the fluorescent light buzzing overhead, and did the thing she always did on stairwells alone: pulled up her contacts, scrolled to the number she had never deleted and never would, and let her thumb hover over Mara's name without pressing call. Six years of unanswered voicemails to a disconnected line, a private ritual she had believed was the one door in her life nobody else could stand behind and listen through.

She thought, and this time made herself say it out loud, quietly, testing the room: "I don't know who's on the other end of this anymore."

Nothing answered. No text arrived. She told herself that was because the phone in her hand had just been unplugged from its silent audience for the first time in six weeks — Choi had disabled the sync before she left the bay — and not because anyone had heard her say it and simply chosen, this once, not to answer back.

Her radio crackled before she reached the bullpen. Adeyemi's voice, tight in a way she'd learned to read as fast as any face. "You need to see this before you sit down. The guy I had watching Okafor's block called it in twenty minutes ago. Same sedan. This time it didn't leave."

Elena was already moving toward the stairs down to the garage, phone dead in her pocket for the first time in six weeks, when she understood the shape of what Choi's timeline meant and what it had always meant, underneath every kind, generous, perfectly-timed thing anyone had ever offered to do for her since the morning she walked into Vivian Mercer's locked office and felt, for one unexamined second, chosen.

She had thought, for six weeks, that she was the one hunting whoever built the Lantern Room.

Nine days before it began, someone had already known exactly which detective would be standing in that doorway — and had started listening to her long before she ever had anything worth overhearing.
