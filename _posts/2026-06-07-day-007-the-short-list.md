The photograph of the arm was still open on her phone when Elena reached her car, and she did not close it. She sat in the driver's seat with the cold of the harbor still on her coat and the engine off and looked at the small clean stipple near the elbow until the screen dimmed itself and went black, and then she woke it and looked again. Not because she expected the image to change. Because she had learned, over the years, that the thing she most wanted to look away from was usually the thing standing nearest the answer, and the discipline of the work was to keep her eyes on it past the point of comfort.

The point was not that the watcher had a photograph of Vivian's arm. The point was when. She had walked in the loading bay at a quarter to eight. She had been on the ramp, leaving, by perhaps eight-thirty. Inside that forty-five minutes, in a sealed white room with no window and one door, Osei had drawn back a sheet and shown her a mark he had told no one about — a mark he intended to write down in the flattest possible language precisely so that no one could lean on it. And before she had reached her car, someone outside that room had sent the same mark back to her.

She made herself say the cold version of it, the version with no comfort in it. Either the watcher had been in the room. Or the watcher had a line into the room so fast and so fine that it was, for practical purposes, the same thing.

Elena started the car for the heat and not the motion. The first real cold had hardened overnight into something with edges; the windshield had fogged from her breathing, and she watched the gray bleed off the glass in a slow tide and thought, against every instinct she had spent three days disciplining, about the people who had stood near the body.

She did not want a suspect. A suspect was a place the mind went to rest, and she had watched too many cases die in that warm bed. What she wanted was a list — not of the guilty, but of the present. Who had been close enough to Vivian Mercer, dead, to know the shape of a puncture no one had photographed for the file. She took out her notebook, the paper one, because a list typed into a phone was a list someone could ask for, and she began.

Osei. She wrote his name first because honesty demanded it and crossed it out almost in the same motion, not because she trusted him — she had learned to mistrust the people she trusted most, it was a tax the job levied — but because a man who spent two nights making certain he would only say what he could prove did not then text the unprovable to the very detective he was protecting. The logic of it was wrong for him. Still, she left the strikethrough light enough to read. A crossed-out name was not a cleared one.

The morgue technician who had assisted on the post. She did not know his name yet and wrote a blank line for it, a thing to fill.

Whoever transcribed Osei's preliminary notes, if he had dictated any. Whoever had access to the morgue's intake system, if the puncture had been logged before Osei decided how to phrase it. Whoever cleaned the room. Whoever moved the body in and would move it out.

And then, because the list was supposed to be honest and not comfortable, she wrote two more lines.

Adeyemi, who had told her to come in the back. Who had known she would be there, in that room, at that hour, looking at that arm.

Sandoval, who was asking how Elena had caught the case — which meant Sandoval was already reading the edges of it, already pulling threads, already in some sense inside the investigation looking out.

She sat with the list. It was a bad list, which was the only kind worth making this early; a good list this early meant you had already decided. The trouble with the leak was not that it pointed at any one of these people. The trouble was the opposite — it pointed at a property they all shared, which was access, and access was exactly the thing the locked office had been telling her about since the first morning. A bolted door, a wiped thumb-turn, a key signed out under a name no one could read, six days before the death, by someone with their own way into the building after hours. A killer who had not broken in but had been let in — by a system, by a habit, by a set of keys that lived in a steel cabinet on a chain. And now a finding that had escaped a sealed morgue almost before it had been spoken aloud. Both of them were the same problem wearing different clothes. The danger was not coming from outside the walls. It was coming from people who were supposed to be standing guard at them.

Her phone buzzed and she flinched, which she hated, and it was Adeyemi.

You see it?

She typed: Saw it. Then she looked at the word and at his name above it and made herself include him in the cold version too, the way she had on paper, and she added nothing else. He would read the brevity as the morgue having shaken her, which was true, and not as suspicion, which was also, faintly, becoming true, and she disliked herself for letting both be true at once. But the discipline of the work did not let her choose the comfortable one.

The phone buzzed again. Sandoval wants you at eleven. I told her you were following up on the autopsy. Don't make me a liar before lunch.

She read it twice. There was a grain of relief in it — Adeyemi was still standing between her and IA, still spending a little of his own credit to buy her time — and the relief itself made her wary, because if she were building a case for how a person could be steered, the first brick would be a friend who kept opening doors for you at exactly the moment you most needed them opened, and never asked what you were carrying through. She thought of Reyes, who had pre-pulled every record before she could ask. She thought of Adeyemi, who had told her to come in the back. She did not believe Adeyemi was Reyes. But she noticed that her mind had set them side by side without being asked to, and she noticed that she could no longer tell whether that was paranoia or pattern, and the inability to tell was, she suspected, exactly the condition someone had wanted her in.

She drove to the General's main entrance, the front one, and went in through the doors she'd skipped at dawn, and asked the desk for the morgue's logbook — the visitor log, the chain-of-custody sheets, anything with names and times. Not because she expected to find the leak in it. Because she expected not to. And an absence, properly read, was a fact like any other.

The clerk was a young man with the particular patience of someone who dealt all day with the bereaved and the official, and he found her the chain-of-custody sheet for case 11-0419 — Mercer, V. — without comment. Elena read it standing at the counter. The body had been received the morning of the death. Osei had signed it in. There were the expected entries: the post itself, the photography, the standard tox draw sent to the lab. And there, two lines from the bottom, in the time column, an entry that did not belong.

A second specimen draw. Logged at 6:14 the previous evening — Saturday — hours before Osei had shown her the arm, hours before Osei had, by his own account, decided how to phrase what he'd found. Drawn by, the column said, and the name in it was not Osei's. It was a set of initials, printed small and square and very clean. A.R.

Elena stood very still. The foghorn was too far to hear from inside, but she heard it anyway, the way you hear a clock in a room after someone points it out.

"This draw," she said. "The six-fourteen. Do you have a name for these initials? It's not the pathologist's hand."

The clerk turned the sheet toward himself and frowned at it the way people frown at things that have always been there and have never once been looked at. "That'd be a referring physician or a consulting one," he said. "Sometimes a family's doctor asks for a sample, for the estate, insurance, that kind of thing. They sign it out, it's all aboveboard if it's on the sheet." He tapped the initials. "I don't know whose those are. But it's logged, so somebody let them."

Somebody let them. The same two words the cabinet had handed her, the same shape the whole case kept taking — not a wall breached but a wall opened, from inside, by someone with the standing to open it and the care to sign their initials small. A consulting physician. A referring one. A man with the right to ask a morgue for a vial of a dead woman's blood and the credentials to be handed it without a question, the evening before her pathologist had even decided what her body meant.

She thought of Osei's voice in the white room. A draw, not a push. Something taken out of her. She had assumed the draw was the killer's, done in the sealed office, secret and unrecorded. But here was a draw that was recorded — official, initialed, aboveboard — taken not in the locked room but in the morgue, the night before she'd seen the arm. Two draws, perhaps. One the killer hid. One a doctor signed for in plain sight. Or one draw she was reading as two because she wanted the case to be tidier than it was. She wrote A.R. — 6:14 Sat in her notebook and underlined it once and did not let herself underline it twice.

"Can I get a copy of this sheet?"

"You can get the original requested through your sergeant," the clerk said, not unkindly, "and a copy right now." He fed it to the machine behind him, and the machine woke with its small mechanical sigh, and Elena watched the light crawl under the glass and thought about how a thing taken out of Vivian Mercer the night before the report was filed could be the most innocent transaction in this building or the most damning, and how the only difference between those two was a name she did not yet have to attach to two clean initials.

Her phone buzzed a third time. She did not flinch now; she had used up the flinch. She expected Adeyemi, or Sandoval moving the eleven o'clock earlier, and she let herself expect it, which was its own small comfort.

It was the clinic exchange. No image this time. Two words, in the same flat thread that had sent her own window back to her in the dark.

How's Osei.

Not a question. There was no mark at the end of it. It sat on the screen with the punctuation of a statement, the way you'd say a man's name to remind someone you knew where he lived — and Elena understood, standing at the counter with the warm copy of the chain-of-custody sheet sliding out into her hand, that she had spent the last hour making a list of who could have leaked the puncture, and that she had left a name off it not because it was innocent but because she had been too careful with her own to see it. The watcher had not breached the sealed room. The watcher had simply, somewhere in this chain of careful, credentialed, aboveboard people, been handed the door.

And now the watcher was telling her, without a question mark, that the next person standing too close to the body might be the one who'd shown it to her.
