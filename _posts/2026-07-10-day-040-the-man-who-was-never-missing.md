The corridor took Elena's light and gave almost nothing back — poured concrete overhead, no drop ceiling to hide a run of dead bulbs behind, which meant the dark down here was not neglect but design, someone's decision made years before anyone alive had a reason to walk this far. She kept her beam low and moving, the way she'd have swept a room with a weapon in it, and behind her Adeyemi's voice ticked off doors the way he'd promised himself he would. "One. Two." Steel, unmarked, padlocked from outside on the first two, the hasps furred with the kind of dust that meant nobody had opened them since before either lock was rated for anything worth stealing.

The third door stood open two inches, and a thread of ordinary electric light lay across the concrete floor in front of it, pale and steady, nothing like the cold unsteady light Voss had described moving room to room the night Vivian died. Elena stopped Adeyemi with two fingers against his sleeve — not a warning, just a beat, the kind of half-second she'd learned over five weeks to spend before a door that had clearly been left open for someone to find — and then she pushed it the rest of the way with the toe of her boot.

The room was small, a mechanical closet built around a boiler that hadn't run in years, pipes lagged in ancient asbestos-colored cloth, a workbench along one wall stacked with the ordinary debris of a building's plumbing history. None of it mattered. What mattered was the folding chair someone had carried in and set facing the door, and the man seated in it, upright, hands folded in his lap one over the other with a care that no living person achieves by accident, chin dropped toward his chest as if he had simply grown tired partway through a long wait and let his head go.

She knew him before she was close enough to be sure, the way you know a name you've been carrying for nine days before the face under the hood finishes registering — the thin shoulders, the two years of watching worn into the set of them, the shelf of dated notebooks she'd carried out of a boardinghouse in her own two arms. Daniel Voss. Person of interest. Left voluntarily, whereabouts unknown.

"Don't touch anything," Adeyemi said, already keying his radio, already giving the sub-level's floor and a room number that didn't exist to a dispatcher who'd have to invent one. Elena heard him say the word deceased and heard how flat and careful he made it, the register he used for things that needed saying exactly once, correctly, so nobody downstream could argue with the transcript later.

She crouched at a distance that let her see without touching, the flashlight held off to the side so it wouldn't wash out what color there was left to read. No blood. No obvious mark of struggle, no bruising at the wrists to suggest he'd fought the strap Kessler described, no scuffing on the concrete that argued for anyone dragged rather than walked or carried. He looked, God help her, peaceful, the particular staged peace she had learned six weeks ago in a high-backed desk chair in a house on the Heights — a body arranged by hands that understood exactly what an untrained eye would read as rest, and exactly how long they had to get it right before the eye stopped being untrained.

His left sleeve had been pushed up two inches past the wrist, precisely, the way a nurse folds a cuff before a draw. In the crook of his elbow, small and clean and already going the pale violet of old bruising rather than fresh, sat a single puncture mark she did not need Osei to describe to her, because she had stood in a cold morgue six weeks ago and had it described to her once already, in words she had never let herself forget: expertly done, no flinch-bruise, nothing nameable in the blood. She did not say the word she was thinking. She photographed it from three angles instead, the way you build a fact instead of feeling one.

"Same," Adeyemi said quietly, close behind her, and she understood he'd made the same walk backward through six weeks that she had, and hadn't needed her to say it either.

It was the folded hands that stopped her longest. Between them, held the way a man might hold a hymnal at a funeral he'd agreed to attend, was a single sheet of paper, creased into quarters and opened flat again, the folds gone soft with handling. Elena recognized the handwriting before she recognized the words — fast, urgent, written from memory rather than copied, the particular slant of a man writing something down because he trusted no shelf to keep it for him. She had photographed this exact hand once already, ten days ago, hunched over an envelope addressed For the detective, if she comes, and then logged its twin — the original page, the one surgically excised from his notebook by an unidentified visitor who'd thanked him first for keeping such good records — as missing, presumed destroyed, its absence itself entered into evidence under her own name at 9:14 that Wednesday morning.

It was not missing. It was here. Someone had kept it for nine days and brought it back to him to die with.

The first two-thirds of the page were exactly what she expected, because she had read the hand-copied duplicate so many times she could have recited it: the window dark early, the unfamiliar cold unsteady light moving room to room for ten minutes, then true blackout, no hall nightlight, the tip line that never called back. But the last four lines were not the ones she knew. They sat lower on the page, in a hand that wanted very badly to be the same hand and was, to a glance, close enough to fool a tired eye at two in the morning — the same forward slant, the same nervous pressure — but wrong in the small places a duplicate always is wrong: the crossbar of a t sitting a fraction too level, a d that closed its loop where Voss's never did, closed early on every other page she'd handled and open here just once, at the hinge of the lie.

I watched that window every night for two years because I couldn't stop, the false lines read. I told myself I was keeping watch. I think I was only keeping myself from having to decide what I'd seen. I can't carry it any further than this room.

No signature. It didn't need one. Set beneath his own true testimony, in a hand built to pass for his, it read exactly like what it was built to be read as: a frightened, guilt-soaked man finally breaking under the weight of his own eyes, choosing a mechanical closet under the building he couldn't stop watching to put himself down. It would hold for the department. It would hold for the six o'clock cameras that had already spent his photograph once this week. It would very nearly have held for her, if she had not spent a month learning this exact hand letter by letter, first out of grief for a stranger's discipline and then out of something closer to love for a witness she'd failed to keep safe.

"He didn't write the last part," she said, and heard her own voice come out steadier than she'd earned. "Someone finished it for him."

Adeyemi looked at her, then at the page, then crouched to read it himself without touching, the way she had. "You're sure."

"I logged his real hand into evidence nine days ago under both our names," she said. "I've read it more times than I've read my own. This isn't him past the halfway fold. It's someone who studied him." She thought, distantly, of Marguerite reading pressure and slant off a nineteen-year-old logbook and calling it a true signature made certain no one could read it — the opposite discipline, here, a false signature made certain everyone would. "Whoever did this kept the real page for nine days. They didn't destroy it. They finished it."

Behind them, in the doorway, Reyes had not stayed with Kessler after all — she registered him there without turning, the particular quality of a presence that had learned to arrive at the edge of a scene rather than its center — and for a long moment nobody told him to leave, because for a long moment nobody in that room said anything at all. Elena watched him take in the chair, the folded hands, the page, with the same fluent concern he brought to everything, the correct order of things crossing his face: shock, then grief, then the beginning shape of an offer to help. But it arrived late. Not by much — a half-second, maybe less, the kind of gap she would once have forgiven as a man needing an extra breath in a room with a body in it — except she had spent five weeks learning to time him, and this was the second time in four days his face had needed a beat it had never needed before to catch up to what his eyes had already understood.

He didn't offer to pull footage this time. He didn't say a word about protocols, or keys, or what he could have overridden by morning. He looked at the folded page in Voss's hands the way a man looks at something he recognizes and cannot, in front of two detectives and a body, afford to recognize out loud, and Elena filed the silence itself the way she'd filed every other true thing she couldn't yet spend — a fact, not a feeling, small enough to carry, too small on its own to build a case anyone else would believe.

The tech who finally came down the service elevator with a case slung over one shoulder took one look at the joint stiffness in Voss's folded hands, the settled color along the backs of them, and said, low, mostly to Adeyemi, that whatever had happened in this room had happened hours ago, not minutes — long enough that the "protocol transfer" Kessler watched go past her with one hand hanging loose from its strap could not have been a living man walking toward his own death. It had been a dead one, already staged, already finished, being moved from wherever the actual act had occurred to the room someone had chosen for him to be found in.

Which meant the six o'clock presser, the stale license photo, left his residence voluntarily, whereabouts unknown — all of it, the whole apparatus the city had built around a frightened man with a shelf of notebooks — had been describing, for two full days, someone who was never missing at all. He had not run. He had not hidden. He had been exactly where his own testimony said the truth would eventually be found, waiting in the dark the way he'd waited at his window every night for two years, and the entire manhunt the department had spent its Wednesday building had been chasing the shape of a man who could not, by then, have been anywhere.

Elena stood in the mechanical closet's flat electric light with the near-match of a dead man's handwriting still open in her mind's eye, four lines built to pass and not quite passing, and understood with a coldness that had nothing to do with the sub-level's breathing chill that whoever had finished Daniel Voss's sentence for him had made, for the first time in six weeks, something very close to a mistake — a small wrongness in a t and a d that a tired detective on any other case would never have caught. She did not believe, standing there, that it had been left for anyone else to find.
