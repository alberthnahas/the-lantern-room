The frost on the Mercer lawn had not melted by ten in the morning, and Elena understood that it was not going to. The season had stopped pretending. Up here on the Heights the cold came off the strait with nothing to slow it, and the grass had gone the color of old nickel, each blade furred white and holding its shape, so that her footprints stayed behind her all the way from the gate like a record of exactly where she had been. She was done resenting records. She had spent ten days learning that somebody kept better ones than she did.

Officer Pollard had the scene log on a clipboard in his cruiser with the heater running, and he climbed out to meet her with the apologetic stiffness of a man who had been guarding an empty house for a week and a half. She signed her name and badge number in the borrower's column, legibly, pressing hard, and did not smile at her own joke because it wasn't one.

"Anybody been in since the techs finished?" she asked.

"Husband, Tuesday, with an escort. Took mail and a coat. That's it." Pollard hesitated. "He didn't go upstairs."

The house had the particular silence of a place that had been processed. Fingerprint dust still grayed the stair rail in patches, like the banister had aged in streaks. The side window Gerald had broken was sheeted in plywood, and the granite frog sat on the hall table where someone had bagged it, photographed it, and put it back, because it had stopped mattering. Elena stood in the hall a moment and let the cold of the house settle on her. Then she went up to the office.

The deadbolt hung open now, the door wedged wide, and the room was exactly as the last tech had left it: the high-backed chair pushed out at its wrong, deliberate distance, the desk clean, the laptop gone to evidence. She did not look long at the chair. She had not come for what was in the room. She had come, this time, for what wasn't.

The killer had owned a key to this room for two years. Osei's lividity band said they had spent the better part of an hour in here with Vivian Mercer's body lying flat on the floor, unhurried, private, while the house stood empty and the bay made its noises. You did not need an hour to pose a dead woman in a chair. You needed an hour to look for something. So the question was no longer how the room had been sealed. The question was what had left it.

She started with the shelves and went slowly, reading absence the way Marguerite had taught her without meaning to — stop staring at the thing everyone stares at; look at everything else. The casebooks were ranked and dustless. The lower cabinet held journals, offprints, a dead woman's professional vanity in matched spines. It was the desk that finally answered her. The wide right-hand drawer had a felt organizer tray, and in the felt was a clean rectangular shadow, three inches by five, where something had sat long enough to keep the dust off its own footprint and was not there anymore. Beside the shadow, pushed to the back, was a charging cradle with its cable still dressed neatly through the grommet hole, and the cradle's molded plastic bed was shaped for something small and oblong that was also not there.

Elena crouched and looked at the cradle for a long time and then she heard, very clearly, in memory, the dry little hiss that ran under the suicide note in her mind whenever she reread it. She went through the other drawers with her gloves on. In the shallow center drawer, under a box of fountain-pen cartridges, she found it: a microcassette recorder, old, German, the chrome worn to brass at the corners from decades of one thumb. Its tape door stood open. It was empty.

The recorder had been missed, or dismissed — logged, no doubt, on some inventory line as office equipment. But the cradle in the right-hand drawer was a second cradle. A woman who keeps two charging beds keeps two machines: one for the desk and one for the pocket. Elena thought of the cardigan Vivian had died in, its pockets holding house key, car key, two clinic keys, and nothing else, and she felt the shape of the missing thing as plainly as if her own hand were closing on it. Vivian Mercer had carried a recorder the way other people carried phones. And the room had been emptied of everything that machine had ever said.

"She talked to it more than she talked to me."

Gerald Mercer stood in the office doorway with his escort hanging back on the landing. He had a cardboard box under one arm, half filled with the kind of things estates are made of — a checkbook, a folder, a scarf — and the healing cut across his right palm had gone the dull rose of a wound that would scar. He was looking at the recorder in her gloved hand, and his face had the punched-soft expression of a man seeing an old enemy at a funeral.

"Thirty years," he said. "Hotel rooms, the car, the bath, two in the morning. You'd hear her through the door, that low voice, like a radio left on in another country. Sessions, notes, letters she never sent. She bought tapes by the case from a man in Tacoma because nobody makes them anymore." He set the box down on the floor as if it had gotten heavy. "I used to think, if that thing could be subpoenaed, half this city would leave it."

"Where are they, Gerald?" Elena said. "The tapes. Thirty years of them."

"Not here." He said it simply, with the authority of a man who had searched a marriage. "I asked her once. She said keeping them at home was sentimental, and sentiment was a security flaw. She said records live where records live." He shrugged, a small, defeated movement. "I assumed the clinic. There's a room there she kept locked that even the cleaners — " He stopped himself, hearing it, and his mouth twisted. "Listen to me. I'm doing your job for the woman I broke a window for."

Elena let the silence stand exactly as long as it wanted to and then she asked him the other question, the one she had carried up the hill. "The settlement. Your debt. She agreed in October, papers next week. Why then? Why at all?"

"You think I didn't ask?" His laugh had no floor under it. "Twenty-six years she watched me drown in increments and called it a learning environment. Then the first week of October she calls me, herself, no lawyer, and says: I'm putting things in order, Gerald, and you're one of the things." He looked at the frost-white window. "I should have heard it. Putting things in order. People say that twice in their lives and the second time there's a will involved."

"And the money itself. Was it there? Could she cover it?"

Something moved behind his face, reluctance and arithmetic together. "That's the thing I'd have told you days ago if you'd asked me like a person instead of a suspect," he said. "Vivian was liquid her whole life. But the last year, year and a half — she was bleeding cash. Withdrawals, monthly, sometimes twice. I still had eyes on the joint accounts even after I moved out; the bank never unlearned my name. Eight thousand here. Five there. Cash, Detective. A woman who billed four hundred an hour and documented her own breathing, taking out grocery money in envelopes." He picked his box back up, hugging it. "I assumed a lawyer I wasn't supposed to know about. Or somebody she was paying to stay quiet, or stay away. I never asked, because by then we only spoke through the failure. And now she's dead and the papers never got signed, so the debt's still mine, the house goes to probate, and your colleagues look at me like the window I broke was a door I locked." His voice cracked, one clean line, like the granite frog going through glass. "She died a week before she would have saved me. Whoever did it cost me the only rescue I had coming. Put that in your motive."

He carried his box down the stairs, and Elena stood in the office of the most documented woman in Hartwell Bay and understood that she was standing inside an erased tape.

She found the notebooks twenty minutes later, because Gerald's bath-and-hotel-rooms litany had told her what to look for: not the archive, which was wherever records lived, but the pocket stuff, the working margin of a mind that wrote everything down. They were in the credenza, a brick of identical black notebooks rubber-banded by year, and the techs had photographed and left them because they were, on their face, appointment books. The current one had an elastic strap and a pen loop with no pen. Elena turned its pages standing up, in her coat, her breath showing.

It was shorthand, but it was a doctor's own shorthand, half legible the way Vivian's bold hand was always legible: times, initials, fragments. And down the margins, at intervals, a notation that was not an appointment. A letter and a number. L-7. L-12. L-3. Never a name beside them, never a time — they sat in the margins next to ordinary days like buoys marking something under the water. The same code, she would have bet her badge, that thirty years of pocket tapes wore on their labels in place of the names of the people on them. A record kept by a woman who believed records were dangerous: everything preserved, nothing said. A ledger written so that only the woman who kept it could read the accounts.

On the page for the last full week of October, under a Thursday, Vivian had written four words in full, no shorthand, pressing hard enough to emboss the next three pages, and Elena read them with the cold of the house climbing into her chest: "The lantern is lit." And below it, smaller, the pen run almost dry: "Undo it properly or not at all."

The strap of the notebook had been holding one more thing flat against the back cover. It dropped into her palm: a microcassette, no case, its label written in Vivian's bold hand — no name, no code even, only a date. October 22. Less than two weeks before her death. One week before someone signed her office key out of a steel cabinet on behalf of a woman six years dead.

Elena stood listening to the house not breathe. Then she fitted the tape into the old recorder with her gloved thumbs, closed the worn door, and pressed play.

Hiss. The sea-sound of old tape, the same hiss she had been hearing under this case since the first morning. Then Vivian Mercer's voice, low and level and unhurried, a radio left on in another country, ten days dead and speaking six inches from Elena's hand.

"Thursday. The lantern is lit. It has been lit for a long time, and I have decided that I am going to put it out myself, while the decision is still mine to — "

A sound behind the voice. Two knocks, and a door opening on a room Elena had never seen, and Vivian's voice changing — not afraid, which was somehow worse, but familiar, dry, almost amused, the voice of a woman interrupted by someone who had never once needed permission to come in.

"Well," the dead woman said. "You knock now?"

The tape clicked off.
