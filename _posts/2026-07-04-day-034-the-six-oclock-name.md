The rain had crossed over sometime after three in the morning, and by the time Elena parked behind the precinct the whole lot wore a skin of ice thin enough to see the paint stripes through, the kind that did not announce itself until a boot went sideways on it. She took the steps one at a time with a hand on the rail, breath fogging under the sodium light, and thought, not for the first time this week, that the season had stopped bothering to warn anyone before it changed its mind.

The evidence room was colder than the lot, the particular manufactured cold of climate control set for paper rather than people, and she signed Daniel Voss's notebooks in herself rather than hand them to the property clerk, because some things she was no longer willing to let another set of hands carry even the width of a counter. Two years of dated spiral covers, sun-faded at the edges from two winters on a windowsill, went into the log one at a time — date received, condition, chain of custody — and when she reached the gap where a single page had been razored clean from its spine, she wrote the fact and nothing around the fact: *page corresponding to the night of Nov. 4 excised prior to recovery by RD; cause and actor unknown.* She did not write about the warmth she'd felt under her palm two nights ago, because a detective did not get to log a feeling, only a finding, and the difference between those two things was most of what she had left to hold onto.

Adeyemi found her there with two coffees he'd clearly meant to drink both of himself before he saw her face. "Commissioner's office confirmed it an hour ago," he said, setting one cup down within her reach and not quite meeting her eyes while he said the rest. "Six o'clock presser. They're not calling it an arrest, they're calling it 'a significant development in the investigation into Dr. Mercer's death,' which is city-hall for a name and a photograph and every camera in three counties pointed at a man who has never hurt anybody." He drank from his own cup like it had personally disappointed him. "I bought you until five. That's the whole negotiation I could get. Give me something better than a scared man with a shelf of ruined notebooks, or Voss is the six o'clock name whether either of us likes it or not."

She told him what she had, in the register she'd learned to use for him — provable first, felt second, and the felt part left mostly unsaid. The notebooks, the missing page, a stranger who'd known which night mattered enough to cut it out with something sharper than scissors. Adeyemi listened with the stillness he kept for things he believed and couldn't yet use, and said only, "Write it up clean. I'll walk it upstairs myself," before the radio on his hip called him away toward whatever the morning was already doing to the rest of the building.

She was still logging the last notebook when Lieutenant Sandoval found her, and there was nothing in the woman's approach that asked to be dreaded — no clipboard held like a weapon, no colleague trailing her for witness — just a woman in a gray suit with wet shoulders who leaned against the counter the way you lean against something you expect to be standing at for a while. "I'm not here about the case," Sandoval said, which was, Elena had learned, precisely the sentence IA used when they were. "I'm closing out a thread. Ten days ago Dr. Reyes called my office. Unprompted — nobody asked him anything. He wanted it on record that he considered your proximity to this case 'useful, given her thoroughness,' his words, and that any question about your objectivity ought to be weighed against how much ground you'd covered that a less personally invested detective might have missed." She said it without inflection, a fact being filed rather than a verdict being delivered, and that, Elena thought, was the whole art of the woman — she did not need to sound suspicious to be doing her job.

"That sounds like him being generous," Elena said, and heard how thin it sounded even as she said it.

"It does," Sandoval agreed, mildly, watching her. "I've been doing this eleven years, and in eleven years I have had exactly four people call me to vouch for someone before I'd given any sign I doubted them. Three of the four turned out to be covering for the person they were vouching for. The fourth just genuinely liked his partner." She let that sit, unhurried, the way a fact sits when it doesn't need help. "I'm not telling you Dr. Reyes is one or the other. I'm telling you that a man doesn't rehearse a defense for a doubt nobody's raised yet unless the doubt already lives somewhere in his head, rent-free, and I'd want to know why, if I were you, and I'd want to know it before I let him keep doing anything nice for me." Then, without any change in her voice at all: "Are the two of you close?"

"He's been helpful," Elena said, which was true, and let it stand as the whole answer, which was not.

Sandoval nodded once, unbothered, the reaction of someone who had expected exactly that sentence and had already filed the shape of the silence around it. "Get some sleep sometime this decade, Vega," she said, and left the way she'd arrived, without urgency, which somehow felt worse than if she'd hurried.

Elena sat with it a moment after the door swung shut — the specific, small-town courtesy of a call made before anyone had thought to be suspicious, and Sandoval's plain arithmetic that such courtesy was rarer than kindness and usually meant something had already been gamed out in private. She had learned this same lesson twice now from two different rooms: that care and control were built from the same raw material, sustained and precise attention, and that the only honest way to tell them apart was time, which was the one thing the six o'clock clock had just decided not to give her.

She drove to Harbor Street through rain that had gone hard and granular, more ice than water now, ticking off the hood in a way that made her wipers stutter, and this time she had told Adeyemi exactly where she was going and why, on the log, in her own name — a small, deliberate correction to a month of habits, as if doing the ordinary thing in the ordinary way might buy Voss some of the protection that secrecy plainly hadn't.

The boardinghouse looked the same from the street — driftwood-gray, one window facing the water — but the landlord, a heavy man in a cardigan who Elena had spoken to only once, in passing, met her on the porch before she reached the door, arms crossed against the cold like he'd been waiting for exactly this conversation and dreading it. "He's gone," the man said. "This morning, early. A car came for him — nice one, not a cab. Fella showed some kind of clinic identification, said Danny'd be safer somewhere quiet with the news about to get ugly, paid me two months back rent in cash so there wouldn't be any trouble about the notice I'd given him in October." He rubbed his jaw. "Danny didn't fight it. Went almost easy. Said something like, 'guess it's my turn,' and I didn't think much of it till you're standing here looking like that."

"Did he give a name? The man in the car?"

"Didn't ask. He had a badge-looking thing, clinic seal on it, real polite. You get old in this business, you stop asking men like that for their names." He looked, for the first time, like he understood he'd made a mistake, and like the understanding had arrived a full day too late to be useful to anyone.

She asked to see the room, and he let her up without argument, and the room itself told her the rest before she'd finished crossing it: the shelf where two years of dated spines had stood was bare and freshly dusted, the bed made with a precision Voss himself, by his own account, had never quite managed, the whole space tidied by hands that liked things straightened into a column — the same close, unhurried thoroughness that had left his notebooks flush and one page missing three nights ago. She stood in the middle of it, in the gray water-light off the window he had spent two years watching, and let herself feel, fully, for one held breath, that whoever had walked through this room twice now was not cleaning up after a frightened man's departure. They were closing a file.

It was the mattress that saved her the trip back empty-handed — Voss's own habit, a habit of a man who trusted no shelf and no drawer with the one thing that mattered, tucked under the corner where a hand reaching to make a bed would never think to check unless it already knew there was something there to find. A plain envelope, unsealed, the handwriting on its face uneven and pressed hard enough to have nearly torn through: *For the detective, if she comes.*

Inside was a single page, folded in thirds, written not in the careful architecture of his shelved entries but in a fast, private hand that had clearly been racing something. It was the night of November the fourth, rebuilt from memory rather than copied from any original — the window gone dark early, the ten strange minutes of a colder, unsteadier light moving room to room like a hand carrying something it did not want set down, and then the true dark, the one with no hall nightlight in it at all — every detail matching, word for word almost, what he'd told her the first time, in his own room, before anyone had thought to take a razor to the page that proved it. He had written it twice, she understood, standing there in the ruined tidiness of his room: once for the shelf, where anyone patient enough could find and remove it, and once for no shelf at all, because some part of him had already known the first copy wasn't going to survive.

Beneath the entry, in a different ink, added later, in the loose scrawl of a man writing fast in a bad light, was one more line that had nothing to do with the night of the fourth and everything to do with this one: *If someone comes asking which night mattered most, that's how you'll know I'm not coming back.*

Elena stood a long moment in the gray, ice-veined light with the page in her hand, the clock somewhere below her already sliding past four, and understood that whatever calm, well-credentialed man had driven Daniel Voss away that morning had asked him exactly the wrong question in exactly the right voice — and that she was holding, now, in a dead man's handwriting that did not yet belong to a dead man, the only surviving proof in the world that any of what he'd seen through that window had ever been real.
