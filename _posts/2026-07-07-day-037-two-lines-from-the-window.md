Adeyemi drove the harbor road at half speed with both hands on the wheel, the way he only drove when the asphalt had gone bad, and outside the windshield the ice that had been beading on the glass at the gate had thickened by the time they cleared the Institute's drive into something closer to a skin — the whole road glazed under the headlights, every parked car wearing a shell of it, the streetlights come up in haloed rings through air gone suddenly, perfectly still. Neither of them had spoken since the lobby. It was the particular silence of two people who had watched the same half-second happen and were choosing, separately, what to do with it.

"You're going to tell me you didn't see it," Adeyemi said finally, not looking away from the road.

"I saw it."

"I don't mean the badge. I mean his face." He eased the car around a curve where the ice had drifted into a long silver tongue across both lanes, and did not relax his hands until they were past it. "You asked for that name like you already knew what shape it was going to come in. Letter, dash, number. You didn't even blink when the field came back blank. Most people, that's confusing. For you it was just — confirmed."

Elena watched the black water slide by on her side, the east-wing window still burning behind them in the mirror, smaller now, gold going to amber going to a coin. She had spent five weeks building a discipline out of not saying things, and the discipline had a cost she was only now, at four in the morning with ice on the road and a man she trusted asking her a straight question, starting to feel as a specific kind of tiredness, located somewhere under the sternum. "There's a format," she said. "I've seen it before. A letter and a number, no name attached. It's how somebody in that building refers to people they don't want written down as people."

"Somebody being."

"I don't know yet. That's not me being careful, Ruben. I mean I actually don't know." It was, she thought, the first fully true sentence she'd handed him in a week, and she watched him take it the way he took most things she gave him lately — accepting the shape of it without demanding she cut it open further, which was its own kind of gift, and one she was aware she hadn't earned tonight.

He didn't say anything for a while. The wipers ticked once, twice, clearing a scrim of ice that had no business forming that fast, and the heater fan labored against a cold that had stopped being weather two weeks ago and become, as far as Elena could tell, a permanent fact of the season. "Four years ago," he said, "when I asked for you on the squad, it wasn't your clearance rate. Danvers didn't care about clearance rates, and neither, if I'm honest, did I. It was that you found the thing that didn't fit before anybody else in the room had finished pretending it fit. You've been doing that since day one on this. What you're bad at is the second half — telling anybody once you've found it." He glanced at her, just once, then back at the road. "I've covered for that because I think it's the same instinct running both ways. But Sandoval is going to hear by nine a.m. that we did a warrantless welfare entry into the Mercer Institute on a security guard's forty-yard ID, and she is going to ask me why my detective looked like she recognized a blank field on a screen, and I would like, for once, to be able to tell her something that isn't a guess."

"Tell her I recognized the format from another case," Elena said. "Tell her I'm not sure it means anything yet. Both of those are true."

"Both of those are also nothing," Adeyemi said, but there wasn't heat in it, only the flat exhaustion of a man doing the math on how many more mornings he could keep spending currency he didn't have unlimited amounts of. "I'm not asking because I don't trust you. I'm asking because in about five hours somebody with a badge and a subpoena power is going to ask me, and I am going to have used up every favor I have left just getting us through tonight's door." He let a breath out through his nose, watching the ice-slick road unspool ahead of the headlights. "The thing with Danvers," he said, quieter, "is that I sat on my hands for eleven days because the paperwork said wait, and by the time I stopped waiting there wasn't anybody left to find in time. I don't sit on my hands anymore. But I need you to understand that not sitting on my hands has a bill, and right now you're the only one who knows what's actually on it."

Elena said nothing for the length of a mile. It was, she thought, the closest thing to an ultimatum Adeyemi had ever handed her, delivered so gently it barely registered as one, and that was exactly the kind of man he was — the reader's reality check, not the reader's antagonist, a man who would spend himself down to nothing before he'd spend her, and who deserved, for that, more than a sentence with two true halves and nothing underneath them. "Give me until tonight," she said. "If I still don't have anything by tonight, I'll tell Sandoval everything I have, and you can hand her whatever's left of me."

"That's not a deal, that's a delay."

"It's the only one I've got."

He drove the rest of the way to her building without answering, and when he pulled up to the curb he didn't kill the engine, which she understood as its own kind of statement — a man who was not, tonight, going to sit with her in the quiet the way he sometimes did, because the quiet tonight would have cost something neither of them could spare. "Go find your nothing," he said, not unkindly. "Fast."

Her phone lit up before she'd even shut the car door — not the clinic exchange, not any of the unnumbered channels she'd learned to dread by their silences as much as their words, but Reyes, by name, in her own contacts, at four-forty in the morning. She let it ring twice before she answered, because answering on the first ring would have told him something about how little she'd slept, and she was no longer willing to give him anything for free.

"I know it's early," he said, and he did sound like a man who had been awake working rather than a man who had been woken, which was its own small data point she filed without comment. "I didn't want you to spend the whole night wondering whether I meant it about the file. I've already called the overnight administrator back — she's pulling the sealed-designation records now, second key holder or not, because I decided the privacy protocol can answer to me if anyone objects, and I'd rather answer for that than for making you wait until nine on a man who's already been gone twelve hours." A pause, warm, unhurried, the same register he'd used over a diner cup of bad coffee weeks ago telling her the answer to who you can trust is no one, including me. "You'll have it by seven. I wanted you to be able to sleep for two of those hours instead of waiting on me."

"That's generous," Elena said, and meant it the way she'd started meaning most things she said to him now — true on its face, and true underneath in a different direction entirely.

"I keep telling you. It costs me nothing." He said it exactly the way he'd said it walking her into a lobby six hours earlier, as if he wanted the phrase filed under kindness and knew, better than anyone she'd ever questioned, exactly how a phrase got filed. She thought of a margin note in her dead sister's file — she is ready to be useful — and did not let the thought reach her voice. "Get some rest, Elena. I'll call the second I have it."

She said something that passed for thanks and ended the call standing on ice that hadn't finished forming, watching her breath go up in a column between the streetlight and the black window of her own bedroom, and understood that whatever the sealed file turned out to hold when it finally reached her desk, it would already have passed through his hands first — read, considered, and handed to her exactly as generously as everything else he had ever handed her. She did not go upstairs to wait for it.

She walked the four blocks to the bus stop on Kestrel instead. She walked the four blocks to the bus stop on Kestrel instead, boots finding careful purchase on sidewalk ice that had gone from a nuisance to a genuine hazard sometime in the last hour, the whole city holding its breath under a coat of glass, and she was there, hood up, breath smoking, twenty minutes before Noah Okafor's six-ten bus was due, because she had learned his schedule the way she'd learned everyone's schedule who mattered to this case, without ever writing it down.

He came around the corner at six-oh-four, lab coat visible under his open jacket the way it always was, two sweaters underneath it against a cold his coat wasn't built for, lanyard swinging with its overloaded ring of keys, and he saw her before she said anything, and stopped walking in a way that told her he'd been half expecting, for weeks now, that a version of this morning was coming.

"You look like someone who didn't sleep," he said.

"I need to ask you about the strip. The one you built her. Not the gold one — a different code. L-14."

Something crossed his face, fast, the particular flinch of a man being asked to reach into a drawer he'd been careful not to open in daylight. He didn't ask her where the code came from, which she noted, because it meant he already had some private theory about where such things came from and had decided, like her, not to say it. "L-14," he repeated, and closed his eyes for a second, the way he did when he was running his hands along a shelf he could no longer see, only remember. "I typed that strip three times. She made me. You don't forget a thing you've typed three times." His breath went out white and slow. "Fourteen was two lines above the window."

"Two lines above."

"The gold pane. The one she carried by hand." His eyes came open and found hers, and there was something in them now that hadn't been there a second ago — not fear exactly, but the recognition of fear's shape arriving early. "I remember because I thought it was strange at the time, that the one she cared about most and this other one sat that close together on the page. Like neighbors. I didn't think anything of it then. Codes don't mean neighbors. A list doesn't care who's next to who." He said it like a man trying to talk himself back down off something, and failing. "But you're not asking me because it doesn't mean anything."

Elena thought of a boardinghouse room tidied twice, a mattress with an envelope under it addressed for the detective if she comes, a man led out of his own life that morning almost easy, guess it's my turn, and a badge that had gone through a service gate on a credential that should have died with its own year, and she thought of a woman with a shaking hand crossing out one symbol in front of a twenty-two-year-old assistant and choosing, out of some private arithmetic she never explained, to carry exactly one of them herself, and she understood, standing on ice at ten past six with a bus not yet visible down the long white throat of the street, that whatever the gold pane had been to Vivian Mercer, it had been close enough on the page to reach out and touch the thing that had walked into a building last night and not walked back out.

"Two lines," she said, mostly to herself, watching her own breath disperse into air gone the color of a held-up hand.

Noah didn't answer. Somewhere behind them the ice-locked street let out a sound like a held note breaking, a branch or a wire giving way under its own new weight, and neither of them turned to look for it, both of them staring instead at the space between two remembered lines on a page neither of them had ever been allowed to see, as if staring hard enough could tell them which direction the distance ran — toward Voss, or away from him, or, worse, toward whoever came next.
