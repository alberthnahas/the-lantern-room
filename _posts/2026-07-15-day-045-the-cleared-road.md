By four o'clock the plows had done what they could to the main roads and left the rest to the cold, and from the precinct's third-floor window Elena watched a salt truck grind past twice in twenty minutes, throwing an orange wash over snow already gone the gray of old newspaper. The envelope sat on Adeyemi's desk between them in its paper evidence bag, both their initials dried dark across the sealed flap, and she had looked at it so many times in the last four hours that she'd started making herself look away on a schedule, the way she'd once taught herself not to check a wound.

"Okonkwo says we can't open it on the warrant alone." Adeyemi hung up his desk phone the way he always did when he didn't like what he'd heard, setting it down with more care than it needed. The assistant DA's voice had carried thin and tinny through the earpiece the whole call, competing with what sounded like her own kid's birthday party in the background. "Warrant covered the box, the box's contents, anything reasonably believed to be evidence of the crime charged. A sealed personal letter addressed to the lead detective by name is its own animal. She wants it authenticated before anybody so much as steams a corner, so the defense — whoever's defense, eventually — can't stand up in six months and say the detective wrote herself a love letter from a dead woman."

"She used those words."

"She used gentler words. That's what she meant." He rubbed at his eyes with the heel of one hand. "She's not wrong, Elena. If it's what we think it is, it's the best thing we've got in this whole case, and the surest way to lose it is to open it wrong."

Elena had already known what Okonkwo would say before Adeyemi placed the call; she had spent the drive back from the bank rehearsing the argument against herself and losing. A woman had written her name on an envelope and hidden it behind a wall meant to hide a program instead, and the wanting to know what it said had a physical weight now, low in her chest, the specific ache of a question she'd carried since she was thirty-five and it had only ever gotten heavier. She set it aside anyway, the way she'd learned to set aside the file in the un-oiled drawer, the tape above the strait, every other true thing she couldn't yet afford to spend.

"Renata Wexler," she said instead. "She's already got two exemplars of the obscured hand and one of Voss's forged confession. Give her a clean, undisputed sample of Vivian's real handwriting — the suicide note, the sealed conservatorship filing, anything the estate's already authenticated — and ask her only one question. Does the hand on this envelope match."

"Not what it says. Just whose hand wrote it."

"Just whose hand." It wasn't nothing. If Wexler could put Vivian's name on the outside of that envelope with a document examiner's certainty, it became the first piece of physical evidence in six weeks that didn't fit both doors — a woman's own hand, unforced, unobscured, saying something to Elena on purpose. Not a feeling. A fact, the way the second clause had been a fact, except this one she could hold up in a room with Sandoval in it.

"Okonkwo wants something else too," Adeyemi added, not looking up from the request form. "A representative of the estate present when it's finally opened. Which means Gerald, unless the court appoints someone else, and I don't love the idea of him in the room either, given everything else he's touched in this case." He capped his pen. "She's not saying no. She's saying not yet, not sloppy, not you alone at your kitchen table with a letter opener at eleven at night, which — don't pretend that wasn't the plan forming behind your face just now."

"It wasn't a plan. It was a temptation." Elena allowed herself, for the first time all day, something close to a smile, thin and short-lived. "There's a difference."

"Not much of one, with you." He slid the courier form across for her signature. "Wexler by tonight, results Monday if we're lucky, Tuesday if we're not. The envelope sits in the property room until then, under both our names, and neither of us goes near it alone. Agreed?"

"Agreed." She signed it, and meant it, and still felt the small ordinary theft of having to wait for something that had already waited six weeks to reach her.

Her phone buzzed against the desk before Adeyemi had finished writing the courier request. Reyes. She let it ring out and watched the missed call fold itself into a text a few seconds later, the cursor of it blinking at her like something patient.

*Heard from a friend at the bank you found something of Vivian's. I know her hand better than her own doctors did — twenty-three years of case notes. If it would save you a week with the lab, I'm happy to look. Just the envelope. I wouldn't ask to see inside.*

She read it twice, the way she read all of them now, sorting it into the country of impression and the country of fact, and found it filed itself in both at once, which was, by this point, its own kind of answer. A generous man who happened, every single time, to offer the exact door that would let him closest to the thing she was trying to keep him away from.

"He wants to authenticate it himself," she said, turning the phone so Adeyemi could see.

Adeyemi read it, exhaled through his nose, and said nothing for a moment that told her everything. "Tell him no."

"I already have. In my head. About four times today." She typed instead: *Lab's already on it. Appreciate the offer.* Six words, the kind that gave him nothing to hold and nothing to push against, the way she'd learned to answer all of them lately — a woman keeping her own private ledger of every offer she declined, each one a line item in a column that had no name yet but was getting longer than the one across from it.

The second call came at half past six, while she was still at her desk eating a vending-machine sandwich she didn't taste, and it was Noah.

"They plowed Verbena," he said, without hello, the words coming fast and a little breathless, like he'd been rehearsing them on the walk from wherever he'd been standing. "Not all the way, but the bus is running again as of two hours ago, first time since Thursday. I can move."

"Noah—"

"I've had it on me since Wednesday." His voice dropped, not to a whisper, just lower, the register of a man checking a room without turning his head. "Not hidden somewhere I have to go get it and come back for. On me. I decided after — after what happened to Voss — that I wasn't going to be the kind of careful that leaves a thing sitting in a drawer for somebody else to find first."

She closed her eyes for a second, there at her desk, and thought of Marguerite's blank leave request, of Gerald's cut palm, of every ally this case had touched and then reached for a second time. "Where are you now?"

"My place. I'm not staying here tonight, though. I already told my roommate I'm going to my aunt's in Puyallup for a few days, which isn't a lie, exactly, it's just not tonight." A short, unamused laugh. "My mother used to tell people she was working late when she meant she was working a second job nobody was supposed to know she needed. I used to think that was sad. Turns out it's just useful."

"Noah. Where and when."

A pause long enough that she heard, underneath it, the particular hollow quiet of an empty apartment — no television, no roommate's footsteps, just a young man standing very still in a room he'd already decided to leave. "The old ferry terminal. Same as before. Tomorrow, first light, before anybody's got a reason to be down on that stretch of road." Another pause. "I want to give it to you in a place where, if something happens to me after, at least the thing itself already made it out."

"Nothing is going to happen to you."

"You don't get to promise that," he said, not unkindly, "and I stopped needing you to. I just need you there at first light." A breath. "There's a discrepancy on the second page I don't fully understand yet — something that doesn't match the first strip, a number that repeats where it shouldn't. I want to show you in person, not read it to you over a phone, not after everything else that's come apart over a phone in this case." He said, almost to himself: "My mother cleaned three offices a night for eleven years and never once left a room looking like she'd been in it. I used to think that was the whole lesson. It isn't. Sometimes the lesson is you stop being the one who leaves it clean."

He hung up before she could answer that, and she sat holding the dead phone a moment, the sandwich cooling forgotten beside her keyboard, feeling the particular vertigo of watching someone choose bravery the exact week it had stopped being safe to.

She told Adeyemi across the desk, low, the same pitch she'd used for the terminal's name the first time. He didn't ask her to reconsider it, which she'd half expected; he only asked what she needed. "I'll be there before he is," he said. "Unmarked, my own car, parked where I can see the water door and not be seen from it. You go in the way you went in before. If it's clean, I never step out of the car. If it isn't—" He didn't finish that sentence, and she didn't make him. "I'll tell Sandoval I'm covering a source meeting. I won't tell her whose."

"That's a risk for you."

"Everything in this case is a risk for somebody." He said it without weight, the way a man says a thing he decided a long time ago and stopped relitigating. "I told you back in the ice why I don't wait on paperwork anymore. This is the same math. A witness who's finally decided to be brave is a witness with about a week's shelf life in a case like this one, and I'd rather spend a Sunday morning in a cold car than read about him in Monday's incident log." He looked at her a beat too long, the look he used when he wanted a thing understood without having to say it outright. "Same goes for you, for what it's worth. Don't go in there thinking you're the only one anybody's counting on to come back."

She didn't have an answer for that either, so she didn't try to make one, and let the silence stand as the closest thing to agreement they had time for.

She had told no one the terminal's name aloud. She had said it to Adeyemi across a desk in a squad room with the door shut, in a voice pitched for one listener, and Noah had said it into a phone from an apartment he swore had no wiring in it, the same promise he'd made about the diner three weeks ago, back when that promise had still held.

Her phone buzzed once, gentle as a held breath, forty seconds after the call ended.

*First light. Bring a coat. It's colder down there than people think.*

The number was the sixth — the one that had followed her into the old ferry terminal itself six weeks before this, that had learned, sometime in the last month, to sit inside a room with no camera and no wire and hear exactly what was said in it. She had told herself, standing in that terminal the first time, that it had followed Noah out into the snow, or else it had never left the room at all. She understood now, reading four words meant to sound like kindness, that it had done something worse than either: it had been in the apartment with him tonight, in the tired quiet where a woman's son decided to stop cleaning up after himself, and it had heard him choose the hour and the place before she had, and it had waited, patient as weather, to tell her so only after there was nothing left to do but go.
