The ice on the precinct steps had gone soft and gray by the time Elena took them two at a time, the afternoon's thin sun having done what sun could do to it, though she already knew from the color of the sky over the harbor — a bruised, purpling white, the kind that meant the temperature was about to fall out from under the day rather than climb toward evening — that it would refreeze into something meaner before six. She had the envelope inside her coat, against her chest, the way she had carried nothing since the L-9 tape, and she found the bullpen already half-emptied, everyone who could invent a reason to be elsewhere having invented one, and Adeyemi at his desk with his tie loosened and two phones going cold beside a legal pad covered in his own crossed-out handwriting.

"Tell me you have something better than a duplicate page," he said, not looking up, "because in forty minutes I have to stand next to a man from the commissioner's office while he reads a sentence I already fought him down from twice."

She put the envelope on his desk anyway and told him what it was and where she'd found it and what the room had looked like when she got there, and watched him read it once fast and once slow, the way he read anything that might matter later, and set it down with the particular gentleness of a man who understood exactly how little a duplicate, hand-copied, unwitnessed page of someone else's memory would weigh in a room built for photographs and warrants. "It's good," he said. "It's not enough to stop this. Nothing's enough to stop this — they moved the deadline three times because a woman with a swearing-in in eleven days wanted it stopped moving, and stopped moving means a name gets said whether the man attached to it is standing next to it or not."

"What did you fight them down from twice?"

He turned the legal pad around so she could see it, a line struck through so hard the pen had torn the page: *armed and dangerous, approach with caution.* "There's no report of a weapon. There's no incident. There's a discharged patient who watches a window and writes down what he sees in it, and somebody upstairs wanted the city to be afraid of him because afraid is a faster word than *missing*." He said it flatly, a man reciting a fact he'd already spent his morning being furious about and had no more fury left to spend. "I got it to 'person of interest, sought for questioning in connection with events surrounding Dr. Mercer's death.' That's the whole win. It's not a good win. It's the win I could get without them pulling me off the podium and putting someone up there who'd read the other version." He looked at her a beat longer than the sentence needed. "I want you to understand I'm not proud of that number. I'm telling you what forty minutes bought."

She thought, not for the first time, that she had spent four years assuming Adeyemi's steadiness was the absence of a fight rather than the far end of one, and said nothing about it, because he would have waved it off the way he waved off everything that came too close to being about him.

She sat with the duplicate page a moment longer than she needed to, in the chair across from his desk, before she let him have it back for the log, and thought of the other file that had never gotten a duplicate — no hand-copied page kept under a mattress, no second witness racing memory against a razor, only Vivian's four clean lines and a signature nobody in that room had ever thought to duplicate-check. Mara had not even rated a room being searched. Voss, guilty of nothing but bad timing and a habit of writing things down, was getting a six o'clock deadline, a photograph, and three hundred strangers deciding what kind of man he was before he'd said a word in his own defense; Mara had gotten a closed file and a sister who hadn't looked at the back of the intake sheet for six years. It was not, she made herself notice, a comfort that the department was finally being careful with somebody. It was a debt coming due to the wrong account.

The presser was held on the plaza outside city hall rather than the precinct, under lights rigged against a sky already losing what little color the day had given it, and the ground beneath the risers had that same soft-gray ice going hard again at the edges, so that Elena, standing at the back with Adeyemi, felt the cold climb up through her boots before the commissioner's spokesman had finished thanking everyone for their patience. The photograph they put on the screen behind him was not the man Elena had sat across from in a boardinghouse room three days ago — it was a license photo, six years stale, a younger and heavier face under fluorescent light, the eyes flat the way every license photo's eyes are flat, and she understood, watching three hundred people study it, that this was the only face of Daniel Voss the city would ever be shown, and that it would outlive, in every archive that mattered, the face of a man who had kept two years of a lit window in careful, dated ink.

The spokesman read Adeyemi's forty-minute win in a voice that made *person of interest* sound only technically different from what the crowd had already decided to call him, and added, without being asked, that Mr. Voss had "left his residence voluntarily and his current whereabouts are unknown," a sentence that did more work than any of the ones Adeyemi had fought over, because a city that had already been told to be afraid of a man did not need to be told twice that he'd run.

Reyes spoke third, after the spokesman and before the questions, in a good dark coat against the cold, and Elena watched him the way she had learned to watch him — not for what he said but for the shape he built with it. He thanked the department for its "extraordinary sensitivity in a matter involving a genuinely vulnerable man," said the Institute stood ready to assist Mr. Voss "the moment he is found, in whatever way keeps him safest, including from himself," and closed with a line that made two reporters visibly soften toward him on camera: "We failed this man once, years ago, by discharging him before he was ready. I would like this city to consider whether naming him a suspect before it has asked him a single question is failing him twice." It was, by any measure she could name, the kindest thing said on that stage all evening, and it was also, she thought, watching the exact angle of his chin and the exact pause he left before *twice*, a sentence built to be quoted, and a sentence that put a fence of compassion around a man nobody but Elena and a landlord in a cardigan had any reason to know was already gone from any place he could be found. She found her eyes drop, out of an old and now unwelcome habit, to the line of his coat over his heart, checking a door she already knew was locked.

Sandoval was there too, off to one side near the barricade tape in a coat Elena had never seen her wear, not taking notes, not speaking to anyone, watching the stage with the same unhurried, unbothered attention she brought to everything, and when her eyes found Elena's across the plaza she didn't look away or nod or do anything at all except hold the look a second longer than a stranger would, which was, Elena had come to understand, Sandoval's version of a whole sentence: *I see you watching him the way I watch both of you.* It occurred to her, standing there in the cold with the IA lieutenant twenty feet off and Reyes's kindness still hanging in the air like breath, that she was no longer sure whether Sandoval had come to observe the department's handling of Voss or to observe her observing Reyes, and that the honest answer was probably that Sandoval, unlike Elena, had never needed to choose between the two questions.

A young woman near the front, introduced by the spokesman almost as an afterthought as being from "Senator-elect Cross's transition office," asked whether the commissioner's office could confirm reports that pressure from the incoming senator's staff had accelerated tonight's timeline, and the spokesman said only that "public confidence matters at every level," which answered nothing and confirmed everything, and Elena filed the woman's face and title away the way she filed anything that arrived without asking to be noticed — not yet a thread she could pull, only a hand she now knew was somewhere on the loom.

Afterward, in the thinning crowd, Adeyemi found her again near the barricades, his collar up, his breath fogging harder than the temperature alone explained. "I logged the duplicate under your name and mine, chain intact, timestamped before the presser," he said. "It won't stop tonight. It might matter later, when *later* asks who knew what and when." He looked at her, and there was something in it she hadn't seen from him before, not doubt exactly, closer to the specific tiredness of a man doing the arithmetic on how many more times he could spend his own credibility on hers before the number ran out. "I need you to start writing things down the way you did with the notebooks. Not for me. For whoever reads this file after both of us are done being careful about each other's feelings."

She said she would, and meant it, and was still working out how to say the rest of what she owed him when the radio at his hip crackled with a call that made him go still in a way she'd learned to read as worse than anger: a security patrol at the Mercer Institute's service gate reporting a man matching the broadcast description let in through the side entrance not forty minutes ago, waved through by a guard who'd seen the six o'clock news and only afterward thought to wonder why a wanted man would walk toward the one building in the city most likely to recognize him.

"That's not possible," Elena said, and heard how thin it sounded, the same thinness she'd heard in her own voice defending Reyes to Sandoval a day and a lifetime ago.

Adeyemi was already moving toward his car, and she went with him, neither of them saying much as he pulled out past the last of the news vans, the radio still murmuring the guard's description back at them — dark coat, moved slow, didn't resist, didn't say a word — a description that fit half the men in the county and, worse, fit the version of Voss the license photo had trained everyone to expect rather than the man Elena had actually sat with, the one who could name the exact night two years running that the window had gone dark early. "If he walked in on his own," she said, mostly to the windshield, "then whatever's waiting for him in there is something he thinks is safer than what's out here. That's not a man fleeing a crime. That's a man going somewhere he's already decided is the last safe room left." Adeyemi didn't answer, which she'd learned by now meant he agreed and didn't like it, and the wipers took another pass at ice starting to bead again on the glass as the temperature did exactly what the sky had promised it would.

They came around the harbor road with the heater laboring and the radio gone quiet, and across the black water as they came around the harbor road the east wing of the Mercer Institute stood exactly where it always stood, four panes and a mullion, and the window that had no business being anything but dark at an hour when its founder's death was being read aloud on every screen in the city was burning, steady and gold, like a lamp someone had gone to the trouble of lighting for a guest they were certain, this time, would actually come home.
