She did not drive back. That was the discipline, and it cost her the rest of the block to hold it: pull over, breathe, do not turn the car around and go pounding up three flights of driftwood-gray stairs to stand between Daniel Voss and a threat she could not yet name, because a detective circling back twice in one morning was exactly the kind of pattern a watcher would notice, and she had spent four weeks learning that the surest way to endanger a person was to be seen caring about them in the wrong rhythm. She read the four words again — *You found him first* — until the rain on the windshield blurred them, and made herself translate the sentence the way she'd learned to translate all of them: not as a threat, which was too simple, but as a report. Something had clocked her arrival at that boardinghouse within the hour and thought it worth saying out loud. That was the fact. Everything after the fact was want.

She called Adeyemi from the gas station on Cannery Row, standing under the awning with the rain coming off the roof in a solid rope beside her, and told him only the part that would survive Sandoval's file: that she'd gone to canvass Voss ahead of the board, that he'd talked, that none of it cleared him and none of it hung him, that she thought the department should hold off putting his face on anything until someone did an actual interview instead of a press release.

"They moved the deadline again," Adeyemi said. "Thursday's gone. Commissioner wants a name for the six o'clock Wednesday, and before you ask, no, that's not us being slow, that's somebody leaning on the building from outside."

"Who."

"Cross's office called the commissioner personally yesterday. Not a staffer — the chief of staff, on Cross's behalf, using the words *public confidence* four times in ninety seconds, according to the girl who took the call. Senator-elect wants the city looking capable before she's sworn in, and a serial-sounding case with a known stalker suspect sitting open for a month doesn't photograph well next to her transition committee." He let that sit a second. "You know she used to talk about the Mercer Clinic. Profile piece, few years back, before she ran. Said some doctor there — didn't name Vivian, but come on — helped her through something rough in her twenties. It's why half the hospital board thinks she'll go easy on them if this stays quiet, and why the other half thinks that's exactly why she wants it closed fast. Either way it's not usually a Senator-elect's business who we charge with what, and it is now, and I don't love that, and neither do you, so don't do the thing where you go quiet on me."

She did not go quiet on him. She told him she was going to write up Voss as a soft lead, no priors, no violence, cooperative — the truth, dressed for a file — and asked him, carefully, not to let anyone move on him before she filed it.

"I can hold it a day," Adeyemi said. "Maybe two, if Sandoval doesn't ask me why I'm holding it." A pause, longer than the last one. "El. She asked me something else yesterday. Not about Voss. About Reyes. Whether I thought it was strange, you and him, how often you two end up in the same room lately. I told her he's a witness who returns his calls, which is more than I can say for most of this case, and she wrote something down and didn't tell me what." Another pause. "I've had your back four years. I requested you, you know that? After the thing with Danvers, when nobody wanted the detective who'd argue with a lieutenant in a briefing. I don't need you to tell me everything. I need you to tell me if I'm about to walk into a room and find out I didn't know something I should've."

She thought of the tape in the un-oiled drawer, and the gray door, and the key riding in a coat pocket two miles from where she stood, and said, "You're not walking into anything today," which was true in the narrowest possible sense and felt, saying it, like the same maneuver she kept accusing other people of making on her.

She spent the afternoon at her desk doing the paperwork properly for once — Voss's statement typed clean, his notebooks noted as *observed, not yet seized, subject cooperative*, nothing that would read to Sandoval as more than diligence — and watched, on the muted newsroom feed above the bullpen, a segment about the Mercer case run under a chyron that had already, without anyone in the department saying his name on the record, found a photograph of Daniel Voss from some old discharge paperwork and put it under the words A PERSON OF INTEREST. She understood then that Adeyemi's day or two was already spending itself, that the shape of the thing did not need the department's permission to close around a man, and that logging him gently in a file nobody outside Homicide would read was not going to be enough.

She went back to the boardinghouse a second time after dark, telling herself it was routine — a follow-up interview, on the log this time, nothing that would look like the thing Adeyemi had asked her not to do off the record — and telling herself, underneath that, the truer and smaller reason: that she wanted the notebooks in evidence, properly witnessed, before the city's appetite reached the shelf under his window and decided two years of a lonely man's careful handwriting was a stalker's trophy case instead of the only clock anyone had ever kept on that room.

The rain had gone colder in the hours since morning, close to sleet again, and Harbor Street was empty in the particular way waterfront streets went empty once the last ferry crossing before the late one had come and gone. She climbed the three flights past the smell of instant coffee and wet wool, and knocked, and waited longer than she had that morning before the chain came off, and when the door opened Daniel Voss's face had a different kind of stillness in it than the fear she'd read on him at nine a.m. — not startled, not relieved to see her. Emptied out.

"Somebody came," he said, before she'd asked, before she was fully through the door. "This afternoon. Maybe two hours after you left."

She kept her voice level, the way you kept your hands level around something that might still fall. "Tell me exactly."

He had let the man in, he said, because the man had known things a stranger shouldn't — said he was with the Institute, "following up on Dr. Mercer's outreach commitments," a phrase official enough to sound rehearsed and vague enough to mean anything, and Voss, who had spent two years being nobody's priority, had wanted, badly, to be somebody's business for ten minutes. Calm, Voss said, unhurried the way some men were unhurried because the world had never once made them run for anything. Didn't show a badge or a card. Asked about the notebooks — not whether they existed, Voss realized only afterward, turning it over in the hours since, but which nights had the most detail in them, which entries Voss himself thought mattered most, phrased soft as concern. Voss, flattered into it, had told him. The man had thanked him for keeping such good records, the exact words, *such good records*, as if he'd known there was a shelf of them before he'd ever been shown it, and before he left he had straightened the stack — unasked, unremarked, the small tidying gesture of a man who couldn't help himself around disorder — squaring the spines into a single clean column by year, and had gone, and had not taken anything, and Voss had stood there for an hour before he understood that not taking anything was not the same as leaving nothing changed.

Elena crossed to the shelf. The column was exact, corners flush, the kind of alignment that took either obsessive care or real time, and she found herself thinking of Reyes's hands on the teacup, the quarter-turn, the man who could not rush glass or people — and made herself stop thinking it, because a straightened shelf was not a signature and she had promised herself, four weeks ago, in a records annex with an un-oiled drawer, that she would not spend a feeling as if it were a fact.

She opened the notebook for the week Vivian died — Voss watching her do it now, no hesitation left in him, past the point where withholding felt like safety — and paged to the date, and stopped.

The entry was gone. Not blank, not skipped: a page had been removed, cut close along the spine with something fine enough to leave almost no shadow of itself, so clean that if she hadn't been looking for exactly that date she might have read the notebook's rhythm as unbroken, the next page's dateline picking up two nights later as though nothing between them had ever been written down.

"I didn't do that," Voss said, and his voice had gone thin in a way that frightened her more than if he'd shouted it. "I never — I wouldn't. Those are the only ones I have. I told you everything that was on it this morning, I told you, but I didn't — that page was there. I know that page was there. I've read it forty times."

She believed him instantly and completely, which was its own small data point she filed without comment: that his verbal account, given to her at nine that morning, was now the only surviving record of what he had watched the night Vivian Mercer died, and that whoever had sat in this cold room being thanked for his good records had known precisely which single page, out of two years and hundreds of nights, was the one worth the trouble of a blade instead of a threat.

"Give them to me," she said. "All of them. Tonight."

He did not hesitate this time, did not slide them two inches and wait to be trusted with a promise. He put the whole stack in her arms himself, dated spine after dated spine, two years of a lonely man's discipline, and she signed his copy of a chain-of-custody form she'd brought exactly in case this hour arrived, and told him, because it was true and because he had earned the truth twice in one day, that she did not think he would be safe alone in this room much longer, and watched that land on him without surprise, only a kind of exhausted recognition, as though some part of him had been waiting two years for someone to finally say what the window already knew.

She carried the stack down three flights in the sleet and set it on the passenger seat where, that morning, a phone had lit up with four words she still hadn't answered, and she was reaching across to steady the notebooks against the turn onto Harbor Street when her palm came down flat on the topmost cover and found it warm — not sun-warm, not car-warm, the specific close warmth of a hand that had been resting on it recently, held there, the particular heat a thing keeps only for minutes after it's set down — and Elena sat very still at the stop sign with the wipers going and the sleet ticking on the roof and understood, with the kind of stillness that comes just before real fear, that whoever had straightened Daniel Voss's books into a column so exact it looked measured had not left two hours ago the way Voss believed.

They had left when she knocked.
