The original logbook came to her the way the worst things had come to her all week — on a turned page, across a counter, handed over by someone afraid of having handed it.

It was Wednesday, and the cold had a different quality now, a dry hardness that made the morning sound brittle, the gulls sharper, the ferry horn out in the strait carrying farther than it had any right to. Elena had not gone home after Hartwell Lock & Safe. She had sat in the car at the dead end of Cannery Road until the gold square in the east wing came up across the water like a struck match, and she had watched it burn and not driven toward it, because she had learned by now that going where the light wanted her was the whole design. Instead she had called Marguerite at the number printed on a card in the clinic's lobby rack, and she had said only: I need the book itself. Not the photographs of it. The book. And Marguerite, who had kept the paper logbook for nineteen years and refused to computerize it, who had told Elena that the danger was always a record and then guarded the record anyway, had been quiet for a long moment and then said, Come to the side door. The one off the alley. Come before eight.

So Elena came before eight, while the institute was still dark and the bronze plaque by the front entrance — we are not what was done to us — caught no light at all, and Marguerite let her in through a service door that smelled of floor wax and cold coffee and led her not to the windowless records room but to a small staff kitchen where the blinds were down and a single bulb burned over a sink. On the laminate table sat the logbook, the real one, its cover the soft oxblood of a thing handled daily for two decades, the chain that normally tethered it to the cabinet coiled beside it like something Marguerite had decided to unleash exactly once.

"I took it off the chain," Marguerite said. Her hands were folded on the tabletop in the way of a woman holding herself very still so as not to fly apart. "Nineteen years it has never left that room. I want you to understand what I am doing." She was not asking for absolution. She was establishing the weight of the thing, the way Whit had wanted to be told that three keys was a number a man could walk away from. Elena was beginning to understand that this was what honest people did at the edge of a thing like this: they made you witness the size of their fear, because they could not afford to carry it alone and could not lie about it either.

"I understand," Elena said.

"You looked at the photographs. You looked at the line." Marguerite's voice was steady, but her thumb moved against her knuckle, once, twice. "You read it the way I read it. A true signature made unreadable. A person who did not want to lie and could not afford the truth." She drew a breath. "But you only ever looked at the borrower's column. The name. You were so hungry for the name." She turned the book toward Elena and opened it to the page she had already found, the page she had clearly sat with through the night herself, because the spine fell open to it without coaxing. "I have looked at that page for ten days, Detective, the way you look at a word you have spelled correctly until it stops looking like a word. And last night I stopped looking at the name. And I looked at everything else."

Elena sat down. She did not reach for the book. She had learned, this week, the discipline of not reaching.

October 29. The line was where it had always been, the home-office key signed out at 9:15 in the evening under that cramped, over-built signature, the name begun and then taught to vanish — the hand she had now seen four times, on Mara's intake, in this logbook, on a case-intake referral stamped beneath with a drowned girl's typed name, on a two-year-old receipt for three identical keys. She had spent a week reading that obscured hand as the center of everything, the spine of the case, the thing that would have a name when she finally pried it open. And she still could not read it. The discipline held. The railing held.

"What did you see," Elena said.

Marguerite put one finger on the column to the left of the signature. Not the borrower's name. The line beside it. The column Elena had photographed and never truly read, because her eye had gone straight past it to the snag of the handwriting the way a tongue goes to a sore tooth. The logbook had columns the way old ledgers did, more of them than anyone used: date, time, key removed, key returned, borrower, and one more, a narrow column at the far left with a faded printed header that Elena, staring now, made herself sound out: ON BEHALF OF.

"Marguerite kept it because the doctors used to send for keys," Marguerite said, speaking of herself in the third person the way the very tired sometimes do, as if narrating a stranger. "Long ago. A doctor would send an assistant down to fetch a patient's file room key, or the key to a consult office, and the assistant would sign their own name as borrower but write the patient's name here — on behalf of — so we'd know whose business the key was on. It fell out of use years ago. Nobody fills it in anymore. I had stopped seeing the column at all." Her finger did not move. "Whoever signed out Vivian's office key on the twenty-ninth filled it in. They are the only person in three years to fill it in. And I think they filled it in for you. I think they wanted you to find it the way you found everything else they left out for you to find."

Elena looked down at the narrow column, at the faded printed words on behalf of, and at the name written there in a hand entirely different from the obscured borrower's signature beside it — not cramped, not hidden, but careful, deliberate, formed in tidy block capitals with the particular over-square evenness of a person who had been taught, somewhere, sometime, to print rather than write so that they could never be mistaken.

She knew the block capitals before she let herself read the letters. She had a photocopy of them in the stiff drawer by her bed. She had traced them at her own kitchen table six nights ago, comparing them to nothing, just looking, the way you look at a dead person's handwriting because it is the closest thing to a voice you have left.

The name in the on-behalf-of column was MARA VEGA.

The kitchen did a slow quarter turn around her. The bulb over the sink hummed. Somewhere in the building a heating pipe knocked, twice, and was still.

Her sister had been dead for six years. Mara had died in a rented room with the bathwater gone cold, ruled a suicide on Vivian Mercer's signed assessment, and Elena had carried the wound of it so far inside herself that she had concealed it from her own department, requested proximity to anything Mercer through channels that made no paper, and told herself for six years that her hatred of the named, hateable woman was the same thing as justice. And eleven days before Vivian Mercer was murdered, somebody had walked into this records room after hours, signed for the key to that woman's most private room in a hand they had spent a lifetime learning to erase — and in the one column nobody fills in anymore, in the careful block capitals of a person teaching themselves to be unmistakable, they had written that the key was taken on behalf of Mara Vega.

A dead woman. Six years dead. The key to the murder room logged out in her name.

"You see it," Marguerite said softly. It was not a question; nobody in Hartwell Bay asked questions anymore. "I did not know who she was. I looked her up last night. I am sorry, Detective." And then, because she was the kind of witness who could not leave a true thing half-said: "I do not think they were careless. I think they printed her name carefully so that when you finally looked at the column you would not be able to tell yourself it was a coincidence. I think the unreadable signature was for the world. And I think this" — her finger, on the block capitals — "was for you."

Elena sat with her hands flat on the cold laminate and did not pick up the book and did not weep and did not, for once, reach for the discipline, because the discipline had been built to keep her from leaping at conclusions and this was not a conclusion. It was a message. It had her sister's name on it. It had been waiting in a column nobody reads for eleven days, the way the dead name SOPHIE LUND had waited on the case intake, the way the puncture had waited in the soft inside of Vivian's elbow, the way the third key was waiting somewhere she could not see — all of it left out for her, dosed to her, the lighthouse burning across the water every single night so that she would lift her face to it.

She understood, finally and completely, what they had told her on the first morning without telling her, what the upright corpse and the perfect grief and the silence on the open phone line had all been spelling out in a hand made deliberately hard to read. The case had not come to her. She had been called to it. Not by the rotation, not by Adeyemi, not by chance. By someone who had reached six years into the ground, taken her sister's name, and used it as the tag on the key that bolted the door of a murder — knowing that Elena Vega, of all the detectives in the city, was the one person alive who would recognize that name in a column no one reads, and would not be able to stop.

"They didn't take the office key because they needed it," Elena heard herself say. Her voice came from very far down. "They had a key. They've had a key for two years." She thought of Whit, of three identical blanks cut on one bitting, of the doctor type who said nothing and did the carrying and put them in his pocket. "They signed this one out so it would be found. They signed it out under my sister's name so that I would find it. The whole logbook was a letter, Marguerite. And the address on it was me."

Marguerite said nothing. She reached across the table and, with a gentleness that undid something in Elena that the horror had not, closed the oxblood cover over the page, as if shutting a coffin, as if that could keep the name in.

Elena drove back along the water with the heater roaring and felt nothing of it. Across the strait the gold window of the east wing had gone dark with the full arrival of morning — the lantern only burned at night, she had been told, the building only a lighthouse after dark — and she found that its darkness frightened her more than its light ever had, because a light that burns to be seen is at least telling you the truth about wanting to be seen. The dark window was the one she could not read. The dark window was the obscured signature. The dark window was the eleven days that name had lain in the book, patient as the rain, waiting for her to be ready to be wounded by it.

Her phone buzzed against the console.

She knew before she looked. The clinic exchange. The same number that had sent her own lit window in the rain with don't trace this, the photograph of the puncture before the ink was dry, the two words about Osei with no question mark. She let it sit, glowing, the length of one held breath, and then she looked.

No image this time. No threat she could name. Three words, lowercase, almost tender, from a number that should not have been able to reach her at all:

welcome home, elena.

And across the water the dark window of the lantern room gave back nothing, and she understood that the first ten days had only ever been the walk down the stairs, and that someone had been standing at the bottom of them the whole time, holding her sister's name like a key, waiting for her to arrive.
