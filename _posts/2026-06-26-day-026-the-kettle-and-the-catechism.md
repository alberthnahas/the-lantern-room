She did not go that night. She told him she would come in the morning, which was the kind of small true thing she could still afford to say, and then she sat in the dark kitchen with the two folders and the cold coffee until the lamp turned to the wall stopped looking like a lamp and started looking like an instruction, and she understood that she had already decided to go and was only deciding what to carry.

Not the file. The file stayed in the un-oiled drawer, photographed and pressed and known by heart. Not the tape; the tape was off-grid above the strait where she had left it, and the surest way to lose a thing was to bring it into a room with a kettle in it. She would carry nothing she could not afford to set down, because the man who had laid his hand flat against the pane of her grief to feel whether the glass was warm would be standing close enough, tomorrow, to read her pockets the way he read her pauses. She would walk into the lit lantern carrying only the thing she could not put down anyway: that she knew, and that she could not say she knew.

That was the trap inside the trap. Reyes had built a room with no third door — trust him or be the kind of person who could not trust anyone, including herself — and she had found the seam in it, the one fact that was true regardless of how she felt, the second clause he should not have been able to speak. But a fact she could not produce was not a key. It was a stone in her shoe. She would go to the clinic tomorrow and let him hand her a name across a kettle, and the whole time she would be standing on the one thing she could not show him, the way you stand on a trapdoor and keep your weight even so the latch does not feel you shift.

In the morning the cold had not changed. It did not change anymore; it only deepened by degrees too small to feel until you were already colder. Thursday, the white-hard sky deciding nothing over the strait, frost in the window seams set like something poured and left. She drove to the Mercer Institute the way she had driven to it the first time, before any of it, and parked where she could see the east wing and the single window that the town called a lighthouse, and it was, in daylight, just a window — leaded, old, four panes around a center mullion, a window divided into lights. She had not let herself see it before. Now she could not unsee it. Four little panes around a center. The glyph in the corner of Mara's tape label. The maker's mark. The architect had signed his work with the shape of this window, and the window had been burning over the water every night for fifteen years while the town called it a kindness.

Marguerite let her in at the desk, and her face did the thing it had learned to do since the empty hook — a flinch held still, a witness pretending she had not become one. "He's expecting you," she said, and then, lower, the way she had learned to lower things: "He came in before light. He's never here before light." It was not gossip. It was the woman with nineteen years of logbook telling her what the page would have said if anyone wrote it down. Elena thanked her and did not write it down either, and went up the stairs that faced the water.

His office was warm and smelled of the kettle, and the kettle was real — a battered enamel thing on a hot plate by the window, steam ghosting the leaded glass — and the two chairs were already turned a little toward each other, the way a man arranges a room for a conversation he has already had in his head. The faded photograph of Sophie Lund was where it had always been on the credenza, the girl squinting into a fifteen-years-dead sun. He had not moved it. He had not hidden it. That was the thing about Reyes that she kept having to relearn: he never cleared the evidence away, because he had decided long ago that the most dangerous record was the one you tried to bury, and the safest was the one you left in plain sight and explained.

"You came," he said, and poured, and the warmth in it was real, and that was the trap.

"You said there'd be a kettle."

"I keep my small promises." He handed her the cup so her hands would have a job, the way Sandoval had slid her bad coffee, the way everyone who had ever wanted to read her had given her hands a job, and she took it because refusing was also a tell. "I won't make you ask twice. The name in Vivian's records, the one you carry like a stone." A pause, placed. "Lund."

For a moment the room went very still, the kettle's small voice the only thing in it. She had braced for Mara. She had built the morning around hearing her sister's name come out of his mouth in his perfect kindness. He had said Lund.

"Sophie Lund," she said, the cold clerk, level.

"Sophie's mother, in the file. But yes. The same name." He sat. "Vivian kept Sophie the way you'd keep a scar where you could see it. But she kept others near her, in the same drawer of her mind, the way grief files itself — and your sister's name was filed beside Sophie's. Not as patients. As" — he turned his cup the quarter-turn, the tic — "as a pair she kept returning to. Two she'd lost. I don't know what it means, Elena. I'm telling you it's there because you'd rather know I've seen it."

It was masterful, she thought, the way you might admire a thing that was killing you. He had taken the one true link she had found in the margins of two files — Sophie and Mara, the same hand, the same grammar, the lantern is ready to be lit and she is ready to be useful — and he had handed it back to her dressed as a dead woman's grief. A pair Vivian kept returning to. He had given her the connection and stolen its meaning in the same breath, the way he had given her the catechism's origin as a fond friend's habit, the way he gave her everything fully so that nothing she found later would feel like a discovery he had not already framed.

She set the cup down, even, careful, on the table between them, and made herself ask the question that was not the question. "What was the same about them," she said. "In the file. You said filed beside each other. Same hand?"

She watched him not answer for the half-second it took a man to decide how much to spend, and she learned more from the half-second than she would have from any answer. Because an innocent friend, a grieving protégé going honestly through his dead mentor's records, would have said I don't know, I'm not a graphologist, they were Vivian's files. He did not say that. He said, gently, "You'd have to read them side by side to see it. The way you already have."

She had not told him she had read them side by side. She had photographed two notations in one frame in a kitchen with the blinds down, and within the hour the clinic exchange had texted how is sophie, elena. The reach was not surveillance. This anticipated. And here was the mouth of it, sitting across a kettle, telling her gently that he knew she had already done the thing the watcher had watched her do — knew it well enough to fold it into a sentence and hand it back as if it were her own idea.

"The way I already have," she repeated.

"You're a pattern reader, Elena. It's why they—" He stopped. He let the unfinished it's why they sit in the warm room with its steam and its dead girl in the photograph, and she finished it for herself the way he had built the sentence so she would: it's why they gave you the case. He had walked her, in his perfect kindness, right up to the rim of the thing — that she had been chosen, that her gift was the lever — and then let her be the one to look down into it, so that the looking would feel like hers.

"Why they what," she said, the cold clerk, making him spend.

"Why they should never have given you this," he said, and the swerve was so clean she almost missed that it was a swerve, "and why I'm glad they did, because anyone else would have filed it suicide and gone home." He leaned forward, and the kindness in him was a real thing, a true warm current, and that was the worst of it, because she could not even hate the hand on the pane. "I want to show you something. Not today. You're not ready, and frankly neither's the building." He glanced, once, at the wall behind the credenza, the east wall, the wall the window was set into, and she made herself not follow his eyes, because the room was on the other side of that wall and she could not be the one who found it, not yet, not today, not with him watching to see whether she already knew it was there. "There's a room. Vivian's. Locked since she died. I have a key and I haven't used it, because some doors you don't open alone." He held her eyes. "When you're ready, we open it together."

He was offering her the room. That was the move, and it was the most frightening generous thing he had done, more than the sister, more than the kettle — he was offering to hand her the Lantern Room himself, on a day of his choosing, framed and explained, with his hand on the light switch, so that when she walked into it she would walk into his version of it, the version where he opened the door for the grieving detective out of love. She understood that she must not take it. She must want it visibly and decline it slowly, the way you refuse the third remedy, because a room he opened for her was a room she could never use, and a room she found alone was a room that would end him — and he knew that too, which was why he had offered.

"When I'm ready," she said.

"When you're ready." He sat back, satisfied or sad, she could not tell, and that was new; she had always been able to tell. The kettle had gone quiet. He poured the last of it into his own cup and turned it the quarter-turn. "Drink your tea, Elena. You haven't slept, and the cold's worse by the water."

By the water. He said it the way he said everything, an idle kindness, and she heard it land — the foghorn under his voice on the phone yesterday, the breather on the second day down by the water listening back, the man not in his office over the strait but somewhere low at the rim of it. He was telling her, in the safest possible sentence, that he knew where the cold was worst, because he had been standing in it. Or he was only a tired man pouring tea. The case had been built so that every fact about him fit both doors, and she drank the tea because the version of her that did not drink it was the version that had already shown him her hand.

She left before noon. Marguerite watched her go and did not say anything, and Elena thought: she wants me to find it too, the woman with the logbook, she has spent nineteen years labeling drawers and she has finally found the one she was never allowed to open. In the lot the window over the water was a pale square in the daylight, four panes around a center, unlit and waiting for dusk to make it a lighthouse again.

Her phone buzzed on the seat. Not the clinic exchange. Not the fifth number. Marguerite — a number Elena had taken on the first day and never used. Three words, typed slow, an old woman's careful thumbs, sent from the desk Elena had just walked away from while the man upstairs poured the last of the kettle:

he has the key
