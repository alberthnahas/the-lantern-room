The snow had stopped sometime after three, the way a held breath finally lets go, and by the time Elena parked outside First Strait Maritime the harbor road lay under a foot of it, unbroken except for the single black seam a plow had cut down the center like a suture. The sky had gone the hard flat white of a light box, no sun in it yet, only the promise of one somewhere behind the cloud deck, and the cold had a new quality she hadn't felt since November — dry, painless, the kind that didn't announce itself until she noticed her jaw had been clenched for an hour.

Adeyemi was already on the steps when she got there, stamping feeling back into his boots, a folded copy of Judge Aldana's order in a plastic sleeve against his chest like something he didn't trust the weather not to take from him. He looked like a man who hadn't slept, which he hadn't; she knew, because she hadn't either, and because at ten past midnight he'd texted her a single word — home — with no punctuation, the kind of message a person sent to prove to himself, more than to anyone else, that he'd made it somewhere safe.

Her own phone had already buzzed twice that morning before she'd finished her coffee — Adeyemi's home, and then, twenty minutes later, Reyes, unprompted as always, offering to have Institute counsel on standby "in case Vivian's estate raises anything you don't want to spend a Saturday arguing about," phrased as though he had any right to know a warrant existed at all. She had not answered it. She had stopped answering most of his offers lately, not out of anger, but because she had started keeping a private ledger of her own, and every unanswered kindness was a line item in it.

"You get eyes on your phone?" she asked, meaning the message from the night before, the one that had arrived on his line instead of hers and told them both, in five words, that the watching had never cared which of them it was following.

"Sandoval's IT people are on it. They'll tell me what they tell everyone — untraceable, spoofed, offshore relay, nothing they can build a warrant on." He said it flatly, a fact he'd already filed. "I didn't sleep because of the phone. I slept badly because Priya asked me this morning whether I wanted a protective detail on my house, and I had to explain to a nine-year-old why a police car might be parked outside for her dance recital." He didn't look at her when he said it. "She's Tuesday. I already told her I'd be there."

"You will be."

"I know," he said, in a voice that wanted it to be true more than it believed it, and pushed open the bank's door before the cold could ask him anything else.

Dolores Pham was waiting inside with a man in a gray suit who introduced himself as bank counsel and shook no one's hand, and a security officer whose only job, it seemed, was to stand near the vault door radiating the specific unhappiness of a man told to open something he'd been paid for two years not to open. Pham's eyes found Elena's the moment she came in, and held a beat longer than a teller's should — not fear, exactly. Vindication with a price tag on it.

"You don't have to be down there for this," Elena told her quietly, while counsel checked his phone by the stairs. "Swearing to a sign-in log is one thing. Standing over an open box is another."

Pham's jaw set in a way that reminded Elena, briefly and without warning, of an eighty-one-year-old coroner's stubborn honesty three weeks past — the particular set of a person who had decided the truth was worth more to them than their own comfort. "I looked at that woman's forged signature for two nights running until I was sure enough to say it out loud to a judge I've never met," Pham said. "I'm not going to miss what's actually inside."

"They drilled it an hour ago," counsel said, leading them down the marble stairs into the vault's cold hush, "since the renter's identity is disputed and no key was recovered. Standard procedure once the court order clears probate concerns. I want it on record that the bank cooperated fully and objects to nothing."

"Noted," Adeyemi said, in the voice he used for people who needed to hear themselves being agreeable.

Box 214 sat open on a steel table under a single overhead light, its little brass door hanging loose on its hinge, and Elena understood before she'd taken three steps toward it that something was wrong with the way it sat — too light, the drawer sliding out of its slot with none of the settled weight a two-year archive should have. She'd expected, walking in, to feel the particular vertigo of finally reaching a thing she'd chased for three days. Instead she felt the older, more familiar vertigo of arriving somewhere and finding the door already open.

The drawer held almost nothing. A slim manila folder with the box's original rental paperwork — Vivian's alias, Pham's own initials on the witness line, a two-year-old date — and, tucked behind it, a single unfilled referral slip to an oncology specialist in Tacoma, fourteen months old, never used, the box for PATIENT SIGNATURE still blank. Elena looked at it a long moment without touching it. A woman who signed everything, in every hand she had, had never signed this one thing.

She thought, unbidden, of the number she still called sometimes at two in the morning — a habit six years old, older than any of this, that rang four times into a voicemail box that had been full since the year Mara died — and understood, for one unwelcome second, that a woman capable of building a program out of other people's fear had also been capable of leaving something unsaid until it killed her. It did not make Vivian sympathetic. It only made her, briefly, familiar.

That was all. In a box built to hold a two-year archive, there was a folder thin enough to carry in one hand.

But the velvet lining at the back of the drawer sat wrong — not flush, the way felt should lie when nothing had disturbed it, but faintly domed along one edge, as if something had been pressed in behind it and left a memory the fabric hadn't relaxed out of yet. It was the kind of thing that meant nothing to anyone who hadn't spent five weeks learning to read the small, patient shapes objects left when someone had been careful with them. Elena had spent five weeks learning exactly that.

"Can I—" she started, and counsel said, "The order permits full examination," before she'd finished asking, which told her he wanted this over as badly as she did.

She worked two gloved fingers under the lining's edge and found, behind it, a shallow gap cut into the box's back wall — a false depth no wider than a hand, the kind of alteration a renter made herself, quietly, over a lunch hour, with a box cutter and patience — and inside it, flat and undisturbed, a single sealed envelope.

It had no stamp, no address, nothing typed. Just one word on the front, in a hand Elena had never seen before and knew instantly wasn't the obscured hand from the logbooks and the margins — that hand built itself carefully, over-struck, disguised even from the eye that made it. This one didn't try to hide anything. It was small, unsteady, slanting harder toward the end of the word than the beginning, the hand of someone writing fast, or in pain, or both.

Elena.

She stood holding it, aware of Adeyemi at her shoulder, of Pham's breath catching audibly behind her, of the security officer shifting his weight in a way that meant he wanted to say something about chain of custody and didn't quite dare.

"That wasn't in the visit log," Pham said quietly. "Whoever came the morning after — they took the binder. I'd swear to the binder, it's not a guess, there's a groove worn in the felt the exact width of a three-ring spine, two years of a hand sliding it in and out." She looked at the shallow cut in the wall, the lining that didn't lie flat. "But that's not where a binder goes. That's a place somebody made for one thing. Small. On purpose."

"Whoever took the ledger wasn't looking for a letter," Adeyemi said. He hadn't touched anything; he was watching Elena's face the way he'd watched it since the L-14 badge log, cataloguing what moved across it before she could put it away. "They knew what they came for. They took it clean and they left."

"Or they looked," Elena said, turning the envelope over once, finding nothing on the back but the same false-bottom dust, "and they didn't find this, because this wasn't meant to be found by someone hunting for a ledger. It was meant to be found by someone hunting for a person." She thought of Noah's voice on the phone the night before — the first mouth, the written thing under the recited thing — and understood, holding it, that he hadn't been wrong; he'd simply been looking for the wrong shape of it. Not a program's archive. A woman's letter, hidden inside the same walls that hid everything else she'd ever been afraid to say out loud.

"You're not opening that here," counsel said, with the relief of a man who had finally found a sentence he was sure of.

"No," Elena agreed, and meant it more than he understood — not because of procedure, though procedure would matter in an hour, but because some part of her that had spent six weeks reaching for exactly this, a page in Vivian Mercer's own unpracticed hand, addressed to no one else in the world, wanted five minutes alone with it before it became evidence, a photograph, a line item, a thing other people were allowed to have opinions about.

Adeyemi logged it into evidence himself, his handwriting steady in a way hers wouldn't have been, sealed it into a paper bag with both their initials across the flap, and didn't say what they were both thinking, which was that a locked box built to hide a program's secrets had also, somewhere along the way, become the only place in the city quiet enough for a dying woman to write down something true.

They came up out of the vault into a lobby gone bright with reflected snow-light, and Elena's phone woke in her coat before she'd finished buttoning it, one buzz, the number she'd first heard in the rain outside her own building six weeks ago, silent since the morning it told her she'd come the long way home.

You found the only thing I couldn't take.

She read it twice, standing on the granite steps where a forger's boots had stood eight days before hers, and understood, with the specific cold clarity of a fact arriving fully formed, that whoever had emptied that box in the dark had known exactly what they left behind — not because a camera had watched her find it, there was no camera on the vault floor, there had never been a camera on the vault floor — but because they had known, long before she ever stood in that room, that she was the only person alive who would think to look behind the felt.
