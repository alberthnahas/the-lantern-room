By the time Elena came up out of the sub-level the snow had stopped trying to mean business and simply meant it, six new inches laid flat and blue-white over everything the whiteout had already buried, the harbor road plowed once into a single ragged lane between banks taller than her car doors. Dawn was doing what dawn did in December in Hartwell Bay, which was arrive without conviction, a gray lightening behind the Institute's east wing that made the lantern window look, for once, like nothing at all — just four panes and a mullion, unlit, waiting its turn like everything else in the building that had learned to wait.

The commissioner's office called Adeyemi's cell before the ME's van had finished loading. Elena stood close enough to hear both halves of it in the cold, her breath a slow white engine in front of her face, and watched her partner's jaw work through the particular patience he reserved for people who outranked him and had never carried a body bag.

"No," he said. "No, sir, we are not releasing a cause pending forensic review." A pause, longer. "I understand there's a narrative that writes itself. I'm asking you not to let it." Another pause. "Because if it's wrong, sir, it's wrong in a way that gets printed and can't be unprinted, and I would rather be the one who's slow than the one who's certain and stupid." He hung up without waiting for whatever came after that, and stood a moment with the phone still against his ear as if bracing for it to ring again.

"They want it clean," he told Elena, not looking at her. "Troubled man, hunted by his own conscience, took himself out rather than face what he thought he'd done. It's a good story. It has a shape. Cross's people will love it — solved before her swearing-in, nobody Institute-adjacent has to answer a single hard question." He pocketed the phone. "I told them we're not confirming manner of death until the lab's had the page."

"You didn't tell them about the t and the d."

"I did not tell them about the t and the d." He met her eyes then, and something in his face had gone older overnight, the way it had once before, briefly, on the drive back from the Danvers house. "Because the second I do, it's real to more people than us, and every one of those people is a door it can walk out of. Right now there are three of us who know Voss didn't write the last four lines of his own confession. I would like to keep it three for as long as I can stand to."

Three, Elena thought, and did not correct him, because the third was Reyes, standing in a doorway with his concern arriving a half-second late, and there was no clean way to say that out loud yet that didn't sound like grief talking.

She drove home through streets the plow trucks hadn't reached, chains biting soft new snow, and slept two hours sitting up in her clothes with her boots still on, the way she'd learned to sleep in the last six weeks — a doze built to break at the first wrong sound. It broke at eleven, not to a sound but to her phone, face-down on the counter, buzzing itself in a slow circle. Not a number she recognized. Not one of the four — five, now, since the transcript photo — channels that had learned to find her either. Noah.

*Can you get to the ferry terminal. The old one, not the new dock. 1pm. Bring nothing that writes.*

The old ferry terminal had been decommissioned nine years ago when the new dock went in half a mile up the shore, and what was left of it — a squat brick waiting room with boarded ticket windows and a bank of wooden benches nobody had bothered to remove — survived now as a place teenagers went to be somewhere their parents weren't and commuters cut through on foul-weather days to avoid the wind off the water. It had two things Elena valued: foot traffic thick enough to be camouflage, and no working cameras, because nobody had thought it worth wiring a building already scheduled twice for demolition and reprieved twice by budget.

Noah was already there when she came in stamping snow off her boots, sitting at the far end of a bench with his collar up and his hands jammed into his coat pockets, and she understood before she'd crossed half the room that something in him had changed overnight. He'd always carried his fear like a passenger — present, managed, riding along while he did the actual work of being careful. What she saw now, sitting down beside him with the cold brick at her back, was fear that had climbed into the driver's seat.

"You heard," she said. Not a question.

"Reyes told the staff this morning. Before it was on the news, before anyone from your department called the clinic to notify next of kin, before anything." Noah's voice was low and fast, a man who had rehearsed the first sentence and was now improvising the rest. "He gathered everyone in the east-wing break room at eight and said a patient had passed, that our thoughts were with the family, that grief counseling would be made available to anyone who needed it, and that he'd appreciate us not speculating to the press." He looked at her sideways. "I didn't ask which patient. I already knew. Everybody already knew, the way you know a smell before you can name it. But nobody had *told* him yet. Not officially. I checked — the department's public notification didn't go out until nine-forty."

Elena filed it the way she'd filed the tea, the vouching call, the exact wrong word for her own private fear — a fact too small to build on and too clean to be nothing. Reyes had known before he had any right to know, and had used the knowing to get ahead of the story inside his own building, the same instinct that had once put Daniel Voss's name in front of the board before the board had thought to ask.

"Noah. Why the ferry terminal."

He was quiet long enough that she almost repeated the question. When he spoke his eyes were on the boarded ticket window across the room, its plywood gone silver-gray with age, as if there were still someone behind it he needed to not be overheard by.

"They killed a man for watching a window," he said. "For two years of nothing but *watching* it, writing down when it was lit and when it wasn't, never going in, never asking anyone anything he wasn't owed an answer to. I typed a strip with my own hands. I know symbols Vivian never wrote down anywhere else. I've been telling myself for six weeks that giving you a code here, a location there, a two-line gap on a page — that's careful. That's surviving. But it isn't giving you anything you could actually stand on, is it. It's giving you enough to keep you circling and not enough to end it. And I think — " His jaw tightened. "I think whoever's finishing sentences for people is going to run out of patience with careful eventually, and I would rather be the one who decided to be reckless than the one it happened to."

"What are you telling me."

He turned to face her fully then, and for a moment he looked younger than twenty-six, younger than the earnest, frightened researcher she'd first found in a records room five weeks ago, and older than that too, the way people go both directions at once in the same year of a bad case.

"Two years ago, before the strip, before her hand started shaking too badly to trust it — Vivian asked me to go with her to First Strait Maritime. The bank." He watched that land; he knew she'd know the name, the cash-drop branch, the teller who'd called it a debt that wasn't on any books. "Not to withdraw anything. To sign as a witness on a safe-deposit box. She rented it under a name that wasn't hers — V. Marsh — and told me the second signature was a formality the bank required for a box held that long without a listed co-owner, and that I should never mention it to anyone, including her. I thought — God, I don't know what I thought. That it was personal. Insurance papers. A will. Something a woman puts away and doesn't want her husband finding." He shook his head. "I only understood what it might actually be two nights ago, lying awake doing what I always do now, which is trying to remember everything I've ever been told to forget. She rented that box the same month she had me build the strip. The same month her hand started shaking. I think it's where she put the pages from before the strip existed — the ones she wrote by hand, before she trusted symbols to anyone else's memory but her own. The first mouth. The written thing under the recited thing."

Elena sat very still, the cold from the brick wall working slowly up through her coat, and thought of a locksmith's question she still hadn't been able to answer — *there were three, where's the third* — and of Vivian on foot, twice a month, carrying envelopes into the worst part of the waterfront to pay a debt that wasn't on any books, and understood that First Strait Maritime had been doing more work in this case than anyone had known to ask it.

"Can you get into it."

"No. That's the whole problem." Noah's laugh had no humor in it. "It's sealed to the estate now — Vivian's dead, the lease was in a name that isn't hers on paper, and I'm not listed as anything but a witness signature from two years ago that the bank has no obligation to remember. I'm not next of kin. I'm not an executor. I'm a research assistant who signed his name once on a form he was told meant nothing." He looked at her, and for the first time since she'd sat down there was something almost like hope in it, badly worn but present. "But you know someone with standing. You already used him once."

Gerald. She thought of him in his waterfront kitchen with the lantern lit gold across the black water, saying *a name I know*, and of the warning that had followed him home for it — *he'll ask the wrong person* — and of the promise she'd made herself afterward to keep him out of the room entirely, a man who was never in it.

"He's not safe to use twice," she said.

"Nobody's safe to use even once anymore. That's what I'm telling you." Noah stood, pulling his collar tighter, a man readying himself to walk back out into weather he'd rather not be in. "I have to get back before anyone counts the minutes I've been gone. Find a way in. I don't care whose name goes on the paperwork. I've been careful for five weeks and a man is dead in a chair with his hands folded like he was praying, and I would rather you have this than me."

She watched him go, a thin coated shape crossing the terminal's cracked floor and out through the propped door into blowing snow, and did not follow immediately, giving the room the two full minutes she'd learned to give any meeting before she moved through it herself. Her phone buzzed once, unhurried, while she still sat on the cold bench with the boarded ticket window in front of her.

Unknown number. Not the clinic exchange. Not Mara's old line. Not the pier's third number, or the fourth from the diner booth. A new one — the sixth to speak to her directly, if she was counting, and she was always counting.

*First Strait Maritime keeps its old boxes on the second floor, past the vault. V. Marsh rented 214 in March, two years before the shaking started. You should ask why a woman who never once lied to a bank in her life used a name that wasn't hers.*

Elena read it twice, standing now, the cold going through her differently than the wall's cold had, because Noah had said those two words — the bank's name, the pseudonym — to her alone, in a room with no wiring, no camera, no phone laid face-up between them, forty seconds before he'd walked out that door into the snow. There had been no one else close enough to hear him. There had been no one else in the room at all.

Which meant the watching had stopped needing a channel to overhear her calls, or a hand inside the department, or a text arriving one beat behind a private conversation in a diner booth. It had learned, somewhere in the last six weeks, how to be standing close enough to hear a frightened man whisper a dead woman's second name — and Elena, turning in the empty terminal with her breath fogging white in front of her, understood that she had no way of knowing whether that closeness had followed Noah out into the storm, or was, even now, still in the room with her.
