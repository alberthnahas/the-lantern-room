She had driven north again because there was nowhere else that wasn't a room.

The turnout above the strait was the same one she had used two days ago, a scrape of frozen gravel where the road bent away from the water before it climbed into the firs, and the cell on the seat beside her showed no bars, which was the only thing in her life she now trusted. Monday morning. The sky had gone past gray into something whiter and harder, the color of the inside of a shell, and the strait below lay flat and unmoving as poured metal. Frost held in the open where the sun should have burned it off hours ago. The cold had stopped pretending. It came in around the door seals of the Crown Victoria and sat with her like a passenger who had been waiting a long time.

On her knee, in the sandwich bag, the tape.

She had not slept. She had told herself she would not play it again — that she had heard the worst thing it could hold, her sister's living voice rising at the end of every sentence the way Mara's had always risen, that small upward lilt that made everything she said sound like a question she already knew the answer to and was too polite to give you. Elena had spent six years failing to remember that sound. The tape had handed it back to her and then, at the exact instant Mara named the man who walked her to the door, the reel had dropped into a clean quarter-second of nothing, a silence cut into the magnetic stock with a surgeon's hand, and her sister had resumed mid-breath — and I trusted him, and now I can't tell anymore which of the things I feel are mine — and Elena had sat in this same cold and understood that someone had given her the grief and removed the name in the same gesture, so that the two would arrive fused and she would chase a hole.

She had told herself that was the bottom.

But there had been the writing. Below for the audience, in the cramped vanishing hand she had now seen seven times and still could not read, three smaller words she had not seen until the light shifted across the bare plastic of side B.

Now turn it over.

It made no sense. She had played side A because it had been handed to her labeled, L-9 in Noah's typed strip, the boy on the rocks reciting his learned sentence. She had played side B because she had chosen to, and side B had been Mara. There was no third side. A microcassette had two. She had turned it over twice, which returned her to where she started — A, then B, then A again — and the message was an absurdity, a taunt with no referent, the kind of thing a watcher writes to make you spend the dark hours turning a thing in your hands until you wear yourself thin against it.

She took the transcriber out of the glovebox. The pawnshop machine, bought two towns over in cash, foam earpiece gone gray at the edge. She had not let herself bring Vivian's desk recorder anywhere near this; a chain of custody had hands, and hands were the whole problem. She fitted the tape, side A up, and pressed her thumb against the play key without pressing it, and looked at the water.

Now turn it over.

There was a way to hear an instruction, she thought, and a way to obey it, and the program had taught her — taught all of them — to mistake the second for the first. She had said as much to herself in this exact turnout. A detective who knelt where she was told and heard what she wanted was no detective at all. And yet she had been turning the tape over in the simple way, the way the words pretended to mean. Side to side. The two faces.

She turned it over in her hands instead. Looked at the edge of it.

The microcassette had a write-protect tab on its spine, the little plastic sliding gate you broke or moved to keep a tape from being recorded over. On a tape Vivian Mercer used — Vivian who said reuse was a kind of lying, whatever you put on top the old word is still under it — the tabs were always open. Always. Lind had told her that: never a reused tape, never a lapse, thirty years. Elena held the L-9 cassette up to the white light and saw that on this one the tab had been closed. Pushed shut. Locked.

A tape Vivian had ordered protected from erasure. A tape someone had been very sure would survive.

She almost set it down. The tab told her nothing she could use; it only confirmed the obvious, that the thing in her hand had been built to last, built to be found, built for her. And then she saw, because the light was doing her that one mercy this morning, that the spine of the cassette under the closed tab was not smooth. There was a seam in the plastic that did not belong, a hairline where the shell had been opened and pressed shut again, the way you would open a tape to splice it. To cut a quarter-second of silence into a reel and lay it back down. To punch a name out of the middle of a dead woman's sentence.

The dropout was not on the tape because the recording had failed. The dropout was on the tape because someone had opened the shell and made it.

She knew that already. She had reasoned it on Sunday — engineered, equipment-clean, a name removed by design. But there was a difference between reasoning a thing and holding the seam of it against your thumbnail in the cold, feeling the ridge where a person had taken her sister apart and put her back together with a piece missing. It was the kind of work that took patience and a steady hand and the right small tools laid out on a clean cloth, and time, and a private room with a door that locked, and Elena thought of the unhurried hour in the sealed office, the careful venipuncture, the lividity that said the body had lain flat and then been sat up before it set, and understood that the man who had cut the name out of her sister's voice and the man who had posed Vivian Mercer in her chair worked with the same hands and the same terrible patience. Whoever had done it had not erased the name. Vivian's whole religion was that erasure left the old word underneath. He had cut it out. He had taken the name somewhere else and left her the wound, the way you leave a person the scar and keep the bullet.

Now turn it over.

Not the tape. The thinking.

She had been listening to side B for what it took away — the name, the dropout, the cruelty of the gift. She had heard her sister and then heard the absence and let the absence be the message, because the absence was where her grief lived and the machine knew it. But Mara had said other things. Mara had said whole sentences before and after the hole, and Elena, struck stupid by the sound of the voice, had let those sentences run past her like water.

She pressed play on side B.

Her sister came into the foam earpiece, small and near and six years dead. I don't know why I keep coming. The lilt. I don't remember a door, Dr. Mercer. I just remember already being inside. And Vivian, low, unhurried, that voice you would follow anywhere: There's always a door, Mara. Someone walked you to it. Do you remember who? And the breath. And the cut. And — I trusted him, and now I can't tell anymore which of the things I feel are mine.

Elena stopped it. Wound back a hair. Let it run past the wound this time and kept going, made herself stay in the chair past the place where on Sunday she had torn the earpiece out.

Mara, still talking. Quieter now, the lilt flattening, and Elena's stomach turned because she had heard that flattening before — in the boy on side A, L-9, the taught smoothness, a sentence worn down by saying. Her sister's voice settling into the same groove.

I am not what was done to me, Mara said.

Elena's hand went still on the wheel.

I am not what was done to me. The water closed and the lantern stayed lit.

The same sentence. The boy's sentence, word for word, the recited line from side A, the one Vivian had pulled out of L-9 like a splinter and that Elena had taken for one man's private liturgy, his particular wound made into a charm he could carry. It was not his. It was Mara's too. The boy on the rocks fifteen years ago and her sister six years dead had been given the identical words to say, the water closed and the lantern stayed lit, two people who as far as the world knew had never met, never shared a room, never shared anything but a doctor and a tide — and they spoke the same sentence in the same flattened voice as if reading it off the same wall.

Not a symptom. A script. Vivian had not been listening to her patients. She had been hearing them recite.

Elena sat with the earpiece pressed to her head and the tape hissing toward its end and felt the case rotate under her, the slow grinding turn of a thing too large to see all of, and understood that this was what the writing had meant. Now turn it over. Not the cassette. The whole shape of it. She had been hunting a killer who hid a name. The tape had been trying, in its terrible curated way, to show her something worse and older than a murder — that the same hand had been writing the same sentence into different mouths for fifteen years, that the wall the words were read off was real and somewhere, and that her sister had stood in front of it and read.

The tape clicked off.

She did not move for a long time. The foghorn sounded once, far out past the breakwater, a single long note that came up the rocks and into the car and faded. A gull hung over the water without beating its wings, holding on the wind, motionless and watching, the way everything in this case watched. She thought: he will know I played it. The moment she dropped back into coverage the watcher would know she had been out of a room again, and would know what she had finally heard, because he had written the words on the spine that walked her to it. He always knew. He had built the road and stood at the end of it holding a lamp.

She thought of Reyes then, because she could not stop herself this morning — Reyes who had been so good to her, who made the tea himself, who had stood at the empty key hook and said we'll need to be very careful with the detectives, who was the only person in the whole rotted building who treated her grief as something to be gentle with. The calm graying generous man she would not let herself name even here, even off the grid, because a name reached because you wanted it was a name you could not trust. He would call her today. She was nearly sure of it. He had a way of arriving exactly when she had learned a thing, as if he could hear the learning happen, and asking her, kindly, what she had found — and she would have to decide, again, how much of her sister to hand to the man holding the lamp.

She put the car in gear.

She had gone perhaps a quarter mile down the climbing road, back toward the firs and the place where the bars would return, when the thought arrived that she should have had at the turnout and had been too undone to have: if the words were a script, then somewhere there was a master. A page. A first mouth. Vivian had not invented the sentence on the morning she gave it to a frightened boy on the rocks; she had read it from somewhere herself, or written it, and a thing written down was the most dangerous thing in any building, Vivian's own law. The catechism existed in a hand before it existed in a voice. Find that, and you found the room. And there was a second thought riding behind the first, colder, the one she had been keeping at the edge of her vision the way you keep a thing you are not ready to look at directly: if Mara and the boy on the rocks had read the same sentence off the same wall, then they were not two cases. They were two of how many. The water closed and the lantern stayed lit was not a wound any one person carried; it was a thing you were issued, like a uniform, like a name, and there were others walking the wet streets of Hartwell Bay right now reciting it under their breath without knowing it had been put there, finished and set, going on with their ordinary lives. Find the others, Vivian had said into the dark of the machine. Most of them don't even know they graduated. Elena had heard it as a warning. She understood now it had also been a count.

And as the first bar climbed back onto the screen, as the car came back into the world and the world came back to the watcher, her phone lit on the seat — not the clinic exchange, not the pier number, not the recycled Bremerton line — a number she knew, a number in her own contacts, warm and ordinary, and the name above it was Reyes, calling at 8:14 in the morning the way a friend calls, and underneath it, arriving a half-second later so that the two sat together on the bright glass, a text from the pier's third number, three words: she said it twice.
