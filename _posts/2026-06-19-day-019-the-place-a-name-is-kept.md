Noah had not said the name. He had leaned close on the receiving dock, close enough that she felt the cold come off his two sweaters, and he had told her only where it was filed, and the where had stopped her breath before she ever heard the what. So she had driven away from the dock the way she had driven away from the relit lantern five nights ago — with the thing she wanted left lit behind her, refusing to walk toward a door someone else had opened. She had slept four hours of the kind that is not sleep. Now it was Saturday, and the where had a street address, and the street address had brought her here, to a low gray building behind the courthouse that she had spent her whole career mistaking for furniture.

The county Hall of Records on Delgado kept its dead paper in a basement that smelled of foxed cardboard and the particular cold of a room no sun had ever entered. Elena had pulled the Sophie Lund box from the Annex two days ago, two blocks north, and the two buildings were cousins in their grayness, their fluorescent honesty, their indifference. But Noah's where had not been the Annex and had not been the clinic. The single real name on Vivian's key strip — the only true name on a list that otherwise held nothing but letters and numbers and a woman's private symbols — had been filed, Noah said, under a heading he had typed three times because she made him retype everything until it set: PROBATE — CONSERVATORSHIP — SEALED. And then a case number, which Noah had remembered the way he remembered all of it, the way a frightened careful man remembers, because his fear had given him nothing to do but memorize.

A conservatorship. Vivian Mercer had filed, at some point in the buried past, to take legal control of a person — their money, their medical decisions, their consent — and the petition had been sealed, and the strip's one real name was the name of the person she had taken.

Elena stood at the request counter and did not put in the slip.

She had learned the price of her own signature. Two days ago she had signed for the Lund box and the ink was barely dry before Sandoval had the request in her hand and Adeyemi was texting her to come in before she came up. Her name was a flare now; anything Mercer-tagged she touched lit a line straight to Internal Affairs, and a sealed probate file was not Mercer-tagged but it was a thread she could not pull without holding the spool where the watcher could see. She thought about the third number's text from the pier. Don't play it where they can hear. A records request was a kind of playing it where they could hear. The county system logged every pull under the badge that pulled it, and the badge was hers, and the badge was watched.

So she did not put in the slip. She did something she had not done in a long time, which was to ask for help and mean it.

The clerk at the counter was a young man with a lanyard and the patient blankness of a person who had explained the same three rules nine hundred times. Sealed records, he told her, required a court order or a party of standing; she could file a motion; it would take weeks; a judge would decide. He was not unkind. He was a wall, and walls were honest, and Elena had spent her week learning to prefer the honesty of walls to the generosity of certain doors. She thanked him. She did not show the badge. She walked back up into the gray Saturday and stood on the courthouse steps and watched her own breath leave her and thought about who, in this city, had standing to open a thing Vivian had sealed.

The answer was Gerald.

She had spent two movements building Gerald into a motive and then carefully taking him back apart — the broken window, the granite frog, the southern debt, the cash he had watched leave the joint accounts and never asked about. A husband apart a year, guilty of cowardice and unentitled grief. But a husband, still, in the eyes of a court that did not care about the heart's geography. If the conservatorship touched the estate, Gerald had standing. And Gerald, she suspected, would do almost anything now to be the man who had done his job for the woman he broke a window for.

She drove to the waterfront. Gerald's rental was a converted second floor over a chandlery that had stopped selling chandlery, the building leaning companionably toward the harbor, the morning's frost still holding in the north-facing seams of its clapboard. The foghorn went off past the breakwater while she climbed the outside stair, that long flat note that had become the city's pulse for her, the sound the breather had let her hear over the open line on the second night so she would know he was down by the water and not at the clinic. She knocked. Gerald opened the door in a cardigan and the particular dishevelment of a man with too much time and nothing to do in it, and his face, when he saw her, did the thing she had not expected: it hoped.

"You found something," he said.

"I need your help," she said, which was almost the same sentence and not the same sentence at all.

He made coffee in a kitchen the size of a closet and listened while she told him as little as she could afford. There was a sealed filing, she said. A conservatorship Vivian had taken out, years ago, over a person Elena had reason to believe mattered to the case. The file was sealed; Elena could not open it without the act of opening it becoming evidence to the wrong people. But Gerald, as the surviving spouse and an interested party to the estate, could petition to view it, and his request would not light the same fuse hers would, and — she said this part carefully, watching his hands — she needed him to ask without knowing why, and to bring her whatever name was inside.

Gerald set down his cup. "You want me to pull a string and not ask what's tied to it."

"Yes."

"That's what I did for twenty years," he said. "That's how I lost her. I watched the money walk out the door twice a month and I told myself a man who asks is a man who doesn't trust, and I never asked, and she paid two hundred thousand dollars to somebody in the worst end of this waterfront and looked, the teller says, at peace, and I let her, because asking was uncomfortable and not-asking was love." He turned his coffee a quarter turn on the table, the way she had seen Vivian's daughter turn glass in the gallery photographs, the way careful damaged people turned small objects to keep their hands from doing something larger. "And now you want me to not ask again."

"I'm asking you to not ask the wrong person," Elena said. "Ask the court. Bring me the name. That's the whole job."

He was quiet a while. The foghorn went again. "She used to say the most dangerous thing in any building was a record," he said. "I never knew what she meant. I thought it was about lawsuits." He looked up. "It wasn't about lawsuits, was it."

"No," Elena said. "It wasn't."

He agreed. Of course he agreed; she had built the request to be agreeable, and she hated, a little, how good she had gotten at building requests, because it was the program's own verb and she kept catching herself using it. She gave him the case number Noah had carried in his head and watched him write it down in a hand that was ordinary and legible and grieving, a hand that had nothing to hide and so hid nothing, and she thought how rare that had become in this case — a person whose writing simply said what it was.

She was halfway down the outside stair when her phone buzzed.

Not the clinic exchange. Not Mara's recycled number, which had gone silent since it isn't paid, the silence of it sitting in her chest like a held breath she could not let out. A third number — the pier number, the one that had told her don't play it where they can hear. Three words again, the watcher's preferred liturgy, arriving the instant she had set the only safe version of her plan in motion, as if the harbor itself had reported her.

He'll ask the wrong person.

She stopped on the stair. Below her the harbor lay flat and the color of a knife under the white sky, and somewhere across the water the east wing kept its gray daytime face, the window that would go gold at dusk for a guest it was expecting. She read the three words twice and made herself not run back up the stair, because running back up the stair was the thing they wanted, the proof that they could move her with a sentence. He'll ask the wrong person. It could mean Gerald would, in his eagerness, take the case number to someone he trusted at the clinic — someone calm and graying and generous who held doors. It could mean the watcher already knew Gerald better than Elena did. It could mean nothing, a guess dressed as prophecy, the cold-reading of a thing that had learned her shape. She did not know. She had stopped pretending the not-knowing would resolve. The watcher dealt in exactly enough certainty to keep her moving and exactly enough fog to keep her from naming the hand, and she recognized the technique now because she had heard it on a tape above the strait: You didn't put Sophie in the water. No. Say the rest. Guidance disguised as questions. A self rebuilt one sentence at a time.

She texted Gerald from the bottom of the stair, before she let herself think better of it. Bring it to no one. Bring it to me. Then she added, because she could not help it, because the boy on the tape had had no one to tell him: Don't ask the clinic. Don't ask anyone who's been kind to you about it. Especially not the kind ones.

The reply took a minute. Then: You sound like her.

She stood in the cold and did not answer, because he was right, and because the rightness of it was the thing she was most afraid of in the whole case — not the killer, not the watcher, not even the L and the number she would not write down, but the slow discovery that to fight a thing built out of careful guidance she was learning to guide carefully, that the wall she preferred to the door was a wall she was becoming. She put the phone in her coat. The foghorn went a last time and she walked to the car.

That night Gerald called. Not texted — called, his voice scraped thin with the particular triumph of a coward who has, once, done the brave small thing. He had gone to the clerk of the probate court, alone, told no one, asked as the surviving spouse to view the sealed conservatorship under the number she gave him. The clerk had pulled it on the screen. It existed. It was real. Vivian Mercer had petitioned, eleven years ago, for conservatorship over a single named person — the financial decisions, the medical consent, the whole architecture of another human's will — and the petition had been granted and then sealed at Vivian's own later request.

"There's a name," Gerald said. "Elena. There's a name on it. Do you want—"

"Not on the phone," she said, too fast. "Not on the phone, Gerald."

A silence. Then, smaller: "It's a name I know."

She gripped the wheel in the dark of the precinct lot. "Don't say it. Write it down. Bring it to me in the morning, on paper, and don't say it to anyone, and don't say it out loud in any room with a window." She heard herself and did not stop herself. "Where are you right now?"

"My kitchen," he said. "Why?"

Across the black water, through the windshield, the east-wing window came on. Gold. The way you light a lamp for someone you are expecting. And Gerald, in his kitchen on the waterfront, said into the silence she had left him, in the voice of a man who did not yet understand that he had just become the person standing too close, "There's a window across the harbor that just lit up. Is that the clinic? It's the strangest thing — it's like somebody knew I was looking."
