She did not sleep, and she did not trace the text, and she lay awake until first light deciding which of those two facts she could live with.

The not-sleeping was nothing. Elena had not slept properly in years; she had long ago made her peace with the ceiling, with the slow gray migration of the harbor light across it, with the way the foghorn out past the breakwater kept time for a city that pretended not to hear it. The not-tracing was the thing that cost her. Twice in the night she had sat up with the phone warm in her hand and the grainy photograph of her own window glowing back at her, and twice she had opened the dialog to forward it to the cyber unit downtown, to put a request in writing, to make it a case and not a private wound — and twice she had stopped, because forwarding it meant explaining how a number from the Mercer Clinic exchange had come to be texting a homicide detective at midnight, and explaining that meant explaining why she had been awake with her sister's photocopied file spread on the floor, and that road led, by the shortest line she could draw, straight into Lieutenant Sandoval's office and out the other side without a badge.

*Don't trace this.*

The instruction galled her precisely because it worked. Whoever had sent it understood her better than she liked — understood that she was a woman with something to hide and that the something would keep her quiet more reliably than any threat. That was not the calculation of a panicking patient or a jealous husband. It was the calculation of someone who had read her file, or read her, and the difference between those two had stopped mattering somewhere around four in the morning.

So she did the only thing that let her feel like the hand and not the glove. She got up while it was still dark, and she made coffee she did not drink, and she went to the clinic — not because the text had told her to stay away, exactly, but because she had decided, somewhere in the long gray hours, that the surest way to lose was to let an unseen hand decide where she would and would not stand. If the lighthouse wanted to watch her, she would walk in the front door and let it. She would just make sure she was looking back.

The rain had thinned to a fine suspended mist by the time she crossed the bay, the kind that did not fall so much as hang, beading on the windshield faster than the wipers cared to clear it. Saturday. The Mercer Institute for Applied Psychology kept reduced hours on weekends — she had checked — and the lot was nearly empty, and the bronze plaque by the door wore its little motto in the wet gray light. *We are not what was done to us.* She read it again, going in, and found that it sat differently on her now than it had two days ago, when it had seemed only the kind of brave nonsense clinics liked to carve over their doors. Now it sounded less like a promise and more like an argument someone very much needed to win.

Marguerite was at the front desk.

Elena had met her on Thursday in passing — the receptionist who managed the key cabinet, a woman of perhaps sixty with reading glasses on a beaded chain and a cardigan the color of oatmeal and the unhurried competence of someone who had outlasted three directors and would outlast a fourth. She had Vivian's logbook habit in her bones; she was the one who kept the paper ledger on its chain, who refused, as Vivian had refused, to let the keys go digital. She looked up when Elena came in, and something crossed her face that was not surprise and not quite welcome.

"Detective." She set down her pen. "You've come for the cabinet."

"I've come to talk to the person who keeps it."

That landed somewhere. Marguerite folded her hands on the blotter. "Dr. Reyes said you might. He said to give you anything you asked for." A small precise pause. "He says that about everyone, lately. It's a generous instinct." Another pause, and then, quieter, as though she were surprising herself: "It's a great deal of generosity for a man who just lost the woman he built his life beside."

Elena let that sit. She had learned that the most useful thing a detective could do in a room was, often, nothing — leave a silence open and watch who could not bear it. Marguerite bore it for a moment, then reached under the desk and brought up a ring of her own keys, and stood.

"You'll want to see the page again," she said. "Not the photograph. The page."

The records office was as Elena remembered it: windowless, close, the steel cabinet against the far wall with its hasp and its paper ledger hanging from a chain by a pen chewed soft at the end. Marguerite opened the cabinet, and the small interior light came on, and there were the hooks in their rows, each with its strip of tape and its cramped clerk's label, and there — third from the left in the bottom row — the one that still read *V. MERCER — HOME OFC.* in white tape, and still hung empty.

"Tell me how this works," Elena said. "Not the rules. What actually happens."

Marguerite considered the question with the seriousness of someone who had never been asked it. "A key leaves this cabinet one of two ways," she said. "I take it down for someone and they sign the line and I watch them do it, or — for the senior people, the directors, the doctors with their own keys to this room — they come in after hours and take it themselves and they're trusted to sign the line on their honor." She touched the chained pen, not quite fondly. "Dr. Mercer wanted it on paper for exactly that reason. She used to say a computer remembers everything and tells you nothing. A book on a chain, she said, makes a person stop and put their own hand to it. She thought the stopping mattered more than the record."

"And on October the twenty-ninth?"

"I wasn't here." Marguerite said it flatly, a fact she had clearly turned over many times. "It was after hours. The page says so — the time column. Quarter past nine in the evening. I was home." She found the line with one finger and held it there, and Elena bent to look, and there it was again, the thing that had snagged on her Thursday and split her open Friday night: the cramped, pressed, overbuilt signature in the borrower's column, a capital at the front and a long descending tail at the end and between them a knot of letters that had tried to be a name and a refusal in the same stroke. Elena did not look at it long. She had learned, last night, on her own floor, that this particular signature could open a trapdoor under her, and she could not afford to fall through one here.

"Who has after-hours access to this room?" she asked instead.

"The doctors. Dr. Reyes. Dr. Mercer, of course." Marguerite's finger came off the page. "And one cleaner with a master, but he's been with us eleven years and he was at his daughter's that night, I checked, I —" She stopped herself. "I checked because I couldn't help it. Because a key left this cabinet under my keeping and went into a house where a woman died, and I have signed every other line in this book for nineteen years and I cannot read that one. It is the only line in the book I cannot read." Her voice had gone tight. "I have looked at it until my eyes water. It is someone's hand. People do not write so that no one can read them by accident. You write that way when you have written your name ten thousand times and learned to make it disappear."

Elena looked at the woman and revised her, the way she was always revising people — not the placid keeper of a cabinet but someone who had spent nineteen years believing the paper on the chain made the building honest, and who now stood at the open steel door of that belief and felt the cold come out of it. "You think someone with their own access took it," Elena said. "Someone who didn't need to be let in."

"I think," Marguerite said carefully, "that whoever signed that line could have signed any name they liked. A made-up name. A dead patient's name. There are names in this book of people who passed through here years ago; I'd not have blinked. But they didn't write a name at all. They wrote — that." She gestured at the page as though it had offended her personally. "They wrote a true signature they made certain no one could read. And I have thought about why a person would do that, Detective, and the only answer I can find is that they did not want to lie and could not afford the truth. And I do not know which of those frightens me more."

It was, Elena thought, the cleanest reading of the page anyone had offered, including herself, and it had come from a sixty-year-old receptionist with glasses on a chain who had simply refused to look away from the one thing she could not read. She felt a flush of something that was almost shame. She had spent the night convinced she alone saw the trapdoor; here was a woman who had been standing on it for a week.

"Has anyone else asked you about this page?" Elena said. "Besides Dr. Reyes."

"Dr. Reyes didn't ask," Marguerite said. "He told me. The morning after she died, before you came — he came in early, earlier than I've ever seen him, and he stood right where you're standing and he looked at the empty hook and he said, *We'll need to be very careful with the detectives about that key.* " She frowned, hearing it again. "I thought he meant careful as in honest. To make sure nothing was lost or muddled. He's a careful man. That's all I thought."

"And now?"

Marguerite did not answer that. She closed the cabinet instead, and the little light went out, and she turned the hasp, and in the windowless quiet Elena heard the small definite sound of a thing being shut. "I've kept this book nineteen years," the woman said, to the steel, not to Elena. "I'd like to know who put a line in it I can't read. I find I would like that very much."

Elena left her there and went back out through the lobby past the bronze motto, and the mist had not lifted, and the clinic's lone gold window — the lantern, somewhere above and behind her on the east wing — would be invisible from down here in daylight, just one more dark pane in a wet gray building. She stood on the step a moment and made herself hold the two new things side by side without leaping, the way she'd taught herself.

One: whoever took the key had access of their own and signed anyway — not to deceive the book, but to mark it, a true name made unreadable on purpose. *They did not want to lie and could not afford the truth.*

Two: Adam Reyes, before any detective had arrived, before the body was cold, had stood at that empty hook and said *we'll need to be very careful with the detectives about that key.* He had not asked who took it. He had begun, that same morning, managing how it would be discussed.

She thought of his grief that had been exactly the right size. She thought of every record pre-pulled and waiting, every door held open, every suspect helpfully named. *A generous instinct,* Marguerite had said. *A great deal of generosity.*

Her phone buzzed against her hip and she nearly came off the step, and she hated that it could still do that to her. She drew it out. Not the clinic exchange this time. Adeyemi.

*Osei finished the post. You need to see it before it's filed. And Vega —* a second bubble, arriving a beat later, the way her partner texted when he was choosing his words — *Sandoval's been asking how you caught this one. Asking me. Come in the back.*

The mist hung in the empty lot, and the bronze plaque said *We are not what was done to us*, and Elena stood very still on the clinic step and understood that two doors had opened at once — the autopsy that might tell her how Vivian Mercer really died, and the internal lieutenant who had begun, somewhere in a downtown office, pulling the one thread that could end Elena before she ever reached the answer. And she could only walk through one of them first.
