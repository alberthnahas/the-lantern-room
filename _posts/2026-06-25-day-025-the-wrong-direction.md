She did not answer the fifth number. She stood in the surf of the dead dryers until the message stopped being a shock and became a fact, which took longer than she liked, and then she walked out into the hard dry cold of Verbena Street and drove home the long way, the way she had told Noah to go, because she had begun to do the things she told other people to do, as if her own advice were a hand on her own back, steering.

It came back wrong direction. She had turned the four words over so many times on the drive that they had worn smooth. They were not a threat. That was the thing that kept her awake past three in the apartment with the lamp turned to the wall the way it had been in the photo someone had taken of it. A threat would have named her, or named Noah, or named the man neither of them said anymore. This was something else. This was a line of dictation overheard — one watcher reporting to another, or one watcher reporting to itself, the way a person mutters the score. The question Reyes was walking from station to station — make a copy — had gone out into the circuit, and at one of the stations it had not behaved. It had come back wrong direction. Which meant that her own move, the warning she had given Noah in a place she had chosen precisely because it kept no record of anyone, had been a move on a board she had not known she was sitting at. Her care had been read. Her protectiveness was a data point. The thing she had done to keep Noah out of the well had told the man at the rim exactly how deep she was willing to lower a bucket, and for whom.

By morning the frost in the window seams had set harder, the way it set now and did not melt, and the sky over the strait had gone the white-hard blankness she had learned to read as the day deciding nothing. Wednesday. She made coffee she did not drink and laid Mara's file out on the kitchen table beside Sophie Lund's, the two thin folders side by side, because the slow vector was the only one she could afford and the slow vector ran through the dead.

She had Sophie's file now only in the shape of what Sandoval had let her glimpse and what Reyes had handed her without seeming to — the credenza photograph, the name on the steering referral, the sun-faded girl Vivian had kept in view to keep herself honest. Mara's file she had carried for six years in the un-oiled drawer. She had read it ten thousand times and never once read it correctly, because she had read it the way the note in the dead woman's chair had told her to: There is no one to blame but the woman in this chair. She had obeyed a suicide note for six years. She made herself look at that without flinching, the way Osei had made himself look at the wrong stillness in a body sat upright after death, because flinching was a kind of obedience too.

What she was hunting in the two files was not a culprit. It was a grammar. The bible of her own method, the thing she trusted over feeling, was that a script leaves the same fingerprints on every page it touches, and if Sophie and Mara had been written by the same hand fifteen years apart, the proof would not be in what the files said. It would be in how they said it — in the over-built strokes of an obscured referral, in a phrase that appeared where a phrase like that had no business being, in the small repeated economies of a person who had written something so many times it had stopped being a sentence and become a reflex. Before it was a voice it was a page. She had said that to herself in a parked car. Now she went looking for the page in the only two copies of it she could reach.

She found it in the margins, where she had never thought to look, because for six years she had stared at the box that held a name and never at the white space around it. On Mara's intake sheet, in the lower corner of the clinician's notes, in the cramped obscured hand she had traced stroke by stroke until she could follow it without being able to read it, someone had written a short notation and underlined it once: she is ready to be useful. Not she is ready for discharge. Not she shows improvement. Useful. The word the program used. The word Reyes had let slip across a diner table when he meant graduate. And on Sophie Lund's referral, in Sandoval's photograph, in the same over-building hand fifteen years older — younger, she corrected herself, fifteen years younger; the hand had aged forward, not back — a notation she had read as boilerplate and now could not unsee: the lantern is ready to be lit.

The lantern is ready to be lit. The phrase Vivian had pressed hard enough into a notebook to emboss three pages. The phrase the dead woman had texted her estranged husband at midnight. The phrase that had been, all this time, not a code Elena needed to break but a stage in a process she needed to recognize — a thing said about a person when the person was finished. Sophie, fifteen years ago, ready to be lit. Mara, six years ago, ready to be useful. The same hand. The same grammar. A script, not a symptom, exactly as the catechism on the off-grid tape had been a script — except that the catechism had been Mara reciting, the subject's voice, and this was the other voice, the writer's, the first mouth, caught in the act of finishing someone in the margin of a form.

She sat with that until the coffee went cold the way coffee did around her now, an instrument she could not warm. The horror was not that the hand had written it. The horror was the direction of it. She had spent six years believing she was reading backward from a death to its cause, a detective's natural motion, effect to origin. But the margins ran the other way. The hand had written she is ready to be useful before Mara died, the way a stage direction precedes the scene. The file was not a record of what had happened to her sister. It was, in part, an instruction for it. And Elena had carried the instruction in a drawer by her bed for six years and pressed it like a bruise to be sure the grief was hers.

The phone, face-down on the table, lit and buzzed once against the wood.

She turned it over the way the annotation had taught her to turn things over. Not the fifth number. Not Mara's recycled line. The clinic exchange — the first one, the oldest one, the one that had sent her own lit window back to her with don't trace this, silent now for many days. Two words, and after them, for the first time from that number, her name.

how is sophie, elena

No question mark. The watcher did not ask. The watcher told her where she was standing. She had taken two dead folders out of two drawers in a kitchen with the blinds down and within the hour the building across the water knew which dead girl she had her hand on. She thought of how Osei's secret finding had come back to her as a photograph before the ink was dry; how the man's question to Noah had reached the fifth channel before Noah reached the door; how nothing she did in a chosen, recordless room stayed in the room. The reach was not surveillance. Surveillance watched. This anticipated. This knew which file she would open next because it had built the shelf the files sat on and labeled the drawers in her own life. how is sophie. As if Sophie were a patient of Elena's now. As if Elena had been handed a case the way you hand a subject a script — gently, and with a name already typed at the bottom.

She did not trace it. She had learned that much. She set the phone down and made herself finish the motion she had started, because the man across the water wanted her to feel watched and the only counter she had was to keep working as if she were alone, even knowing she was not — the way you keep walking steadily across a room you know is wired, because the wire wants your stumble. She photographed both notations side by side, the useful and the lantern, in the same frame, the way Osei had photographed lividity, the way she had photographed the empty hook. Two phrases, fifteen years and six years dead, in one hand. Enough to follow. Not enough to file. Not yet.

In the afternoon Reyes called.

She watched the name sit on the lit screen and breathe. ADAM REYES. He called her the way a friend calls, at the hour a friend would, with the timing that was always, she had decided, exactly the right size, like his grief. She let it ring three times so that answering would not look like waiting, and then she answered, because the slow vector ran through him too — because he was the first mouth or he was a man who could reach the first mouth, and either one ended him, and either one was the case, and she could not afford to stop letting him talk.

"You sound like you haven't slept," he said. The same words Noah had used in the launderette. She wondered, briefly and coldly, whether he had taught the whole city to open with concern.

"I never sleep," she said. "You know that. You've offered me three different remedies."

"And you've refused all three." A warmth in it. The warmth was real; that was the trap; the trap had been built without a third door. "I won't offer a fourth. I called because I've been thinking about your sister."

The kitchen went very still around her, the dryers' surf a memory, the cold lying along the floor like something poured there and left to set. He had never, in twenty-four days, said your sister. She had disclosed Mara only to Sandoval, in a fourth-floor room with a window that faced a wall. The department did not have it on paper. And here was the man, gently, at the right hour, in the right size, putting his hand on the exact place she had told one person and no file.

"I didn't know you knew about my sister," she said, and kept her voice the cold clerk's, level because she made it level.

"I didn't, the way you mean." A pause that was not hesitation; a pause placed. "Vivian kept everyone, Elena. You've seen the credenza. She kept Sophie there to stay honest, and she kept others I never let myself look at, and after she died I have been looking, because someone has to, and I found a name I recognized only because you carry it like a stone. I'm not going to pretend that's a comfort. I'm telling you because you'd rather know that I know than discover it later and wonder what else I've been holding."

It was, she thought, the most generous thing anyone had said to her in six years, and it was a hand laid flat against the exact pane of her grief to feel whether the glass was warm. She could not spend it. She could not spend any of it. She thought of the margin in Mara's file, she is ready to be useful, and she understood that the man on the line was, in this very moment, in his perfect kindness, reading her — was lighting her lantern to see if it was ready — and that she could not tell him she knew, because telling him would expose the file and the tracing and the off-grid tape and hand the brittle whole of it to Sandoval, who would kindly pull her off and hand them the next person.

"What's the name," she said. The slow vector. Make him spend, not her.

A breath on the line. Behind it, faint, under everything, a sound she knew the way she knew her own pulse — a single long note, past a breakwater, answering nothing. He was not in his office over the water. He was somewhere lower, somewhere by the strait, somewhere the foghorn reached the same way it had reached down the open line on the second day, when the breather had not been at the clinic at all but down by the water, listening back.

"I'd rather not say it into a phone," Reyes said gently. "Come to the clinic. The lantern's lit. I'll put the kettle on."
