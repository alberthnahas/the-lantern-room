Hartwell Lock & Safe sat at the dead end of Cannery Road where the pavement gave out and became the kind of broken asphalt that remembered being a working waterfront, and Elena parked the car so that the windshield faced the strait and watched, for a moment, the rain come in sideways off the water before she went in. It was Tuesday. The cold that had arrived over the weekend had settled now into a fixed thing, no longer a snap but a season, and the puddles in the lot had skinned over at their edges with the first thin ice of the year. She had not slept. She had stopped pretending to herself that this was unusual.

The shop smelled of cut brass and machine oil, a smell so specific it was almost a taste, and a bell on a curl of spring announced her without enthusiasm. Behind the counter a man in his sixties looked up from a key blank he was deburring with a file, and Elena saw him do the arithmetic that everyone in this part of town did when a stranger in a wool coat walked in alone on a weekday: cop or trouble or both.

"Help you," he said. Not a question. The whole town spoke in statements lately, she thought, or she had simply started hearing it.

"Carl Briggs gave me your name," Elena said, which was half true; Briggs the lock tech had given her the shop's name three days ago, not the man's, and the man's eyes warmed a degree at the borrowed familiarity anyway. "Detective Vega. Hartwell Bay PD. You did the lockwork on the Mercer Institute. The whole building, a couple years back."

"Did the building. Did the founder's house too, the office in it." He set the file down. His name, according to the embroidery over his pocket, was Whit. "That the dead woman."

"That's the dead woman."

Whit wiped his hands on a rag that had stopped being any particular color around the time the cannery closed. "Figured somebody'd come. Thought it'd be the insurance, not the police." He did not ask whether it was a suicide. People who worked with their hands, Elena had learned, rarely asked the question the newspaper had already answered; they asked the one underneath it. "What do you want to know about the locks."

"I want to know how many keys exist for the home office, and where they went."

He went still in the particular way of a man being careful, and Elena recognized the stillness because she had been watching people perform versions of it all week — Reyes's perfect grief, Sandoval's interviewer's hush, Marguerite's nineteen years of refusal to look away. But Whit's was a different animal. It was not the stillness of a man with something to hide. It was the stillness of a man who had just realized that something he'd done in the ordinary course of business might turn out to matter, and was afraid of being on the wrong side of it without ever having chosen a side at all.

"You'll want the work order," he said, and went into the back.

She waited. On the pegboard behind the counter hung a hundred key blanks in their nickel ranks, and below them a cork square pinned thick with curling business cards and a child's drawing gone yellow and a Polaroid of a black dog. A space heater ticked. Out the window the foghorn started up, three counties of gray water away, that low note that had ridden the open phone line back to her on the second night and had been living somewhere behind her sternum ever since. She did not turn toward it. She had taught herself, this week, not to turn toward the sounds that wanted her to turn.

Whit came back with a manila folder soft at the corners and laid it open on the counter, turning it so she could read, and she thought, unbidden, of Sandoval turning a folder the same way the day before, of how the worst things in her life were arriving on turned pages. The work order was two years old. A lock change, the home office of the Mercer residence, single interior door, keyed cylinder plus thumb-turn deadbolt. Reason for service, in a clerk's hand: prior key compromised — client request. Authorized by V. Mercer. Keys cut: she ran her finger down to the line.

Three.

"Three keys," she said.

"Standard's two for a job like that. One for the door, one to live in a drawer." Whit tapped the line with a blunt nail. "She asked for three. I remember because I asked her did she want them all on the same bitting or did she want one of them blind-coded so it couldn't be copied at a hardware store, and she looked at me like I'd said something interesting, which people don't, about keys." He almost smiled and then didn't. "She said make all three the same. She said the danger was never the key. The danger was the record of who had it."

Elena's pulse did something small and exact. The most dangerous thing in any building is a record. Reyes had quoted it to her at the clinic as Vivian's saying, three days ago, in his second-floor office with the photograph of the drowned girl on the credenza, and here it was again coming out of a locksmith's mouth two years older than the murder, and the consistency of it — the way the dead woman's philosophy held its shape across rooms that had never met — made Vivian feel suddenly less like a victim Elena was avenging and more like a mind she was walking deeper into, room by room, each one built by the same hand.

"Three identical keys," she said. "Where did they go?"

"Where she took them. I cut 'em, I handed 'em over, that's where my knowing stops." Whit shrugged, but his eyes stayed on her, wanting, she understood, to be told that this was fine, that three keys was a number a man could hand a paying client and walk away from with a clean conscience. She did not give him that. Not because she was cruel but because she did not yet know if it was true.

"Did anyone ever come back," Elena said, "to copy one?"

And there it was — the thing the stillness had been guarding. Whit looked at the work order and not at her. "Last month," he said. "Maybe five weeks back. Fella came in with a key on a plain ring, wanted a duplicate. Nice enough. Educated. Knew to ask whether it'd take a copy or whether it was blind-coded, which most folks don't, which is the only reason I clocked him at all." He drew a breath. "It was blind-coded. One of mine. Mercer bitting. I told him I couldn't run it off the machine, he'd need the code and the authorization, and he said of course, he'd been foolish, and he thanked me and he left and that was that. Didn't push. Polite about it."

"Five weeks ago." Late September. A month before the Oct 29 sign-out at the clinic cabinet. Six weeks before the death. The dates were assembling themselves in her chest in an order she did not like, a staircase descending toward a door. "Can you describe him."

"Coat like yours. Better, maybe." A dry flick of the eyes. "Forties. The kind of handsome that's mostly being calm. Gray coming in at the sides, on purpose-looking. Hands like he'd never used 'em for anything but talking." Whit paused, and then said the thing that turned the floor of the shop to water under her. "Had a way of making you want to help him. I stood here genuinely sorry I couldn't run his key. Took me till he was gone to notice he never told me his name, and I never thought to ask, and asking's the whole front half of my job."

Elena kept her face the way Osei kept his over a fact that wouldn't hold weight yet. Inside, the room of standing people from Sandoval's office got to its feet a second time. A man, calm, well-coated, graying on purpose, who left a stranger sorry to have failed him and unable afterward to say why or who. She had met that man. She had been doing it for three days, registering it as cooperation, calling it unusually precise. She made herself not say the name even inside her own skull, because she had a discipline now about names she could not yet read, and the discipline was the only railing left on the staircase.

"If I brought you a sample of handwriting," she said carefully, "could you tell me whether it matched a signature on your work orders? Any of your own paperwork from the Mercer job?"

Whit's brow folded. "I'm a locksmith, not a — what do you call it."

"You're a man who watches hands for a living. I've met two of you this week and you both saw more than the experts." She thought of Marguerite, nineteen years at the cabinet, reading an unreadable line and knowing it for a true signature made deliberately illegible. "I'm not asking you to be an expert. I'm asking what you saw."

He considered her. The space heater ticked. Then he reached under the counter and brought up a second sheet stapled to the first, the delivery receipt, the one a client signed on pickup, and he turned it toward her, and Elena looked down at the line where Vivian Mercer had signed for three identical keys two years ago, that bold legible doctor's hand she had seen on the death-assessment in the cold drawer, the hand that had ruled her sister deliberate and self-directed — and below it, in the receiving-clerk's box, a second signature, smaller, cramped, the pressure building over itself, a name begun and then taught to disappear.

She had seen that hand three times now. On Mara's intake sheet under referred by. On the clinic logbook under the Oct 29 sign-out. On a case-intake referral, Sandoval had said, half-printed and half-run-together, stamped beneath with the clean typed name of a girl fifteen years drowned. And now here, two years ago, on the day three keys to a dead woman's office walked out of this shop — somebody else had signed for them. Not Vivian. Somebody who had been allowed to receive the keys to her most private room, and who had written their name the way you write a name you intend never to be read.

"That one," Whit said, watching her face go where it was going. "I remember her not signing for all three herself. She brought somebody. Doctor type. Didn't say a word the whole time. She did the talking; he did the carrying." He frowned at the old paper. "Funny. Never thought about it till just now. She walked in with the keys still to be cut, and she walked out, and it was him put 'em in his pocket."

Elena straightened slowly. Outside the ice was filming wider across the puddles and the foghorn called once more across the gray, and she did not turn toward it, and for the first time all week she understood that the second key she had been hunting — the wiped thumb-turn, the missing cylinder key, the bolt thrown from a room no living hand should have been able to bolt — had never been missing at all. It had been cut, in triplicate, two years before the murder, by Vivian's own order, and carried out of this shop in the pocket of a man whose name nobody could read because he had spent his whole life learning to make it vanish — and who, six weeks ago, had walked back in to make one more.

"Mr. Whit," she said, and was surprised her voice held. "I'm going to need that receipt. The original. And I'm going to need you not to tell anyone I was here." She thought of How's Osei, no question mark, glowing on her phone in the morgue lot. "For your sake more than mine."

He looked at her, and at the receipt, and at the door behind her as though it might already be opening, and the last of the wanting-to-be-told-it-was-fine went out of his eyes and left something older and more frightened in its place.

"There were three," he said quietly. "I only ever heard about the one that went missing. Where's the third, Detective?"

Elena had no answer for him. But across the bay, in the failing afternoon, she knew the gold square in the east wing would soon begin to burn, and she thought — for the first time as a certainty rather than a fear — that the person who had the third key had always meant for her to come this far, and no further, and that tomorrow she would learn whose dead name was waiting at the bottom of the stairs.
