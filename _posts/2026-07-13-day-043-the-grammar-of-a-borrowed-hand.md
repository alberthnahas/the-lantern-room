She stood on the granite steps of First Strait Maritime until the cold in her feet stopped being a feeling and became a fact, and then she made herself walk down them, because standing still in front of a bank with a badge in her coat was its own kind of announcement, and she had spent five weeks learning not to make those. The snow that had been near-white-out since dawn had not let up so much as changed its manner, coming down now in a straight, businesslike fall that the wind no longer bothered to argue with. Harbor Street had gone the flat blue-gray of a photograph of itself. A plow crawled past throwing a wake of dirty slush against the parked cars, and somewhere under all of it a hand that was not Vivian Mercer's had stood exactly where Elena was standing, on a morning eight days ago, waiting for a door to open on a body not yet cold.

She sat behind the wheel for a moment without starting the engine, watching the plow's taillights shrink into the white at the end of the block, and made herself say the fact out loud once, quietly, the way she'd learned to test a thing before she carried it anywhere that mattered: someone had stood on these steps before the sun was up, eight days ago, wearing a dead woman's signature the way another person might wear her coat, and no one had thought to ask the granite what it remembered.

She got in the car and, this time, she called Adeyemi.

It cost her something to admit that the calculation had changed since the bridge — since she'd told Reyes no without telling him why — but it had changed cleanly, the way good facts did: she did not need Noah's name to ask for box 214 anymore. She needed Dolores Pham's, and Pham had watched a signature made twenty-five times over two years and could swear, in any courtroom in the state, that the twenty-sixth was wrong. That was a bank employee's sworn observation about a forged instrument used to access a private vault the morning after a homicide victim's death. It did not require an affidavit that mentioned a research assistant who was not supposed to know the box existed. It required a judge who was awake.

"Say that again," Adeyemi said, when she'd said it once already, and she understood he wasn't asking her to repeat it because he'd missed it.

"The teller recognized her. Independently, off a news photo, before I ever walked in. She's willing to swear the last signature in the log — the one made the morning after Vivian died, before the bank opened — doesn't match the twenty-five before it. Rehearsed. Careful the way a hand gets careful copying somebody else's."

"And Noah's not in any of that."

"Noah's not in any of that," she said, and heard, in the silence that followed, Adeyemi doing the same arithmetic she'd already finished — that a case which had spent six weeks accumulating things she couldn't spend had just handed her one she could.

He called her back forty minutes later from a hallway with bad reception, snow audible in the gaps between his words the way it was audible in hers, and told her a Superior Court judge named Emmett Aldana, reachable only because his clerk owed Adeyemi a favor from a wrongful-death case two winters back, had agreed to hear it by phone at eight that night rather than wait for Monday's calendar — homicide, exigent, a financial instrument likely to be sealed by the decedent's estate the moment probate noticed it existed. "He wants it in writing by six," Adeyemi said. "You writing, or am I?"

"Both," Elena said. "But before six I need an hour with Wexler."

Renata Wexler ran the crime lab's questioned-documents bench out of a windowless room two floors below evidence intake, a room that smelled permanently of toner and the particular mineral smell of old paper, and she had the unhurried patience of someone who had spent thirty years being right about things nobody wanted to hear yet. Elena had brought her two things: the photograph of the Oct. 29 key-cabinet sign-out line, the illegible signature under the newly taped HOME OFC. hook, and, beside it, the department's own evidence copy of the four lines appended beneath Daniel Voss's returned, hand-copied testimony — the lines that read like a confession and weren't his.

Wexler laid them under the same lamp and didn't speak for a long time.

"These aren't the same act," she said finally. "I want to be careful, because you're going to want them to be, and wanting is how bad testimony gets made." She tapped the Oct. 29 line first. "This is a person disguising their own hand. You can see it in the over-building — extra strokes laid down specifically to bury the habitual shapes underneath, the way you'd paint over a signature you didn't want read even by yourself in a year. It's suppression. Hard to do consistently, but it's your own hand fighting you, which is a fight you can win with practice." She moved to the second sheet. "This is the opposite problem. Somebody had to reproduce habitual features that were never theirs — Voss's spacing, his particular closed loop on the lowercase d, the way his crossbars ran a little uphill. That's not suppression, that's construction. You're building a stranger's signature out of nothing, and it's a much harder trick to bring off convincingly enough to fool anyone who's seen the real thing recently." She sat back. "Two different disciplines. I wouldn't put either one in front of a jury and call them the same hand."

"But," Elena said, because she'd heard the word sitting unspoken at the end of the sentence.

"But." Wexler took off her glasses, which Elena had learned over three prior cases meant she was about to say something she'd deny having implied if it were ever repeated back to her from a witness stand. "Both of these took years off somebody's life to get this good. Suppression this clean, construction this clean — that's not a hobby. That's someone who thinks about handwriting the way a locksmith thinks about tumblers. Practiced, drilled, probably taught. I've examined maybe four documents in my career that showed this kind of control, and every one of them turned out to belong to someone with formal training — a forensic background, a courtroom exhibit background, or —" she considered it, unwillingly, the way she considered everything unwillingly before she said it out loud — "a clinical one. People who write case notes for a living learn to control their hand in ways the rest of us don't. Whether that's this person, I can't tell you. I can tell you it's the same tier of skill in both documents. And your teller's description of a rehearsed, over-careful copy of an alias signature — if that's real, and I haven't seen it, so I can't certify it — that's a third instance of the same tier, going back at least six years if your Oct. 29 sample is what you say it is."

Elena drove back to the precinct through streets the plows couldn't keep ahead of, turning that phrase over the way she'd turned over every true, unusable thing this case had handed her: the same tier of skill, three times, six years apart, still active. Not an insider with a stolen key. Someone who had made a study — a long, patient, private study — of becoming other people's hands. She thought of Vivian's own line, the one Reyes liked to repeat as though it explained him instead of indicting whoever had actually learned it: we are not what was done to us. She thought, for the first time, that the sentence might describe a method as easily as a mercy. Somewhere in this city was a person who had been taught, so thoroughly and for so long, that a name — a hand, a self — could be set down and picked back up like a coat, that doing it to a bank signature and doing it to a dying man's last page had become the same unremarkable skill. She did not write that down. It was, like the second clause, true regardless of how she felt about it, and utterly useless in a room with a jury in it.

She called Noah from the car, because she had promised herself she would do it before the day ended, and because she did not trust tomorrow to leave her time.

"You're not on anything," she told him. "Not the warrant, not Wexler's file, not even in the version I gave Adeyemi. Pham did this. You didn't."

The line was quiet long enough that she checked the screen to see if the storm had dropped the call. Then: "I want to be on it." His voice had none of the shake she'd expected. "Everyone who's helped you has ended up carrying something alone in the dark — Marguerite, Voss, probably you — and I keep getting to hand you things and walk away clean, and I don't — " He stopped. Started again, quieter. "My mother used to clean offices at night. Two of them, before the clinic hired her steady. She used to say the people who leave the mess are never the ones who find out what it costs to pick it up. I've been the one leaving it clean for four years. I don't want to be that anymore."

"Noah—"

"I'm not asking you to put my name on the warrant. I'm telling you I'm not going to stop just because you got smart about the paperwork." A breath, and under it something that might have been a laugh with all the humor taken out of it. "You keep telling me to go home and be careful, like careful is a place I can still get to. I've been careful for four years. I signed for tapes I didn't ask about. I typed labels for people I never met. Careful is how I got here." A pause, longer this time, the kind she'd learned not to fill. "There's a second concordance page. Not the strip — a page I typed for her twice, months apart, and something about the second copy was wrong, and I never told anyone that because I didn't understand it was wrong until three nights ago. I'll bring it to you. Not tonight — the roads are gone and I'm not driving that ferry road blind — but soon. I wanted you to know it exists before I lost my nerve."

She told him to write down where he'd hidden it, in his own hand, and give the location to no one, not even her, until he could put it directly in her hands — a small, useless piece of tradecraft that was really just her trying to keep one more person off a channel she couldn't see. He agreed, too easily, in the way of someone already deciding not to be careful.

She was still holding the dead phone when Adeyemi's lit up on the seat beside her, face-up, and she watched rather than heard it arrive — a single buzz, a name that wasn't a name, a preview line long enough to read before he snatched it up.

Unknown number. Not her line. His.

Ask the judge if he's warm enough for where he's headed tonight.

Adeyemi read it twice, and then looked at her the way a man looks at a floor he has just understood is not as solid as the one he'd been standing on for six weeks, because for five weeks every message like that had arrived on Elena's phone, addressed to Elena's grief, timed to Elena's movements — and now, forty minutes before a judge she had never met signed an order the watcher should have had no way to know about yet, it had reached out and touched someone else entirely.

It hadn't been following her.

It had been following the case — which meant it had been following the warrant before the warrant existed, following a judge Adeyemi had reached only because a clerk owed him a favor from two winters back, a chain of small private choices no reasonable person could have anticipated an hour in advance. Elena thought of the granite steps, and the practiced signature, and Wexler's four documents in a whole career, and understood, watching Adeyemi's thumb hover uselessly over a reply he would never send, that the thing they were chasing did not need to be in the room to know what the room was about to decide.

It had simply always been early.
